INTRODUCTION 
 
I, the Chairperson, Committee on Public Undertakings (2026-27) having been 
authorized by the Committee to submit the Report on their behalf, present this Thirty-Second 
Report (18th Lok Sabha) on the “Review of Performance of Insurance Sector CPSUs”. 
 
2. The Committee on Public Undertakings (2024-25) had selected the said subject for 
detailed examination and report. The subject was subsequently carried forward by the 
successor Committees in (2025-26) and (2026-27) to complete the unfinished task. 
 
3. The Committee on Public Undertakings (2024-25) was briefed about the subject by 
the representatives of the Department of Financial Services, Ministry of Finance on 18th 
February, 2025. Thereafter, the Committee took evidence of the five of the seven Insurance 
sector CPSUs viz. Agriculture Insurance Company of India Limited, Life Insurance 
Corporation of India, General Insurance Corporation of India, The New India Assurance 
Company Limited and National Insurance Company Limited. The Committee also took the 
evidence of Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India. For United India 
Insurance Company Limited and Oriental Insurance Company Limited, the Committee 
examined the written submissions made by the companies. Thereafter, The Committee on 
Public Undertakings (2025-26) took the evidence of two Private Insurance Companies 
namely Kotak Mahindra Life Insurance Company Ltd. and Tata AIG General Insurance 
Company Ltd. The Committee, then, took evidence of the Department of Financial Services, 
Ministry of Finance for concluding the examination of the subject.  
 
4. The Committee (2026-27) considered and adopted the draft Report at their sitting held 
on 03 August, 2026. 
 
5. The Committee wish to express thanks to the representatives of Department of 
Financial Services, Ministry of Finance and the afore-mentioned five CPSUs and two private 
insurance companies and Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India for 
tendering evidence before the Committee and furnishing the requisite information to them in 
connection with examination of the subject. 
 
6. The Committee wish to express their sincere thanks to the predecessor Committee for 
their valuable contribution in examination of the subject. 
 
7.   For facility of reference and convenience, the Observations and Recommendations of 
the Committee have been printed in bold letters in Part-II of the Report.

CHAPTER-I 
OVERVIEW OF THE INSURANCE SECTOR AND CPSUs 
 
A. Evolution and Structure of the Insurance Sector in India 
The insurance sector plays a critical role in modern economies by providing 
financial protection against risks and uncertainties while mobilising long-term financial 
resources for investment and economic development. Insurance enables individuals, 
businesses and institutions to mitigate financial losses arising from unforeseen events 
such as accidents, natural disasters, health emergencies and other contingencies. In 
addition to providing risk protection, the insurance sector also contributes significantly to 
financial intermediation by mobilising savings and investing them in infrastructure and 
other productive sectors of the economy.  
 
1.2 Brief History of the Modern Insurance Sector (Global): Modern insurance 
began in the 17th–18th centuries, built on the foundation of actuarial science and 
probability theory. The Great Fire of London (1666) spurred the creation of organised fire 
insurance. Later, in 1766, the Equitable Life Assurance Society revolutionised life 
insurance by basing premiums on life expectancy, making it a sound financial product. 
The Industrial Revolution, global trade, and urbanisation fuelled demand for more diverse 
coverage. By the mid-19th century, reinsurance emerged to distribute large-scale risks 
globally. Over the 20th century, insurance became a core component of the global 
financial system. 
 
1.3 History of the Modern Insurance Sector in India: The Indian life insurance 
industry began in 1818 with the Oriental Life Insurance Company in Kolkata, followed by 
Bombay Life Assurance (1823) and Madras Equitable (1829). The Indian Life Assurance 
Companies Act (1912) was the first legislation, followed by the Insurance Act (1938) 
consolidating regulatory oversight. In 1956, 245 companies were nationalised and 
merged to form the Life Insurance Corporation of India (LIC). General insurance was 
introduced in India in 1850 with Triton Insurance in Kolkata. Indian Mercantile Insurance 
Ltd. (1907) was the first Indian general insurer. Post-independence, the sector was 
nationalised under the General Insurance Business (Nationalisation) Act, 1972, merging 
107 insurers into four PSUs under General Insurance Corporation (GIC). IRDAI was 
established as a statutory regulator in 2000, with powers to register insurers, protect 
policyholders, and develop the sector. Foreign investment caps were progressively 
increased — initially allowing 26% FDI in 2000, increasing to 49% in 2015, and to 74% in 
2021. The number of insurers has since increased from 7 (pre-2000) to 74 (as on date),

Insurance is viable in welfare states where governments provide essential services 
like health care, pensions, and disaster relief. It fosters financial stability, protects 
livelihoods, and supports consumption by reducing vulnerability to adverse events. 
Beyond risk management, insurance facilitates access to credit and channels long-term 
household savings into national development projects through investments. It also 
complements public welfare schemes by promoting individual risk-sharing, easing the 
burden on state systems. Furthermore, insurance encourages entrepreneurship and 
productive investments by enhancing the capacity for risk-taking across various sectors. 
While India implements targeted welfare schemes like Ayushman Bharat, PMFBY, and 
Atal Pension Yojana, coverage to large segments, including informal and middle-income 
groups, is provided by a number of insurance companies. The insurance sector 
complements various public schemes by filling protection gaps, enabling financial 
inclusion, and mobilising long-term funds for nation-building. Moreover, with rising 
healthcare costs, increased life expectancy, and emergence of new risks (cybersecurity, 
climate events), a robust insurance system is essential for a sustainable welfare state.  
 
(a) The regulatory framework governing the insurance sector therefore rests primarily 
on the Insurance Act, 1938 and the Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority Act, 
1999, under which IRDAI functions as the sector regulator responsible for protecting 
policyholder interests and promoting the orderly development of the insurance industry. 
The Committee sought to understand the regulatory architecture governing the insurance 
sector in India and the institutional mechanisms responsible for supervising and 
regulating the insurance industry. In their written submission to the Committee, the IRDAI 
stated as under: 
“Insurance sector in India is regulated by the provisions of Insurance Act, 
1938 and IRDA Act, 1999. IRDAI has holistically examined the provisions of 
Insurance Act, 1938 and IRDA Act, 1999 and amendments to various 
sections of the Acts have been suggested to the Department of Financial 
Services so as to further enhance effective monitoring and development of 

3 
 
insurance sector. Government vide notification dated 26th November 2024, 
has invited public comments on proposed amendments to the Insurance Act, 
1938, and other relevant laws. Provisions under Insurance Act, 1938 and 
IRDA Act, 1999 for regulating the insurance industry have hitherto helped 
protecting the interest of policyholders and the sector has grown since its 
opening. Changes in regulations have been also made over this period as the 
industry keeps evolving and it is a continuous process.  Some of the 
important amendments recommended were (i) raising foreign direct 
investment (FDI) limit from 74% to 100% in the insurance companies; (ii) 
enabling issuance of registrations for composite insurance business so that 
an insurer may carry on one or more classes of insurance business; (iii) 
enabling IRDAI to specify the minimum lower capital norms so that more 
number of insurance companies can be permitted to operate; (iv) enabling 
IRDAI to specify investment norms applicable to the insurance sector; and (v) 
allowing insurers to offer value added services related/incidental to insurance 
business. Further, the requirement of net-owned funds for foreign re-insurers 
establishing a branch in India is also proposed to be reduced from Rs 5,000 
crore to Rs 1,000 crore.  The proposed amendments primarily focus on 
promoting policyholders’ interests, enhancing the financial security of the 
policyholders, facilitating entry of more players in the insurance market 
leading to economic growth and employment generation, enhancing 
efficiencies of the insurance industry, enabling ease of doing business and 
enhancing insurance penetration to achieve the goal of ‘Insurance for All by 
2047’.” 
 
(b) While examining the performance of Insurance Sector CPSUs, the Committee 
sought to understand the historical evolution of the life insurance sector in India, the 
institutional framework governing the sector and the role played by public sector insurers 
in expanding insurance coverage across the country. In their written submission to the 
Committee, the Life Insurance Corporation of India stated as under: 
“On the 19th of January, 1956, life insurance business in India was 
nationalised wherein 154 Indian insurance companies, 16 non-Indian 
companies and 75 provident companies that were operating in India at that 
time were nationalised. Nationalisation was accomplished in two stages – 
initially the management of the companies was taken over by means of an 
Ordinance and later the ownership too by means of a comprehensive bill. The 
Parliament of India passed the Life Insurance Corporation Act on 19th June, 
1956. The Life Insurance Corporation of India was created on 1st September, 
1956, with the objective of spreading life insurance much more widely and in 

4 
 
particular to the rural areas with a view to reach all insurable persons in the 
country, providing them adequate financial cover at a reasonable cost.” 
 
(c) The reorganisation of the sector resulted in the establishment of the General 
Insurance Corporation of India (GIC Re) as the national reinsurer and the formation of 
four public sector general insurers National Insurance Company Limited (NICL), The New 
India Assurance Company Limited (NIACL), Oriental Insurance Company Limited (OICL) 
and United India Insurance Company Limited (UIICL) which were entrusted with carrying 
on general insurance business across the country. The Committee further sought to 
understand the role played by the General Insurance Corporation of India in the Indian 
insurance ecosystem. In their written submission to the Committee, the General 
Insurance Corporation of India (GIC Re) submitted as under: 
“The General Insurance Business (Nationalization) Act (GIBNA) was passed 
in 1972 and the general insurance business was nationalised with effect from 
1st January 1973. 107 insurers were amalgamated and grouped into four 
companies namely National Insurance Company Ltd., the New India 
Assurance Company Ltd., the Oriental Insurance Company Ltd. and the 
United India Insurance Company Ltd. General Insurance Corporation of India 
(GIC Re) was formed in pursuance of Section 9(1) of GIBNA for the purpose 
of superintending, controlling and carrying on the business of general 
insurance. Subsequent to formation of Insurance Regulatory and 
Development Authority (IRDA) in 1999, GIC Re was notified as national re-
insurer and the four subsidiaries were delinked from GIC Re. The General 
Insurance Business (Nationalization) Act (GIBNA) of 1972 was amended in 
2002 and w.e.f. 21.03.2003 the words ‘superintending, controlling and 
carrying on the business of general insurance’ were substituted by ‘carrying 
on reinsurance Business’.” 
 
(d) The establishment of AICIL as a specialised agricultural insurer represented an 
important institutional innovation, creating a dedicated entity focused exclusively on 
agricultural risk management and thereby strengthening the institutional architecture of 
the insurance sector as a whole. The Committee also sought to understand the role of 
specialised public sector insurance institutions established to address specific sectoral 
requirements. In their written submission to the Committee, the Agriculture Insurance 
Company of India Limited (AICIL) furnished information on its establishment and mandate 
as under: 
“The Agriculture Insurance Company of India Limited (AICIL) was 
incorporated on December 20, 2002 and commenced business operations on 
April 1, 2003. It was created as a specialised agency to address the 
increasing challenges in agricultural risk management and to streamline the 

5 
 
implementation of crop insurance schemes in India. Prior to AICIL’s 
formation, crop insurance schemes such as the Comprehensive Crop 
Insurance Scheme (CCIS) and the National Agricultural Insurance Scheme 
(NAIS) were implemented through the General Insurance Corporation of India 
(GIC). Recognizing the need for a focused and dedicated institution, AICIL 
was established with an exclusive mandate to manage agricultural insurance 
in India, with equity participation from General Insurance Corporation of India 
– 35% (GIC), National Bank for Agriculture and Rural Development – 30% 
(NABARD) and 8.75% each by National Insurance Company Ltd. (NICL), 
New India Assurance Company Ltd. (NIACL), Oriental Insurance Company 
Ltd. (OICL), and United India Insurance Company Ltd. (UIICL) making 
collective holding of 35%.” 
 
(e). The Committee also examined the transformation of the insurance sector following 
liberalisation in 2000. The liberalisation marked a transformative shift in the Indian 
insurance landscape, introducing private sector competition while public sector insurers 
continued to play a dominant role in expanding insurance coverage, particularly in rural 
and underserved segments. In this context, in their written submission to the Committee, 
the Life Insurance Corporation of India submitted as under: 
“In the year 2000 insurance sector was liberalised and entry of private players 
was permitted. Today, there are 26 life insurers in Indian market and Life 
Insurance Corporation continues to be the dominant life insurer in this 
liberalised scenario in terms of new business premiums.”

across the country. Before examining their individual roles and performance in 
subsequent chapters, the Committee considers it appropriate to present a brief 
institutional profile of each Insurance Sector CPSU that was examined by the Committee.

(i) Life Insurance Corporation of India (LIC) 
1.6 LIC is India’s largest financial institution and the world’s sixth largest insurance 
company by total premium. Established on 1st September, 1956 through the 
nationalisation of 245 life insurance companies, LIC was created with the explicit 
mandate of providing life insurance to every insurable person in India, especially in rural 
areas. Over seven decades, LIC has grown from a small government entity with Rs. 200 
crore of new business into a global institution with an AUM of Rs. 54 lakh crore as on 
31.03.2025 — making it the largest institutional investor in India. LIC manages 26.89 
crore individual in-force policies and has a workforce of 91606 permanent employees 
supported by over 14.87 lakh active agents (as on 31.3.2025). LIC is the principal 
implementing agency for the Government’s flagship social protection scheme, the 
Pradhan Mantri Jeevan Jyoti Bima Yojana (PMJJBY), and has enrolled over 16 crore 
subscribers under the scheme. LIC is also listed on BSE and NSE following its IPO in 
May 2022, the largest IPO in Indian history. Its market share in First Year Premium 
income stands at 57.05% as on 31.03.2025. 

7 
 
 
(ii) General Insurance Corporation of India (GIC Re) 
1.7 GIC Re is India’s sole national reinsurer, established under the General Insurance 
Business (Nationalisation) Act, 1972 (GIBNA). It was constituted to superintend, control, 
and carry on the business of general insurance reinsurance in India. Subsequent to the 
establishment of IRDAI in 1999 and the delinking of its four subsidiary PSGICs, GIC Re 
was formally designated as the national reinsurer. GIC Re is ranked the 10th largest 
reinsurer globally on a non-IFRS 17 basis as per AM Best Ranking 2024 and is the only 
Indian insurance entity on the Forbes Global 2000 list. GIC Re holds approximately 60% 
share of the domestic reinsurance market and services around 1,144 cedant companies. 
It operates internationally through subsidiaries in South Africa and Russia and 
representative offices in London, Dubai, Malaysia, Moscow, Singapore, and Kuala 
Lumpur. GIC Re is rated A- by AM Best, A3 by Moody’s, and A- by S&P. The 
Government of India holds 82.40% of GIC Re’s equity, with the balance held by the public 
following its IPO (2017) and OFS (2024). GIC Re has paid cumulative dividends of Rs. 
10,576.34 crore to the Government of India since incorporation. 
 
(iii) The New India Assurance Company Limited (NIACL) 
1.8 NIACL is the largest public sector general insurance company in India and among 
the few Indian insurance companies with a significant global presence. Established in 
1919 under the aegis of the Tata Group and subsequently nationalised under GIBNA, 
1972, NIACL has grown with a gross written premium of Rs. 43,618 crore in FY 2024-25 
as per its annual report 2024-25. NIACL is listed on BSE and NSE. It operates with 
11,109 employees across 3,513 offices in India and maintains offices in 25 countries. 
NIACL is the designated implementing insurer under the Pradhan Mantri Suraksha Bima 
Yojana (PMSBY) across several states and union territories, and is a primary insurer 
under the Ayushman Bharat – PMJAY scheme for Andaman & Nicobar Islands, 
Chandigarh, Jammu & Kashmir, Ladakh, and other union territories. NIACL is the only 
PSGIC with a consistently positive solvency ratio, which stood at 1.81 in FY 2023-24. 
 
(iv) National Insurance Company Limited (NICL) 
1.9 NICL was incorporated on 5th December, 1906 and is the second oldest general 
insurance company in India. Nationalised in 1972 under GIBNA, NICL operates from its 
headquarters in Kolkata with 866 offices and 7,004 employees across the country. NICL 
offers a wide range of non-life products including Fire, Marine, Motor, Health, and 
Miscellaneous lines. With a gross direct premium income of Rs. 16,833 crore in FY 2024-
25 (as per annual report 2024-25), NICL commands a market share of approximately 
6.3% in the general insurance market. NICL has been undergoing a strategic turnaround 
under its current management — reducing underwriting losses from Rs. 6,033 crore in 
2022-23 to Rs. 198 crore in 2023-24 and returning to profitability in Q2 and Q3 of FY 

8 
 
2024-25. NICL’s negative solvency ratio of (0.45) in FY 2023-24 is being addressed 
through a Board-approved solvency restoration plan involving a 90% loss ratio target and 
portfolio rationalisation. 
 
(v) The Oriental Insurance Company Limited (OICL) 
1.10 The Oriental Insurance Company was established in 1947, initially as a subsidiary 
of Oriental Life Insurance, and was nationalised under GIBNA, 1972. Headquartered in 
New Delhi, OICL operates through 1,049 offices with 6,220 employees. Its gross premium 
stood at Rs. 15,761 crore in FY 2024-25. OICL has a presence in Nepal, Kuwait, and 
Dubai through foreign branches and offices. OICL’s product portfolio covers Motor, 
Health, Fire, Marine, Engineering, and miscellaneous lines. OICL’s solvency ratio of 
(1.06) in FY 2023-24 is the lowest among all PSGICs, driven primarily by persistent 
losses in Health and Motor TP lines. Despite the capital infusion of Rs. 4,420 crore 
received between 2019-20 and 2021-22, OICL’s solvency turned negative in 2022-23 due 
to continued underwriting losses. OICL returned to profitability at the PAT level in FY 
2023-24 for the first time in several years, marking the beginning of its recovery. 
 
(vi) United India Insurance Company Limited (UIICL) 
1.11 United India Insurance Company was established in 1938 as a subsidiary of the 
Bank of India and was nationalised under GIBNA, 1972. Headquartered in Chennai, 
UIICL operates through 1,359 offices with 8,791 employees. Its gross premium was Rs. 
17,191 crore in FY 2024-25. UIICL has a significant presence in South India and has 
been a key implementing insurer under Ayushman Bharat – PMJAY in Andhra Pradesh 
and Tamil Nadu. UIICL has also been a principal underwriter of the Government’s mass 
health insurance schemes, which has historically contributed to its high claims ratio. 
UIICL’s solvency ratio of (0.59) in FY 2023-24 reflects the impact of cumulative 
underwriting losses. The company received capital infusion of Rs. 3,755 crore between 
2019-20 and 2021-22. UIICL returned to PAT-level profitability in FY 2023-24 with a 
return on net worth of 52.35%, reflecting improving investment income and better portfolio 
management. 
 
(vii) Agriculture Insurance Company of India Limited (AICIL) 
1.12 AICIL was incorporated on 20th December, 2002 and commenced business on 1st 
April, 2003, as a specialised agricultural insurer established to address the insurance 
needs of India’s farming community. AICIL’s shareholding is distributed across the 
Government of India (through GIC Re — 35%, NABARD — 30%, and others), making it a 
unique CPSU with a multi-institutional public ownership structure. AICIL is the market 
leader in Government crop insurance schemes with a 32% market share under the 
Pradhan Mantri Fasal Bima Yojana (PMFBY) and the Restructured Weather Based Crop 
Insurance Scheme (RWBCIS). In the last five years (10 crop seasons), AICIL has insured 

9

50,000 crore under PMFBY and RWBCIS. AICIL has a gross premium of Rs. 9,742 crore 
in FY 2024-25 and a solvency ratio of 4.07 — the highest among all Insurance Sector 
CPSUs. It operates with approximately 375 permanent employees supplemented by 
3,000 field-level outsourced staff deployed across India. AICIL does not require any 
Government capital infusion and has been consistently profitable, paying dividends since 
FY 2021-22.

C. Position of Indian Insurance Sector in Global Context 
Insurance penetration (premium as a percentage of GDP) and insurance density (per 
capita premium expenditure in US dollars) are the two principal indicators used globally to 
compare the relative development of insurance markets. The Committee examined 
India’s standing on these indicators in relation to major global economies. 
 
1.21 The Committee sought comparative information regarding the position of India’s 
insurance sector in relation to global benchmarks. In their written submission to the 
Committee, IRDAI submitted the following information: 
“Over the past decade, India’s insurance penetration (premium as a 
percentage of GDP) is in the range of 3.3% to 4.2%. During the same period, 
the insurance penetration level in Japan, despite declining, remains above 
8%, and China is in the range of 3.2% to 4.6%. The United States, with a 
large mature market, has consistently maintained penetration levels between 
7% and 12%.  In terms of insurance density, India’s growth has been gradual 
— from US$55 in 2014 to US$95 in 2023, driven by incremental gains in both 
life (from US$44 to US$70) and non-life (from US$11 to US$25) segments. In 

13 
 
comparison, China’s density rose from US$235 to US$508, the United States 
from US$4,017 to US$9,640, and Japan — although showing a declining 
trend — still recorded US$2,938 in 2023. These figures reflect India’s position 
has significant potential for growth. Over the past decade, life insurance 
penetration in India has exceeded that of China, and has also surpassed the 
USA in the last four years. Non-life insurance penetration in India has been 
lower than all these countries in all the years. India liberalised its economy in 
early 90s and the insurance sector was opened up for private participation in 
2000 whereas China has initiated the same at least a decade before. FDI in 
India was further liberalised in the insurance sector in March 2021 with the 
limit being increased to 74%. It is expected that insurance market in India will 
grow further in the next decade. The comparative analysis of insurance 
penetration over the past decade highlights India’s steady growth in both life 
and general insurance while indicating scope for significant improvement in 
both segments. While India’s total insurance penetration rose from 3.3% in 
2014 to a peak of 3.7% in 2023-24, the level of insurance density has gone 
up from USD 55 to USD 95 during the same period. The Authority continues 
to take calibrated steps to deepen insurance penetration, including 
simplification of product approvals, expansion of distribution networks, 
rationalisation of regulatory compliance, and encouraging collaboration with 
InsurTech. Focused reforms under the vision of ‘Insurance for All by 2047’ 
aim to build a more inclusive, accessible, and resilient insurance ecosystem, 
with targeted measures to increase penetration across both life and non-life 
segments.”

that particular class of business. As per Section 103 of the Indian Insurance 
Act, 1938, in case any entity is carrying on insurance business without 
registration, the entity is liable to a penalty not exceeding Rs. 25 crores and

violation of code of conduct and/or fraud. Insurance Companies submit Fraud

includes frauds reported under Intermediaries category.”

3.12 The Committee sought to understand the primary causes of continued underwriting 
losses in PSGICs and the corrective measures being taken. In their written submission, 
the Department of Financial Services stated as under: 
“Insurers have consistently faced losses in Motor Third Party (TP) segment, 
primarily because the premiums charged were insufficient to cover the 
volume and severity of claims. These challenges have been aggravated by 
social inflation — an upward trend in claims costs due to increased litigation, 
broader definitions of liability, and higher compensation expectations. 
Additionally, substantial court awards have placed further financial strain on 
insurers operating in this space. Other contributing factors include long tail 
liabilities in Motor TP and other suit claims; slow adoption of digitalisation in 
claims; and slow adoption of fraud detection and predictive analytics. The 
introduction of the ‘One More Pension Option’ (OMOP) in FY 2019-20 had a 
one-time impact on underwriting results of PSGICs. Settlement of all COVID-
related claims also resulted in spikes in underwriting losses. Increased claim 
frequency under Health LoB and better medical facilities including modern 
treatment methods have led to high cost of health claims. To achieve a 
sustainable combined ratio, PSGICs are shifting from a volume-driven to a 
risk-based, profitability-focused underwriting model. This involves better 
underwriting and pricing of group health and motor portfolios, investment in 
technology, data analytics and actuarial tools, digitisation of claims 

47 
 
settlement, fraud detection systems, rationalisation of administrative 
overheads, and optimisation of reinsurance strategies.”

3.24 The Committee sought IRDAI’s views on the adequacy of investment norms for

claim obligations at all times. Given that PSGICs have significant equity

solvency through profitability improvements rather than relying indefinitely on

3.25 The Committee also sought IRDAI’s assessment of whether PSGICs’ investment 
income was sufficient to cover their underwriting losses on a sustained basis. IRDAI 
responded as under: 
“The investment income of PSGICs, while growing, is not sufficient to fully 
offset underwriting losses in most years. In FY 2023-24, the combined 
underwriting loss of the four PSGICs was Rs. 18,862 crore while combined 
investment income was Rs. 18,035 crore — a shortfall of Rs. 827 crore. This 
means that PSGICs are in a structurally precarious position where even 
strong investment performance cannot fully compensate for underwriting 
deficiencies. The sustainable path forward requires PSGICs to achieve a 
combined ratio below 100% through improved underwriting discipline, not 
investment income. IRDAI has been directing PSGICs to adopt risk-based 
pricing, exit loss-making portfolios, and invest in technology to improve claims 
management.”

3.38 The Committee sought IRDAI’s views on the proposed transition to a Risk-Based 
Capital (RBC) framework and its implications for Insurance CPSUs. In this regard, IRDAI 
stated as under: 
“IRDAI has been working on the Indian RBC Framework since 2021, under 
the guidance of an expert consultative committee. The current solvency 
regime is based on a factor-based approach which applies uniform capital 
charges regardless of the actual risk profile of an insurer. The RBC 
framework will replace this with a risk-sensitive capital requirement that 
accounts for underwriting risk, market risk, credit risk, and operational risk. 
For PSGICs with large, high-quality equity portfolios generating unrealised 
gains, RBC may provide partial relief by recognising the true economic value 
of their assets. For insurers with concentrated underwriting risk in volatile 
segments like Motor TP and Health, RBC may require higher capital buffers. 
IRDAI expects to publish the final RBC framework for consultation in FY 
2025-26 with phased implementation from FY 2027-28.”

(i) “Reduction in GST Rates: Historically, insurance and reinsurance were 
never taxed at this high rate, and the expectations from GST were for 
these sectors to be bracketed in the 12% slab. Lowering the GST rate on 
insurance premiums (currently 18%) to levels comparable with global 
peers (e.g., 0% in the UK, 6% in China, 7% in Singapore) could make 

68 
 
insurance products more affordable for the Indian population. This reform 
is expected to boost insurance uptake, particularly among price-sensitive 
segments. 
 
(ii) Zero/Reduced GST for Life and Health Insurance: Exempting or 
reducing GST on life and health insurance and removing reinsurance from 
the ambit of GST as practiced in several countries, would encourage wider 
coverage, especially in underserved rural and low-income markets. 
 
(iii) Input Tax Credit (ITC) Reforms: Allowing broader ITC on expenses 
related to insurance services can further reduce the cost structure for 
insurers, enabling more competitive pricing. 
 
(iv) Simplification & Rationalisation: Streamlining GST compliance, 
reducing/clubbing of forms under GST and clarifying the tax treatment 
across different insurance products would reduce administrative burdens 
and improve transparency for both insurers and policyholders. 
 
(v) Impact: These reforms would likely increase insurance penetration, 
improve financial inclusion, and support the government’s Insurance for 
ALL by 2047 objectives. They would also enhance the competitiveness of 
Indian insurers and reinsurers in the global market. 
 
(vi) Rationale for Different GST Rates on Insurance Policies 
1. Product Nature and Social Importance: Life insurance often serves a 
social security function, while general insurance covers property, health, 
and liability risks. Some countries differentiate GST rates to encourage 
socially beneficial products (e.g., zero GST on life insurance in Singapore 
and the UK). 
 
2. Policy Structure: In India, all insurance products currently attract 18% 
GST except for exempted agriculture policies or for units in SEZ, but 
these can be considered as policy decisions and no changes can be 
expected unless insurance itself is moved to a lower tax bracket. 
Globally, rates differ based on the nature of risk covered and the policy’s 
tenure or investment component. 
 
3. Reinsurance Pricing: The GST paid on direct insurance premiums is 
factored into the cost structure and, subsequently, into reinsurance 

69 
 
pricing. Higher GST on direct insurance raises the overall cost, impacting 
reinsurance demand and pricing. 
 
(vii) Estimated Increase in Insurance Penetration & Benefit to GIC Re 
1. Potential Penetration Increase: International experience suggests that 
reducing GST on insurance can lead to a rise in new policy uptake over a 
few years, especially in price-sensitive segments however presenting an 
accurate percentage would be subjective. For India, a nominal basis point 
increase in penetration (from the current 3.7% towards the global 
average of 7%) over the medium term is possible if GST is reduced to 
single digits. 
 
2. Benefit for GIC Re: Higher insurance penetration would directly increase 
the volume of primary insurance business, leading to greater demand for 
reinsurance. This would enhance GIC Re’s premium income, market 
share, and contribution to financial stability. 
 
(viii) Premium Loss Attributed to GST-Related Affordability Issues 
Estimated Impact: While exact figures vary, industry estimates indicate 
that high GST rates contribute to a reduction in potential premium volumes 
for direct insurers due to affordability barriers. This, in turn, leads to a 
proportional reduction in reinsurance demand, impacting GIC Re’s 
business pipeline.” 
 
4.14 The Committee also examined the impact of GST on insurance products, 
particularly agricultural insurance. In their written submission to the Committee, AICIL 
stated as under: 
“Currently, Government-sponsored crop insurance schemes like PMFBY and 
RWBCIS are GST-exempt vide entry number 35 of notification number 
12/2017 Central Tax (Rate) dated 28th June 2017. Cattle Insurance falling

70 
 
currently covered under any form of crop insurance. This leaves nearly 9.5

million animals, of which less than 5% are currently insured under any formal 
livestock insurance scheme. Removing GST on agricultural insurance 
products will lower the premium burden and make insurance more attractive

PART-II 
 
OBSERVATIONS/RECOMMENDATIONS OF THE COMMITTEE 
 
Overview 
The Committee note that the insurance sector plays a critical role in India's 
financial system by providing protection against risk and uncertainty, mobilising 
long-term savings, and channelling capital into national development. The 
Department of Financial Services (DFS) under the Ministry of Finance oversees 
seven Central Public Sector Undertakings (CPSUs) in this sector. These are Life 
Insurance Corporation of India (LIC), General Insurance Corporation of India (GIC 
Re), Agriculture Insurance Company of India Limited (AICIL), The New India 
Assurance Company Limited (NIACL), National Insurance Company Limited (NICL), 
The Oriental Insurance Company Limited (OICL), and United India Insurance 
Company Limited (UIICL). These entities collectively serve hundreds of millions of 
policyholders across life, general, agricultural, and reinsurance segments, making 
them the backbone of insurance coverage in India, particularly for the poor, the 
rural population, and the vulnerable. The Committee observe that India's insurance 
history dates to 1818, progressing through nationalisation in 1956 and 1972 to the 
present liberalised landscape of 74 insurers. The establishment of IRDAI as an 
independent regulator in 2000 opened the sector to private and foreign 
competition, and FDI limits have been consequently raised progressively to 74% in 
2021. India today ranks 10th globally by total insurance premium volume at USD 
136 billion and is projected to become the 6th largest insurance market by 2032. 
While acknowledging this growth, India's insurance penetration of 3.7% of GDP 
against a global average of 7%, and a density of USD 95 per capita against USD 
9,640 in the United States, indicate a substantial coverage differential that the 
sector is encouraged to systematically mitigate. The Committee also observe that 
LIC, the world's 6th largest insurer with an AUM of Rs. 54,52,297 crore, is the 
largest institutional investor in India, while GIC Re is the 10th largest reinsurer 
globally and commands approximately 60% of the domestic reinsurance market. 
AICIL is the market leader in Government crop insurance schemes and maintains 

79 
 
the highest solvency ratio among all Insurance Sector CPSUs at 4.07. hese 
achievements demonstrate that public sector insurers are capable of achieving 
sector-leading excellence when enabled by strategic governance and robust 
institutional support.  
The Committee are also aware that the sector presents a comprehensive 
landscape for strategic modernisation. While three of the four Public Sector 
General Insurance Companies (NICL, OICL, and UIICL) currently report negative 
solvency ratios following combined Government capital infusions of Rs. 17,450 
crore between 2019-20 and 2021-22, this establishes a clear baseline for 
subsequent capital optimisation. The imperative to calibrate underwriting 
frameworks in the Motor Third Party and Health segments, diversify risk allocation 
in social scheme implementation, expand the scope of operational autonomy, 
revitalise talent acquisition models, and review the GST framework on insurance 
products collectively underscores the pathway to restoring the competitive 
resilience and long-term financial robustness of public sector insurers. The 
Committee are of the considered view that many of these operational complexities 
are capable of being addressed through a combination of governance reforms, 
operational improvements, technological modernisation, prudent risk management 
and appropriate policy support. The Committee also note that several Insurance 
Sector CPSUs have demonstrated encouraging progress in recent years and 
therefore possess the institutional capability required to successfully undertake 
further reforms and improvements. Taking all these factors into consideration, the 
Committee have put forth their Observations and Recommendations in the 
subsequent paragraphs, chapter-wise, after having heard the views of DFS, IRDAI, 
Insurance Sector CPSUs and some of the major Private Sector Insurance 
companies. The Committee hope that DFS and IRDAI make earnest efforts to 
implement these Observations and Recommendations in letter and spirit, so that 
Insurance Sector as a whole alongwith the Public Sector CPSUs can grow 
holistically and serve the people of India more effectively.

2.  Expanding Insurance Penetration Towards the Global Norm 
The Committee note that India's insurance penetration has remained 
stagnant at around 3.7% of GDP over recent years, well below the global average of 
7%, while insurance density stands at USD 95 per capita against a global average 
of USD 889. The Committee take note of the explanation offered by DFS that the 
stagnation in penetration is partly a reflection of India's rapidly growing GDP rather 
than an actual decline in the number of people insured. However, the Committee 
observe that in absolute terms, a large majority of India's population, including a 
significant proportion of the middle-income group, currently navigates suboptimal 
levels of life, health, or property insurance cover. Non-life insurance penetration in 
particular, at 1.0% of GDP, presents a variance from the world average of 4.2% and 
indicates a substantial coverage differential in property, health, and liability 
coverage. The Committee are of the view that insurance penetration should not 
merely be assessed through premium-to-GDP ratios but also through the extent of 
meaningful financial protection available to households, farmers, workers, small 
businesses and vulnerable sections of society. The persistence of a sizeable 
protection gap indicates the need for a coordinated strategy encompassing 
affordability, accessibility, awareness, product suitability and ease of claims 
settlement. The Committee further note that rapid digitisation, expanding financial 
inclusion infrastructure and growing public awareness provide significant 
opportunities for accelerating insurance adoption across the country. The 
Committee recommend that DFS and IRDAI set specific, time-bound targets for 
increasing both life and non-life penetration, with annual milestones published 
transparently so that progress can be tracked. Further, such targets may be 
disaggregated, wherever feasible, by geography, demographic segment and line of 
business so as to facilitate more focused policy interventions and performance 
monitoring. The Ministry may also commission periodic independent assessments 
of how each intervention from GST rationalisation to digital distribution is 
contributing to penetration growth, so that policy corrections can be made in time. 
The Committee further recommend periodic review of progress achieved under 

81 
 
these initiatives so that emerging opportunities for process optimisation may be 
identified and addressed in a timely manner. 
3. Leveraging State-Owned Insurers as Vehicles of Financial Inclusion 
The Committee observe that public sector insurers have historically played a 
foundational role in extending insurance to rural, social, and underserved 
segments where private insurers have had limited presence. Also, public sector 
insurers have consistently acted as instruments of social and financial inclusion 
by ensuring the availability of insurance services in regions and segments that 
may not always be commercially attractive, as such areas remains important for 
achieving balanced and inclusive growth of the insurance sector. Data shows that 
public sector insurers underwrote 18.38% of total premium from rural areas in FY 
2023-24, reflecting a proportional transition from 24.42% in 2019-20, even as the 
absolute rural premium amount grew. The Committee note that the rural market 
share of public insurers is experiencing a demographic variance in the context of 
India's aim for Insurance for All by 2047, and the share of crop insurance business 
underwritten by the public sector has also recorded a shift relative to private 
insurers in recent years. The Committee are of the considered view that expansion 
of insurance coverage in rural and underserved areas contributes not only to 
financial inclusion but also to economic stability and resilience. Increased 
insurance protection can help households better manage risks associated with 
agriculture, health, livelihoods and natural disasters, thereby reducing vulnerability 
and supporting sustainable development. The Committee therefore recommend 
that DFS should instruct Insurance Sector CPSUs to prepare dedicated rural 
expansion strategies with segment-specific goals particularly in crop insurance, 
livestock insurance, microinsurance, and low-ticket health products and that IRDAI 
should also monitor and publish annual progress reports on rural and social sector 
obligations for all insurers. The Committee further recommend periodic 
assessment of outcomes achieved under such strategies and dissemination of 
best practices across insurers so that successful approaches may be replicated 
more widely. 

82 
 
4. Strengthening Board Governance and Independent Director Appointments 
The Committee note that the constitution of Independent Directors on the 
Boards of several Insurance Sector CPSUs presents a primary mandate for 
governance alignment, as current compositions diverge from the structural 
minimums outlined in IRDAI's Corporate Governance Regulations. The five 
financial years from 2020-21 to 2024-25 at AICIL highlight an immediate opportunity 
to formalise board constitution and meet the regulatory requirement of a minimum 
of three Independent Directors. GIC Re's current structure of one Independent 
Director similarly establishes a clear target for fulfilling the prescribed minimum of 
three. The compositional variances across several PSGICs in recent years further 
underscore the sector-wide imperative to accelerate structural governance 
appointments. The Committee recognise that independent oversight is essential 
for sound governance of institutions that manage the savings and risk protection 
of crores of citizens. The Committee further note that strong governance 
frameworks assume particular importance in insurance institutions because of the 
long-term nature of insurance liabilities and the fiduciary responsibility towards 
policyholders. Independent Directors bring specialised expertise, strengthen 
oversight of risk management practices and contribute to balanced decision-
making. Their timely appointment therefore supports not only regulatory 
compliance but also institutional effectiveness and stakeholder confidence. 
Conversely, variances in Independent Director appointments have the potential to 
influence Board-level deliberations, alter accountability dynamics, and affect 
investor and policyholder assurance. The Committee note that extended pending 
appointments may impact governance frameworks and strategic oversight.  
The Committee therefore highlight the strategic value of robust institutional 
mechanisms that facilitate the proactive identification and induction of qualified 
candidates to maintain optimal board constitution. The Committee instruct DFS to 
prioritise the appointment of Independent Directors across all Insurance CPSUs, 
mandating the fulfilment of all pending appointments within a strict six-month 
timeframe. To sustain optimal governance continuity and eliminate extended 

83 
 
compositional variances, the framework for identifying and empanelling 
professionals with domain expertise in insurance, finance, actuarial science, or 
technology must be modernised for maximum transparency and efficiency. 
Furthermore, the Committee encourage the strategic expansion of the prospective 
Independent Director pool by integrating expertise from academia, financial 
services, technology, risk management, and public administration, thereby 
elevating Board deliberations and fortifying long-term institutional governance.  
5.  Operational Independence and Level Playing Field for Public Sector Insurers 
The Committee note that Public Sector General Insurance Companies face 
structural disadvantages in competing with private insurers. These include lower 
managerial pay making it difficult to attract top talent, more limited operational 
flexibility, and the obligation to implement social protection schemes that private 
insurers largely avoid. As acknowledged by IRDAI before the Committee, the 
variances in senior management compensation frameworks represent a primary 
factor contributing to the performance differentials between PSGICs and their 
private market peers. The Committee note that strengthening the operational 
capabilities of PSGICs would not only improve their market positioning but also 
enhance their ability to fulfil broader developmental objectives. Further, the 
Committee note that equitable access to distribution channels is an important 
prerequisite for healthy competition within the insurance sector. A balanced 
distribution of business opportunities would contribute towards strengthening the 
financial sustainability of all market participants while preserving customer choice. 
In this regard, the Committee also note the portfolio distribution patterns in 
bancassurance highlight a clear mandate for strategic realignment while public 
sector banks channel PMSBY and other mandatory social scheme business to 
PSGICs, the current engagement with private insurers for commercially optimal 
loan-linked products presents an opportunity to expand PSGICs' bancassurance 
share beyond the current 1% of their portfolio. 

84 
 
The Committee recommend that DFS work with the Reserve Bank of India 
and IRDAI to issue clear guidelines ensuring that public sector banks distribute 
social protection scheme business equitably across all insurers, and that PSGICs 
receive fair access to the loan-linked insurance business that currently flows 
primarily to private sector bancassurance tie-ups. The Committee further 
recommend that DFS explore flexible and performance linked compensation 
frameworks for PSGIC senior management and specialist roles within the bounds 
of public sector norms to enable competitive talent acquisition, particularly in 
areas such as actuarial science, data analytics, investment management, and 
technology. The Committee further note that such availability of specialised human 
resources will increasingly determine the ability of insurers to manage emerging 
risks, undertake digital transformation and compete effectively in a rapidly 
evolving marketplace. At the same time, the Committee also note IRDAI's 
assurance that from a regulatory standpoint there is no distinction between public 
and private insurers, and that regulatory forbearance extended to PSGICs will not 
continue indefinitely. The Committee recommend that PSGICs use the breathing 
room provided by the current recovery to fundamentally reform their operations 
rather than simply relying on regulatory leniency. The Committee are confident that 
with continued operational improvements and appropriate policy support, PSGICs 
can strengthen their market position and improve long-term financial 
sustainability. 
6.  Improving Operational Performance and Reducing Claims Ratios 
The Committee note that PSGICs' combined Incurred Claims Ratio (ICR) 
stood at 97.23% in FY 2023-24, compared to 76.49% for private non-life insurers 
presenting a differential of over 20 percentage points that highlights a strategic 
opportunity for portfolio optimisation in the Motor Third Party and Health 
segments. The Motor ICR of PSGICs was 99.57% and Health ICR was 103.16% in FY 
2023-24, compared to 73.30% and 83.49% respectively for private insurers. PSGICs 
are legally required to underwrite Motor TP policies for any vehicle owner who 
seeks cover, and cannot exit this business, even as Motor TP premiums have 

85 
 
remained inadequate relative to rising court awards and accident claim 
settlements. In the Health segment, rapidly rising hospital costs, the absence of 
standardised pricing across hospitals, and the high GST burden on senior citizen 
policies have driven up claims without a commensurate ability to reprice. The 
Committee observe that persistently elevated claims ratios place considerable 
pressure on profitability, solvency and capital adequacy.  
The Committee are of the view that sustained improvement in underwriting 
quality and claims management will be essential for strengthening the financial 
health of PSGICs. The Committee further note that effective use of data analytics, 
fraud detection systems, predictive modelling and technology-enabled claims 
processing can contribute significantly towards reducing claims costs and 
improving operational efficiency. The Committee recommend that DFS, IRDAI, and 
the Ministry of Road Transport and Highways work jointly to revise Motor TP 
premium rates to actuarially sound levels on an expedited timeline, and that IRDAI 
develop a standardised hospital pricing framework for cashless health insurance 
claims to enable better cost control. The Committee also recommend that 
stakeholders continue examining measures for improving pricing efficiency and 
reducing systemic cost pressures within major lines of business. Further, PSGICs 
could be encouraged to exit persistently loss-making segments of group 
mediclaim business as NICL has begun to do rather than continuing to accumulate 
underwriting losses to preserve top-line market share. The Committee further 
recommend periodic review of claims experience across major product categories 
so that corrective action may be undertaken in a timely manner. 
7. Policyholder Services, Grievance Redressal and Unclaimed Funds 
The Committee note that while LIC disposes 99.20% of grievances within 15 
days and AICIL has an average grievance turnaround time of under 7 days, PSGICs 
as a group present a strategic opportunity to drive performance parity with private 
non-life insurers in this metric, having processed 92.52% within 15 days in FY 
2024-25 against the 98% industry benchmark. The Committee also observe that 

86 
 
unclaimed policyholder funds with PSGICs stood at Rs. 851 crore as at end of FY 
2023-24, an increase from Rs. 600 crore in 2019-20 indicating that many rightful 
claimants or beneficiaries are not receiving funds that belong to them. Further, 
LIC's welcome reduction of unclaimed funds from Rs. 17,318 crore to Rs. 7,165 
crore in FY 2024-25 demonstrates what proactive outreach can achieve. The 
Committee therefore consider efficient grievance redressal to be one of the most 
important indicators of service quality and customer confidence in the insurance 
sector. Timely resolution of grievances not only enhances customer satisfaction 
but also strengthens trust in insurance institutions. The Committee appreciate the 
efforts undertaken by LIC in this regard and are of the view that similar practices 
may be adopted more widely across the insurance sector. The Committee are of 
the considered view that all Insurance Sector CPSUs should adopt specific 
turnaround time targets for grievance redressal that meet/exceed private sector 
standards, and to this extent, IRDAI publish comparative data on grievance 
redressal performance annually to incentivise improvement. The Committee also 
recommend that CPSUs proactively reach unclaimed policyholders through all 
available channels and set a target to reduce unclaimed fund balances to minimum 
levels within a defined timeframe. The Committee further recommend greater use 
of technology-enabled search mechanisms and data integration tools to facilitate 
identification of policyholders and beneficiaries, as also, the Cases of mis-selling 
and Unfair Business Practices must be monitored rigorously through Fraud 
Monitoring Reports and appropriate action taken. The Committee also recommend 
periodic review of grievance disposal timelines and unclaimed fund balances so 
that progress may be monitored effectively. 
8. Restoring Financial Health and Solvency of Public Sector General Insurance 
Companies 
The Committee note with concern that three of the four PSGICs i.e. NICL, 
OICL, and UIICL have negative solvency ratios, with OICL recording the lowest at 
(1.06) in FY 2023-24, and that cumulatively all four PSGICs have incurred 
underwriting losses of approximately Rs. 96,861 crore over five years (FY 2019-20 

87 
 
to 2023-24). The Committee acknowledge that all four PSGICs have returned to 
PAT-level profitability, which is a positive development. However, the Committee 
note that the current recovery facilitated by investment income and IRDAI's 
regulatory frameworks for the Fair Value Change Account highlights a strategic 
opportunity to drive systemic enhancements in core underwriting performance.  In 
FY 2023-24, combined underwriting variances of Rs. 18,862 crore were primarily 
counterbalanced by an investment income of Rs. 18,035 crore, indicating that 
PSGICs' ongoing financial sustainability is currently closely correlated with market 
performance. The Committee note that solvency is one of the most important 
indicators of an insurer's financial strength and capacity to honour policyholder 
obligations and profitability supported predominantly by investment income, 
though encouraging in the short term, cannot substitute for sustained 
improvement in core underwriting performance. Long-term financial sustainability 
would require balanced progress across underwriting, claims management, cost 
control and risk management. The Committee, while highlighting the strategic 
imperative to transition towards a financially resilient operational model for public 
institutions managing policyholder capital, recommend that DFS and IRDAI 
collaboratively oversee Board-approved solvency restoration plans for each 
PSGIC, featuring specific quarterly milestones and independent review. 
Furthermore, the Committee urge that further Government capital infusion be 
positioned as a supplementary measure, evaluated only following the maximal 
optimisation of internal mechanisms for solvency enhancement. The Committee 
further recommend that solvency restoration plans include measurable 
operational, financial and technological milestones capable of being monitored 
periodically. The Committee are confident that timely implementation of these 
measures would contribute significantly towards strengthening the long-term 
financial resilience of PSGICs. To this extent, the Committee desire that DFS within 
six months, present a comprehensive solvency restoration status report for each 
of the three PSGICs with negative solvency, including the specific business mix 
changes, profitability targets, and technology investments that each company is 
making to achieve the regulatory minimum solvency ratio of 1.50. 

88 
 
9. Financial Performance of LIC – Sustaining Strength and Improving Quality of 
Business 
The Committee note that LIC has demonstrated strong financial performance 
with PAT of Rs. 48,151 crore in FY 2024-25, AUM of Rs. 54,52,297 crore, an 
improving VNB margin of 17.1%, and a solvency ratio of 2.02, all achieved without 
any Government capital support since nationalisation in 1956. The Committee 
appreciate LIC's consistent dividend payments to the Government of India and its 
role as the principal implementing agency for PMJJBY. The Committee further note 
that LIC continues to enjoy a unique position in the Indian insurance landscape 
owing to its extensive reach, strong brand recognition and long-standing public 
trust. However, the Committee also note that LIC's evolving market share dynamics 
in First Year Premium Income—from 74.18% in 2006-07 to 57.05% in 2024-25 
highlight a strategic opportunity for market realignment. Furthermore, the 
bancassurance channel's current contribution of 4.13% of FYPI presents a clear 
mandate to accelerate structural growth towards the 10%+ target by FY 2028. 
Further, the October 2024 regulatory changes on minimum sum assured and 
surrender value provisioning, have resulted in a moderation in the volume of 
policies issued. The Committee note that increasing competition and changing 
consumer preferences require continuous adaptation of business models and 
distribution strategies. The Committee are of the view that LIC's scale and 
institutional strengths provide significant opportunities for maintaining leadership 
while simultaneously driving operational excellence and transformative service 
delivery. The Committee recommend LIC implement a proactive, time-bound 
strategic roadmap to expand its market presence by elevating the effectiveness of 
its bancassurance partnerships, diversifying its product portfolio towards higher-
margin non-par and protection products, and expanding its digital distribution 
channels. LIC's policy persistency ratio, currently between 64% and 67% at the 13-
month interval, presents a primary area for operational focus, as current 
persistency levels influence Value of New Business (VNB) and policy retention 
metrics. The Committee recommend LIC to prioritise focused efforts towards 

89 
 
improving policy persistency by strengthening customer engagement and service 
quality protocols, ensuring that long-term customer relationship stability is 
consistently maintained. 
10. GIC Re – Sustaining Profitability and Strengthening Core Underwriting 
Performance 
The Committee note that GIC Re has demonstrated a remarkable financial 
turnaround, moving from losses in FY 2019-20 to record profitability in FY 2024-25, 
while maintaining a strong solvency position and continuing to contribute 
substantial dividends to the Government of India. The Committee further 
appreciate GIC Re's successful expansion into international markets and its 
emergence as one of the world's leading reinsurers. However, the Committee note 
that although profitability has advanced, the combined ratio highlights a strategic 
mandate to enhance underwriting margins, thereby strengthening the long-term 
balance between core operational yield and investment returns. The Committee 
while acknowledging that GIC Re occupies a strategically important position within 
the Indian insurance ecosystem are of the view that although investment income 
remains an important component of profitability, sustained improvement in 
technical performance would further strengthen the company's long-term financial 
position and competitiveness in global markets. The Committee recommend that 
GIC Re continue efforts to improve underwriting discipline, diversify business 
across geographies and lines of business, and strengthen risk assessment 
frameworks. The Committee further recommend continued investment in analytical 
capabilities, catastrophe modelling, portfolio optimisation and risk management 
systems in order to enhance underwriting outcomes and operational resilience. 
The Committee expect continued efforts towards strengthening performance and 
improving technical profitability. 
  

90 
 
11. AICIL – Protecting Agricultural Insurance Continuity and Expanding Farmer 
Coverage 
The Committee note that AICIL's gross premium income recorded a variance 
from Rs. 14,620 crore in FY 2022-23 to Rs. 9,742 crore in FY 2024-25, reflecting 
shifts in PMFBY participation, market competition and broader industry dynamics.  
Nevertheless, the Committee also note with satisfaction that AICIL continues to 
maintain strong profitability and the highest solvency ratio among Insurance 
Sector CPSUs. The Committee observe that increasing climate variability, extreme 
weather events and evolving agricultural risks underscore the importance of 
maintaining a robust and financially sustainable agricultural insurance framework. 
The Committee note the administrative matters regarding the release of subsidies 
by certain State Governments, as well as the operational dynamics associated with 
the current tender-based framework. The Committee observe that timely settlement 
of claims and prompt release of premium subsidies are important for maintaining 
farmer confidence in insurance programmes and ensuring long-term sustainability. 
The Committee therefore recommend that the Ministry of Agriculture and Farmers 
Welfare, DFS and IRDAI continue working together to ensure that agricultural 
insurance remains financially viable, actuarially sound and operationally effective. 
The Committee also recommend focused efforts towards increasing coverage of 
non-loanee farmers and livestock insurance through awareness campaigns, 
technology-enabled outreach and simplified enrolment processes. The Committee 
are of the view that expansion of voluntary agricultural insurance coverage would 
also contribute significantly towards reducing rural vulnerability and strengthening 
risk management in the agricultural sector. 
12. Investment Management and Capital Adequacy 
The Committee note that Insurance Sector CPSUs collectively manage some 
of the largest pools of long-term financial assets in the country and play a critical 
role in capital formation and financial market stability. Investment income has 
provided substantial support to the financial performance of several insurers, 

91 
 
particularly PSGICs, during periods of underwriting stress. The Committee note 
that prudent investment management is essential not only for generating returns 
but also for safeguarding policyholder interests and maintaining long-term 
institutional stability. Given the scale of assets under management, investment 
decisions undertaken by Insurance Sector CPSUs have implications extending 
beyond individual institutions. The Committee recommend that all Insurance 
Sector CPSUs continue strengthening investment governance frameworks, risk 
management systems and asset-liability management practices. The Committee 
further emphasise that investment strategies should continue to balance safety, 
liquidity and returns while remaining aligned with the long-term nature of 
insurance liabilities. Strong Board oversight and professional investment 
management practices would contribute significantly towards maintaining financial 
resilience. The Committee suggest that Insurance Sector CPSUs formalise a 
periodic review mechanism for investment performance, while fostering the cross-
sectoral dissemination of benchmarking insights and operational best practices.  
13. Market Share, Competition and Transition to Risk-Based Capital 
The Committee note that public sector insurers' combined share of the non-
life insurance market has declined significantly over the years while private sector 
insurers have expanded their presence across multiple segments. The Committee 
recognise that a degree of market share redistribution is a natural consequence of 
increased competition, however, the pace of decline in certain segments warrants 
careful examination. The Committee note that while market share is a recognised 
metric, it should be viewed in conjunction with other indicators of institutional 
performance, such as business quality, profitability, customer retention, service 
standards, and financial sustainability, which collectively inform long-term 
viability. The Committee to this extent recommend that each Insurance Sector 
CPSU prepare a clear market positioning strategy identifying priority customer 
segments, competitive strengths and business development opportunities. The 
Committee observe that IRDAI's strategic transition towards a Risk-Based Capital 
(RBC) regime represents a landmark regulatory advancement, proactively 

92 
 
strengthening systemic risk management and establishing a robust mechanism to 
harmonise capital requirements with contemporary risk profiles. The Committee 
are of the view that successful implementation of RBC would improve sectoral 
resilience, promote sound risk practices and strengthen policyholder protection. 
The Committee recommend that IRDAI expedite finalisation of the framework and 
provide adequate implementation support to all insurers. 
14. Digital Transformation and Claims Automation 
The Committee observe that the current digital infrastructure of PSGICs 
presents a strategic opportunity to accelerate technological integration and drive 
alignment with leading industry standards. While LIC and AICIL have made 
substantial progress in automation and digital integration, claims processing and 
customer service functions within PSGICs continue to rely heavily on manual 
processes. The Committee further observe that digital maturity levels vary 
considerably across Insurance Sector CPSUs. The Committee note that digital 
transformation has emerged as a critical driver of competitiveness, operational 
efficiency and customer satisfaction within the insurance industry and are of the 
view that investment in digital infrastructure should be regarded as a strategic 
priority rather than a technology initiative as successful digital transformation can 
contribute significantly towards improving underwriting quality, customer 
acquisition, operational productivity and risk management. The Committee 
recommend that DFS establish clear and measurable digital transformation 
benchmarks for PSGICs and that each company prepare a comprehensive 
roadmap with defined milestones. The Committee further recommend greater 
emphasis on claims automation, fraud analytics, customer self-service platforms 
and integration with emerging digital insurance infrastructure. The Committee note 
that integration with Bima Sugam and related digital initiatives offers significant 
opportunities for expanding access and improving efficiency. The Committee 
therefore recommend and encourage wider adoption of advanced technologies 
including artificial intelligence, machine learning, predictive analytics and data-
driven decision-making tools, subject to appropriate governance and risk controls. 

93 
 
15. GST Rationalisation on Insurance Products 
The Committee note that India presently levies GST at the rate of 18% on 
most insurance products, which is among the higher rates prevailing 
internationally. The Committee further note that various stakeholders have 
represented that the existing tax structure increases the cost of insurance 
products and may adversely affect affordability, particularly for middle-income 
households, senior citizens and first-time policyholders. The Committee recognise 
that affordability remains one of the most important determinants of insurance 
adoption. The Committee are of the view that measures aimed at reducing the 
effective cost of insurance have the potential to significantly improve insurance 
penetration and support the national objective of wider financial protection. The 
Committee also note that several countries with higher levels of insurance 
penetration follow relatively lower taxation regimes for insurance products. While 
recognising that taxation policy involves broader fiscal considerations, the 
Committee believe that the impact of GST on insurance affordability merits careful 
examination.The Committee further observe that insurance should be viewed not 
merely as a financial product but also as a social protection instrument that helps 
households manage financial risks arising from illness, accidents, death, property 
damage and natural calamities. Increased insurance coverage can reduce future 
economic distress and contribute positively to financial stability. The Committee 
while noting that the GST Council has already constituted a Group of Ministers to 
examine opportunities for optimisation relating to GST on insurance products, 
recommend that the matter be examined on priority, keeping in view the objectives 
of affordability, financial inclusion and Insurance for All by 2047. The Committee 
further recommend that the matter of GST on health insurance, term life insurance, 
agricultural insurance products and reinsurance services may be examined 
comprehensively so that the overall impact on insurance penetration and 
affordability can be properly assessed. The Committee are of the view that 
measures which improve affordability without compromising fiscal sustainability 

94 
 
would contribute positively towards expanding insurance coverage and reducing 
the existing protection gap. 
16. Regulatory and Policy Reforms to Accelerate Insurance Penetration 
The Committee observe that IRDAI has undertaken significant regulatory 
reforms in recent years including reduction in the number of regulations, adoption 
of use-and-file procedures for products, simplification of regulatory processes and 
development of digital infrastructure initiatives such as Bima Sugam. The 
Committee appreciate these initiatives and note that they have the potential to 
improve operational flexibility, encourage innovation and enhance ease of doing 
business within the insurance sector. However, the Committee note that several 
areas for strategic alignment continue to influence insurance penetration, 
including opportunities for coverage optimisation in health and agriculture, the 
potential for broader inclusivity among certain population groups, and the clear 
mandate for elevated awareness regarding the benefits of insurance products. The 
Committee recommend that DFS, IRDAI and the concerned Ministries develop a 
coordinated action plan to navigate these operational complexities and accelerate 
insurance adoption across all segments of society. The Committee further 
recommend that particular attention may be given to underserved groups including 
small farmers, informal sector workers, micro and small enterprises, senior 
citizens and populations residing in remote and difficult areas. The Committee also 
recommend that technology-enabled verification systems, simplified enrolment 
procedures and enhanced awareness campaigns be utilised to improve 
accessibility and encourage voluntary participation in insurance schemes. The 
Committee further recommend that DFS periodically review the progress of major 
policy initiatives and undertake course corrections wherever necessary. 
  

95 
 
17. Social and Developmental Role – CSR and ESG  
The Committee note that Insurance Sector CPSUs perform an important 
developmental role beyond their commercial operations and continue to contribute 
significantly through Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) initiatives and various 
social sector programmes. The Committee appreciate the efforts undertaken by 
Insurance Sector CPSUs in areas such as education, healthcare, skill development, 
environmental sustainability and community welfare. The Committee recommend 
that Insurance Sector CPSUs continue to align their CSR initiatives with national 
development priorities and strengthen monitoring mechanisms to ensure effective 
utilisation of resources. The Committee further recommend periodic independent 
evaluation of major CSR projects so that successful interventions may be scaled 
up and replicated wherever appropriate. The Committee also note the increasing 
importance of Environmental, Social and Governance (ESG) considerations in the 
insurance sector, as these principles are increasingly becoming integral 
components of responsible business practices and risk management frameworks. 
Therefore, adoption of appropriate ESG practices can contribute towards long-term 
sustainability, improved governance standards and enhanced stakeholder 
confidence. The Committee encourage Insurance Sector CPSUs to continue 
integrating ESG considerations into their business strategies and operational 
processes. 
18. Achieving Insurance for All by 2047 
The Committee note the vision articulated by IRDAI for achieving Insurance 
for All by 2047 under which every citizen would have appropriate life, health and 
property insurance coverage and every enterprise would have access to suitable 
risk protection solutions. The Committee appreciate the efforts being undertaken 
through initiatives such as Bima Sugam, Bima Vistaar and Bima Vahak which seek 
to create a more accessible, affordable and inclusive insurance ecosystem. The 
Committee further note that despite substantial progress in recent years, 
Considerable administrative considerations remain regarding affordability, 

96 
 
accessibility, awareness, distribution reach and insurance literacy. The Committee 
are of the view that addressing these operational complexities will require 
sustained efforts over an extended period and close coordination among 
Government Ministries, State Governments, regulators, insurers, financial 
institutions, intermediaries and local delivery channels. The Committee 
recommend that DFS and IRDAI prepare a comprehensive roadmap for achieving 
Insurance for All by 2047 with clearly defined institutional responsibilities, 
measurable milestones and periodic review mechanisms. The Committee further 
recommend that the roadmap incorporate outcome-based performance indicators 
covering insurance penetration, insurance density, rural coverage, women's 
participation, claims settlement efficiency and customer service standards so that 
progress can be monitored objectively. The Committee also recommend that 
periodic assessments be undertaken to identify implementation operational 
complexities and facilitate timely policy adjustments. The Committee further 
emphasise that public sector insurers will continue to play a pivotal role in 
achieving this vision through their extensive reach, institutional capacity and 
commitment to serving underserved segments of society.
