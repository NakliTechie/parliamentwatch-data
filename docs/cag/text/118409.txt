This Report for the year ended 31 March 2016 has been prepared for submission 
to the Governor of the State of Gujarat.
This Report relates to Audit of receipts and expenditure of the Local Bodies in 
Gujarat conducted under provisions of the Comptroller and Auditor General 
(Duties, Power and Conditions of Service) Act, 1971 and read with proviso 
of Gujarat Panchayat Act, 1993, Gujarat Provincial Municipal Corporations 
Act, 1949 and Gujarat Municipalities Act, 1963, as amended on 04 April 2011 
which empowers the Comptroller and Auditor General of India to conduct 
Audit of the accounts of Panchayati Raj Institutions (PRLs) and Urban Local 
Bodies (ULBs), and submit such Audit Report to the State Government for its 
placement in the State Legislature.
The instances mentioned in this Report are those, which came to notice in the 
course of test audit for the period 2015-16 as well as those, which came to 
notice in earlier years, but could not be reported in the previous Audit Reports; 
instances relating to the period subsequent to 2015-16 have also been included, 
wherever necessary.
The Audit has been conducted in conformity with the Auditing Standards  
(March 2002) issued by the Comptroller and Auditor General of India.

OVERVIEW
This Report contains four chapters. The first and the third chapters contain an 
overview of the functioning, accountability mechanism and financial reporting 
issues of Panchayati Raj Institutions (PRIs) and Urban Local Bodies (ULBs) 
respectively. The second chapter contains one Compliance Audit paragraph 
and one individual paragraph on the Audit of financial transactions of PRIs. 
The fourth chapter contains two Performance Audit paragraphs and two 
Compliance Audit paragraphs on ULBs. A synopsis of the findings contained in 
the Performance Audits and Compliance Audits are presented in this overview.
	 1	 An	 overview	of	the	functioning,	accountability	mechanism	and	financial	
reporting issues of Panchayati Raj Institutions
The State Government could devolve only 19 out of 29 functions to the PRIs as 
envisaged in the 11th Schedule of the Constitution. District Planning Committees 
(DPCs) had been constituted in only eight districts. Meetings of DPC were not 
held in these eight districts during 2015-16. The appointment of Lokayukta 
had not been made till December 2016. As on March 2016, 243 Utilisation

accepted recommendations of first and second SFCs respectively. An unspent 
grant of ₹ 79.45 crore of Thirteenth Finance Commission (ThFC) was lying

accounts of PRIs as of date.

 5 Relocation and Rehabilitation of slum dwellers in Ahmedabad Municipal 
Corporation Area
With an aim to improve the quality of life of urban poor, Ahmedabad Municipal 
Corporation (AMC) had prepared (2006) City Development Plan (CDP) for the 
period 2006-12. AMC had also prepared (2012) Slum Free City Plan of Action 
(SFCPoA) for the period 2013-22 based on socio-economic survey (2010) of 
all slum households. AMC identified 572 slums under Slum Free City Plan of 
Action (SFCPoA) to rehabilitate and redevelop through Government funding 
and private developers. The performance audit of “Relocation and Rehabilitation 
of slum dwellers in AMC area” was conducted for the period 2011-16 between 
May and September 2016. Following observations came to light –
 • As against the target of 60,000 Dwelling Units (DUs) under CDP , AMC had 
constructed only 20,112 DUs under Basic Service to Urban Poor (BSUP) 
scheme. Infrastructure development works were not taken up in 149 slums 
identified in the CDP .

 6 Service Delivery in Urban Local Bodies
With an objective to measure the performance of each ULB, GoI issued (July 
2008) 28 Service Level Benchmarks (SLBs) for four services. The services 
included water supply, sewage, solid waste and storm water management. 
Recommendations of ThFC also provided that each State Government shall 
evolve methods to benchmark the services provided by ULBs. The SLBs issued 
by GoI were operationalised in the State from March 2011. The performance 
audit on “Service Delivery in ULBs” was conducted for the period 2011-16 
between April and October 2016 which revealed some areas of concern relating 
to services delivered by ULBs as highlighted below –
 • NPs test-checked had not prepared the City Development Plan. The SLB 
Cell constituted in November 2011 had not carried out any activity envisaged 
in the Terms of Reference.
 • SLB notification for water supply services indicated that all the MCs had 
achieved the SLBs in respect of per capita supply of water per day, non-
revenue water, water quality and redressal of complaints. All the NPs have 
achieved the SLBs in respect of water quality and redressal of complaints. 
Only 31 NPs in the State could provide prescribed SLB of 135 lpcd of water. 
Efficiency in usage of water and leakages in the distribution system had not 
been ensured in any of the ULBs. Metered water connections had not been 
implemented in any of the ULBs except Surat and Vadodara MCs. None of 
the ULBs in the State could provide water for 24 hours. The supply of water 
ranged between 0.12 hours and nine hours.
 • Discrepancies between actual performance and the performance indicators 
notified in respect of test-checked MCs and NPs were noticed. The actual 
coverage of water connections in Bhavnagar and Vadodara MCs was only 
79 and 90 per cent as against 100 and 98 per cent notified. MCs and NPs have 
been reporting estimated Non-Revenue Water (NRW) instead of measuring 
the actual NRW. No Water Audit had been conducted to assess the actual 
NRW. Out of 13 test-checked NPs, two NPs supplied water on alternate 
days and four NPs supplied water once in three days. Tests prescribed for 
ensuring quality of water was not being carried out by the test-checked MCs 
and NPs. Actual recovery of cost for supply of water varied as compared to 
the recovery of cost notified.
 • Out of 14 Water Supply projects implemented in test-checked ULBs, 
inordinate delay in execution of work was noticed in four projects. Five 
out of ten completed WS projects had not been completely put to use 
by the ULBs due to irregular/non-supply of water from source, faulty 
design and abandoning the work by the agency, etc. Test-checked ULBs 
had not achieved the prescribed SLBs in respect of water supply despite 
implementation of water supply projects.
 • SLB notification for sewage management indicated that all the MCs and 
NPs had achieved the SLBs in respect of coverage of toilets and redressal 
of complaints. The coverage of sewage network in MCs ranged between

1.1 Introduction
The 73 rd Constitutional amendment gave constitutional status to Panchayati 
Raj Institutions (PRIs). It established a system of uniform structure, regular 
elections and continuous funds flow through Finance Commissions. As a follow 
up, the States are required to entrust these bodies with such powers, functions 
and responsibilities so as to enable them to function as Local Self Government 
Institutions (LSGIs). In particular, the PRIs are required to prepare plans and 
implement schemes for economic development and social justice. It also included 
those enumerated in the Eleventh Schedule of the Constitution.
A three-tier1 system of Panchayats was envisaged in the Gujarat Panchayats Act, 
1961. This Act was amended in April 1993 to incorporate the provisions of the 
73rd Constitutional amendment.
The population growth in Gujarat during the last decade (2001-2011) was 19.30 
per cent and was more than the national average of 17.70 per cent. By the year 
2011, the population of the State was 6.04 crore, of which women comprised 
47.90 per cent . The rural population of the State was 3.47 crore (57.45 per 
cent) and urban population was 2.57 crore (42.55 per cent). The comparative 
demographic and developmental picture of the State is given in Table 1 below –2

1.2 Organisational set-up of the PRIs
Additional Chief Secretary, Panchayats, Rural Housing and Rural Development 
Department (PRHRDD) exercises administrative control over the PRIs. 
The PRHRDD is responsible for framing policies pertaining to formulation 
and implementation of developmental schemes and administration. The 
PRHRDD exercises administrative control through office of the Development 
Commissioner, Gandhinagar. The President and Vice President of the DPs and 
TPs are elected from amongst the elected representatives. The Sarpanch of a 
GP is elected directly by the villagers and the Upa-Sarpanch is elected from 
amongst the elected representatives. The Gujarat Panchayats Act envisages 
the functioning of the DPs, TPs and GPs through Standing Committees having 
elected representatives as members and chairperson. The Presidents in respect of 
DPs and TPs and Sarpanches of GPs are ex-officio Chairpersons of the Standing 
Committees.

1.3 Functioning of PRIs
The 73 rd Amendment to the Constitution envisaged transfer of 29 functions 
listed in the 11 th Schedule of the Constitution to the PRIs. Article 243 G of the 
Constitution had empowered the State Legislature to decide and confer powers 
and responsibilities to the PRIs. As per Section 180 (2) of the Gujarat Panchayats 
Act, the State Government may entrust 29 functions to the PRIs to prepare and 
implement schemes3. State Government has, however, devolved (April 1993) 14 
functions fully and five functions partially to PRIs. Ten functions have not yet 
been devolved. This indicated that the spirit of the Constitutional Amendment for 
the PRIs to function as grassroots level LSGIs has not been fulfilled.
1.4 Formation of various Committees
The number of Committees prescribed under the Gujarat Panchayats Act4 is seven5 
for DPs and two6 for both TPs and GPs respectively. In addition, the Panchayats 
may, with the prior approval of the State Government, constitute Committee(s) 
for specific purposes. 
Article 243 ZD of the Constitution of India envisages constitution of District 
Planning Committee (DPC) at district level in every State. DPC consists of such 
number of elected, nominated and permanent invitee members (not less than 15 
and not more than 30) as determined by the Collector of the district. The Minister 
in-charge of the District is the Chairperson of the DPC. The tenure of DPC is five 
years and it is required to meet at least once in three months. 
DPCs are constitutionally responsible to consolidate the plans prepared by Local 
Self Government Institute (LSGIs) in the District. They are also responsible 
for preparing a Draft Development Plan (DDP) of the District as a whole for 
onward transmission to the Government. The DPC is to monitor the quantitative 
and qualitative progress, especially its physical and financial achievements in 
implementation of the approved DDP. The State Government, while preparing 
the State plan, considers the proposal and priority included in the DDPs prepared 
for each District by the DPC. 
DPCs were constituted in only eight districts out of 23 districts; Meetings of DPC 
were not held in these eight districts7 during 2015-16. State could not provide the 
information of remaining 10 districts. This could have factored the aspirations 
and felt needs of the populace.

1.9 Internal Audit and Internal Control System of PRI
Internal Audit and Internal Control System in an organisation is meant to ensure 
that they have instituted their own internal audit function. It helps to ensure 
effective management of their own operations and sustain quality of their 
administrative as well as financial performance. It also helps to ensure whether 
the operations are carried out according to the applicable laws and regulations 
and in an economical, efficient and effective manner. A built-in Internal Control 
System and strict adherence to Statutes, Codes and Manuals minimise the risk of 
errors and irregularities. It helps to protect resources against loss due to waste, 
abuse and mismanagement.
The State Government had constituted (1982) an Audit Branch in each DP under 
the direct supervision of an Accounts Officer. The Audit Branch helps all branches 
of the DP in preparation and maintenance of the Accounts, Budget and all other 
required financial statements. All bills of DPs and TPs exceeding ₹ 40,000 are 
being pre-audited by the Audit Branch.

2.1.1  Introduction
The Gujarat Land Revenue Code1, 1879 (the Code), came into force to enable the 
Government to administer land use in the State. The State Government framed 
(1972) Gujarat Land Revenue Rules, 1972 (GLR Rules) for enforcement of 
the Code. The Code and Rules enables the Government to monitor activities to 
discourage/indiscriminate conversion of agricultural land for non-agricultural 
purposes. It provided powers to accord necessary permission for conversion of 
land for non-agricultural purposes such as industrial, commercial, residential, 
charity and temporary use.
The Additional Chief Secretary (ACS) is the administrative head of Panchayats, 
Rural Housing and Rural Development Department (PRHRDD). He is 
responsible for overall supervision of the PRIs. He is assisted by the District 
Development Officer (DDO) of District Panchayat at the district level and Taluka 
Development Officer (TDO) of Taluka Panchayat at taluka level. The District 
Collector is responsible in the municipal and notified area of the district. The 
DDO is responsible for implementation of policies and rules in rural area under 
the jurisdiction of District Panchayat and the TDO is responsible for areas under 
Taluka Panchayat and Village Panchayat.
An Audit was conducted to assess whether system for conversion of agricultural 
land to non-agricultural purposes by the Panchayati Raj Institutions was for 
rightful purpose and in accordance with rules and regulations. For this purpose, 
Audit test-checked (May 2016 to October 2016) records of seven 2 out of 33 
District Panchayats (DPs) and four Taluka Panchayats (TPs) under each selected 
DPs ( i.e. 28 TPs) in the State. Audit test-checked the records for the period 
2011-16. Audit also conducted joint field visits of 17 non-agricultural lands with 
officials of test-checked DPs and TPs.

2.1.2.2 Permission granted without approval of the Town Planner
The State Government instructions (March 1982) provided that the applicant 
shall apply directly to the Town Planning Authority for approval of the lay-out 
plan of the proposed land to be converted. The approved lay-out plan was re -

granted without obtaining the lay-out plan approved by the Town Planner. In 
these cases, the applicants had not submitted the approved lay-out plan. The 
TDOs granted the permission without ascertaining the correctness of the plan 
from the Town Planner and granted the permission on the basis of lay-out plan 
prepared by Private Engineer.
With a view to ascertain the correctness of the lay-out plan of the private engineer, 
Audit obtained views of the Town Planner in respect of three cases approved by 
Mundra TP. The Town Planner stated (August 2016) that the permission in these 
three cases could not be granted due to following deficiencies –
•	 Parking space for commercial purpose had not been provided in all the 
cases.
•	 Details of construction work had not been provided by the applicant in the 
lay-out plan in all cases. 
•	 In one case (Samagoga village), the site for construction of commercial 
unit was adjacent to six meter road which is not permissible as per Rule.
This indicated that the TDOs in the above cases had granted permission for 
conversion of agricultural land for non-agricultural purposes without proper 
scrutiny and without following the prescribed procedure leading to undue favour 
to the applicants. 
The TDOs concerned accepted the audit observation and stated (August 2016) 
that due care would be taken in this respect in future.

land conversion granted by TDOs instead of DDOs and Collector concerned.

conversion.

3.1 Introduction
Consequent upon the 74th Constitutional Amendment in 1993, Articles 243 P to 
243 ZG were inserted in the Constitution. These Articles provided constitution 
and composition of municipalities and ward committees, reservation of seats 
for SCs/STs, powers, authority and responsibilities of municipalities, power 
to impose taxes, audit of accounts, elections to the municipalities, constitution 
of district planning committee, etc. This provided that the legislatures could 
endow certain powers and duties to the Urban Local Bodies (ULBs) in order to 
enable them to function as LSGIs. It would also enable the ULBs to carry out 
the responsibilities conferred upon them including those listed in the Twelfth 
Schedule of the Constitution.
As per Census 2011, Gujarat ranks sixth after Maharashtra, Uttar Pradesh, Tamil 
Nadu, West Bengal and Andhra Pradesh in the tally of most urbanised States. The 
urban population of Gujarat State was 2.57 crore, which constituted 42.55 per 
cent of the total population (6.04 crore) of the State and 2.12 per cent of the total 
population (121.06 crore) of India. In Gujarat, there were 200 ULBs i.e. eight 
Municipal Corporations (MCs), 162 Nagarpalikas (NPs) and 30  Notified 
Areas1 (NAs) as of October 2016. Each MC/NP is divided into a number of 
wards. Wards are determined and notified by the State Government considering 
the population, dwelling pattern, geographical condition and economic status of 
the respective area.

3.3 Functioning of ULBs
3.3.1 Powers and functions
ULBs exercise their powers and functions in accordance with provision of 
Section 87 of the Gujarat Municipalities Act, 1963. Section 87 of the Gujarat 
Municipalities Act, 1963 provides for various functions to be exercised in the 
sphere of Public Works, Education, Public Health and Sanitation, Development, 
Town planning and Administration. Under Section 63 to 72 of the Bombay 
Provincial Municipal Corporation Act, 1949, the State Government had devolved 
various functions and powers to Municipal Corporations.
•	 Devolution of Funds, Functions and Functionaries to Urban Local Bodies
Twelfth Schedule (Article-243 W) of the Constitution of India envisaged that the 
State Government may, by law, endow the ULBs with such powers and authority 
as may be necessary to enable them to function as LSGIs. The State Government 
devolved all the 18 functions envisaged in the Twelfth Schedule to the NPs and 
MCs to enable them to function as LSGIs.

3.4 Formation of various Committees
3.4.1 District Planning Committee 
Article 243 ZD of the Constitution of India envisaged constitution of District 
Planning Committee (DPC) at district level in every State. The tenure of DPC 
is five years and it is required to meet at least once in three months. DPCs are 
constitutionally responsible to consolidate the plans prepared by LSGIs in the 
District. Thereafter, prepare a Draft Development Plan (DDP) for the District 
as a whole for onward transmission to the Government. The DPC is to monitor 
the quantitative and qualitative progress, especially its physical and financial 
achievements in the implementation of the approved DDP. The State Government, 
while preparing the State plan, considers the proposal and priority included in the 
DDPs prepared for each District by the DPC.  
DPCs were constituted in only eight districts out of 23 districts; Meetings of DPC 
were not held in these eight districts2 during 2015-16. State could not provide the 
information of remaining 10 districts. This could have factored the aspirations 
and felt needs of the populace.
3.4.2 Formation of committees in Urban Local Bodies
Bombay Provincial Municipal Corporation (BPMC) Act, 1949, provided that 
there shall be two mandatory committees in each MC i.e. Standing committee 
and Transport committee. It also provided that the MC may, from time to time, 
appoint out of its own body, special committees which shall conform to any 
instructions that the MC may from time to time give them. As per the information 
provided to Audit, the above mandatory committees have been formed in all MCs 
and additional committees have been formed based on their requirements.
Gujarat Municipalities Act, 1963 provided that there shall be two mandatory 
committees in each NP i.e. Executive committee and Pilgrim committee. It also 
provided that the NPs may from time to time constitute other committees to 
exercise the powers and perform the duties of the NP. As per the information 
provided to Audit, the above mandatory committees have been formed in all NPs 
and other committees have been formed based on their requirements.

3.13.3 Recommendations of the State Finance Commission (SFC)
Article 243 I of the Constitution made it mandatory for the State Government 
to constitute a State Finance Commission (SFC). The SFC was to be constituted 
within one year from the enactment of 73 rd Constitutional Amendment and 
thereafter on expiry of every five years. The SFC was responsible to review the 
financial condition of the ULBs and to make recommendations to the Governor 
for devolution of funds. GoI guidelines (June 2005) stipulated that the State 
Government was to act within six months of SFC’s recommendations.
It was, however, noticed 8 that the State Government had neither maintained 
periodicity for constitution of SFCs nor placed report (submitted by the belatedly 
constituted SFC) in the Assembly. This defeated the very purpose of the 
constitution of an SFC.
The State Government had accepted 29 out of 64 recommendations (45 per cent) 
made by the First SFC and 12 out of 42 recommendations (29 per cent) made by 
the Second SFC. The department could implement only 17 and eight accepted 
recommendations of First and Second SFCs respectively (December 2016).

Executive Summary
With an aim to improve the quality of life of urban poor, Ahmedabad Municipal 
Corporation (AMC) had prepared (2006) City Development Plan (CDP) for the

all slum households. AMC identified 572 slums under SFCPoA to rehabilitate 
and redevelop through Government funding and private developers. The 
performance audit of “Relocation and Rehabilitation of slum dwellers in AMC 
area” was conducted for the period 2011-16 between May and September 2016. 
Following observations came to light –
•	 AMC had constructed only 20,112 Dwelling Units (DUs) against the

infrastructure development in 149 slums.

•	 Under SFCPoA, only 572 out of 691 slums were planned for redevelopment. 
AMC could achieve the target of only 10,151 beneficiaries (32 per 
cent) as of March 2016 against the target of 31,578 slum dwellers to be

(BSUP) scheme.

construction (2012) due to the dilapidated conditions of the buildings.

4.1.9 Implementation of Regulations for slum rehabilitation through private 
developers
4.1.9.1 Undue favour to the developers by providing excess TDR and relaxation 
in execution of works under RRRS-2010
The State Government constituted (June 2009) a committee for formulation of a 
policy for rehabilitation of slums. Accordingly, the State Government introduced 
(March 2010) “The Regulations for the Rehabilitation and Redevelopment of the 
Slums – 2010” (RRRS-2010). Paragraph 14.3 of RRRS-2010 provided allotment 
of free of cost Dwelling Unit (DU) with basic infrastructure to all eligible slum 
dwellers by the developer. As per Paragraph 12.6.2, the developer would get 
additional Floor Space Index 28 (FSI) for construction of extra DUs/commercial 
units on vacant slum land for sale. UD&UHD amended (May 2012) RRRS-2010 
wherein developers had been provided the option of Transferrable Development 
Right (TDR) for additional FSI29 which could not be utilised on the vacant slum 
plot. The TDR provided could be utilised on any other plot or could be sold to any 
other developer/builder on the basis of proportionate jantri rates 30 of respective 
plots. In case of TDR, the financial benefits to the developers varied, as the jantri 
rates varied among the slum plots selected for in-situ rehabilitation i.e. at the rate 
of ₹ 7,000 to ₹ 36,500 per square metre (sq. mtr.).To rectify this discrepancy in 
RRRS-2010, the State Government introduced (July 2013) a new policy “In-
situ Rehabilitation of Slums 2013 (ISRS-2013)”. Comparison of important 
provisions of RRRS-2010 and ISRS-2013 are shown in Appendix-II. Paragraph 
8.7 of ISRS-2013 (as clarified in Point 8 of General Information in Tender 
Document) provided TDR equal to built-up area used for slum rehabilitation 
multiplied by the ratio of project cost per sq. mtr. and jantri rates. The State 
Government further issued (July 2013) a new planning regulation “Planning 
Regulation for Rehabilitation of slums on Public Land (PRRSPL)”. Paragraph 
7 of the new regulation provided that the decisions taken under RRRS-2010 
stood repealed and TDR would be calculated as per provision in ISRS-2013. In 
case any discrepancy arising on account of applicability of these regulations, the 
decision of the Government should be final. Audit, however, observed that AMC 
followed the provisions of RRRS-2010 and had incorrectly calculated the TDR 
to be provided to the developer. This led to undue benefits to the developers as 
discussed in the succeeding paragraphs. 
•	 Transferrable Development Right

under RRRS-2010. Out of 11 projects, AMC issued (June 2013 to July 2015)

4.2.1 Introduction
The urban population of Gujarat State was 2.57 crore as per census 2011, which 
constituted 42.55 per cent of the total population (6.04 crore) of the State. This 
constituted 2.12 per cent of the total population of India (121.06 crore). The Urban 
Local Bodies (ULBs) in the State consisted of  eight Municipal Corporations 
(MCs), 162 Nagarpalikas (NPs) and 31 Notified Areas. 

65
Chapter-IV : Performance and Compliance Audits of Urban Local Bodies
The 74th Constitutional amendment in 1993 endowed responsibilities 45 listed in 
the Twelfth Schedule to the ULBs to function as institutions of self-Government. 
Government of India (GoI) made huge investments over the years through 
Centrally Sponsored Schemes and by devolution of direct funds as per the 
recommendations of various Finance Commissions. 
Benchmarking is well recognized as an important mechanism for introducing 
accountability in service delivery. It involves measuring and monitoring of service 
provider’s performance on a systematic and continuous basis. Recognizing 
its importance, Ministry of Urban Development (MoUD), GoI had launched 
the Service Level Benchmarking (SLB) initiative in July 2008 covering four 
services namely water supply, sanitation, solid waste management and storm 
water drainage (Appendix-VII). Thirteenth Finance Commission (ThFC) in 
its recommendation for the period of 2010-15 mandated that State Government 
should put in place standards for delivery of essential services by Local Bodies 
among the fulfilment of other conditions linked to the access of the performance 
grants. 
4.2.2 Organisational set-up
The Additional Chief Secretary (ACS), Urban Development and Urban Housing 
Department (UD&UHD) is the administrative head of the department. Director 
of Municipalities (DoM) exercises administrative supervision of the NPs. The 
Gujarat Municipal Finance Board (GMFB) is responsible for distributing funds 
to MCs and NPs. 
Gujarat Urban Development Mission (GUDM) is the State Level Nodal 
Agency (SLNA) for implementation of Centrally Sponsored Schemes 
(CSS) and State Sponsored Schemes (SSS). GUDM accorded administrative 
approval and technical sanctions for projects submitted by the ULBs. 
Water Supply Schemes (WSSs) are implemented either by Gujarat Urban 
Development Company (GUDC), Gujarat Water Supply and Sewerage Board 
(GWSSB) or the concerned ULBs. Underground Drainage Schemes (UDSs) 
are implemented by GUDC or GWSSB and Solid Waste Management (SWM) 
are implemented by GUDC.
4.2.3 Audit Objectives
The objectives of the Performance Audit were to ascertain whether – 
•	 proper planning was done to put in place standards of service delivery of all 
essential services to be provided by ULBs;
•	 ULBs were able to provide the basic urban services; and 
•	 an effective mechanism was in place to make the service level benchmarking 
operational as well as monitor and evaluate performance on provision of 
basic urban services.

4.2.6.2 Notification of Service Level Benchmarks
The Thirteenth Finance Commission (ThFC) grants are divided into two 
components – General Basic Grant (GBG) and General Performance Grant 
(GPG). GPG can be accessed only on complying with the conditions stipulated 
in the ThFC Report. One of the conditions of ThFC envisaged that the State 
Government shall notify targets and achievement for SLBs each year for each 
ULB. 
Hand Book on Service Delivery issued by MoUD prescribed methods of 
measurement and calculation of benchmarks. The reliability of these benchmarks 
are fixed on the basis of methods adopted in measuring the benchmarks. It also 
envisaged that the State Governments and their nodal agencies need to evaluate 
periodically the SLBs. The results of evaluation shall be considered as an 
input for decision making relating to policy, allocation of resource, incentives

SLB framework in the State.

maintaining any records regarding methods adopted for assessing the achievement 
and fixing of targets for SLBs. No system existed in respect of regular collection 
of data from ULBs by GUDM.

States. The project is being funded by Bill and Melinda Gates Foundation. Based 
on the information collected from ULBs for their project, CEPT developed a 
website with online tools/modules for fixing targets and calculating achievement. 
The said database of the CEPT was being utilised by the State Government for 
notifying the achievement of SLBs.

4.2.11 Performance against Service Level Benchmarks for Solid Waste 
Management
To streamline the process of handling, collection, transportation and disposal of 
Municipal Solid Waste (MSW) and to avoid any adverse impact on human health, 
GoI framed MSW (Management and Handling) Rules, 2000. GoI prescribed eight 
benchmarks to measure the performance of solid waste management services by 
ULBs –
•	 Household level coverage of solid waste management services – it 
denotes the extent of households and establishments covered by door-to-
door collection of Municipal Solid Wastes (MSW) on daily basis;
•	 Efficiency	in	collection	of	municipal	solid	waste – it denotes the extent 
of total MSW collected by the ULB as against the total MSW generated 
within the ULB area;
•	 Extent of segregation of municipal solid waste – it denotes the extent of 
MSW being segregated. Segregation of wet and dry wastes should be done 
at the point of generation;
•	 Extent of municipal solid waste recovered  – it denotes the quantum of 
collected MSW having been recycled or processed;
•	 Extent	of	scientific	disposal	of	municipal	solid	waste	– it denotes the total 
quantity of wastes disposed in Sanitary Landfill Facility (SLF) as against 
total waste disposal in all sites;
•	 Efficiency	in	redressal	of	consumer	complaints	–	It denotes the number of 
complaints redressed within 24 hours as against total number of complaints 
received;
•	 Extent in cost recovery in Solid Waste Management (SWM) services - 
It is the extent to which the ULB is able to recover all operating expenses 
relating to SWM services; and
•	 Efficiency	in	collection	of	SWM	charges	– It is the revenue collected in 
the current year against the total demand of operating revenues.

4.2.13 Conclusion
With an objective to measure the performance of each ULB, GoI issued (July 
2008) 28 Service Level Benchmarks (SLBs) for four services. The services 
included water supply, sewage, solid waste and storm water management. 
Recommendations of Thirteenth Finance Commission (ThFC) also provided that 
each State Government shall evolve methods to benchmark the services provided 
by ULBs. The SLBs issued by GoI were operationalised in the State from March 
2011. 
A Performance Audit was conducted to assess whether the SLB framework was 
implemented in the State as envisaged in GoI guidelines/instructions. Various 
deficiencies in planning, achievement and monitoring the SLBs were noticed.
ULBs had not prepared City Development Plan though was envisaged in the hand 
book on SLBs. The SLB Cell constituted in November 2011 had met only once 
till December 2016 and had not carried out any activity envisaged in the Terms 
of Reference. The achievement and targets of SLBs were being notified without 
proper measurement and without analysing the actual facts at the ULB level. 
Data analysis of SLBs notified for water supply revealed that only 18 NPs in 
the State could provide WS connections to 100 per cent households. Supply of 
prescribed 135 lpcd of water was achieved only by 31 NPs and Junagadh MC 
could supply only 50 lpcd of water. The ULBs in the State had not ensured the 
efficiency in usage of water and leakages in the distribution system as water 
connections were not metered. 
The achievement of SLBs notified varied as against the actual as per records. The 
variation was noticed in respect of WS connections, quantum of water supplied, 
continuity of water supply, quality and cost recovery. Water Audit was not being 
done by the test-checked ULBs to assess the actual Non-Revenue Water. In six test-
checked NPs, water was not being supplied on daily basis i.e. on alternate days (two 
NPs) and on third day (four NPs). Samples of water were not being taken for testing 
the quality in five NPs. In remaining ten test-checked ULBs, monthly samples 
from each prescribed points were not being taken on regular basis. SLBs had not

making adequate provisions related to public works, education, public health 
and sanitation, and other development works. They are also responsible for 
management of assets which included planning for creation/acquisition of 
assets, accounting, utilisation, maintenance and disposal of obsolete assets. 
The Additional Chief Secretary (ACS), Urban Development and Urban Housing 
Department (UD&UHD) is the administrative head of the department. Director 
of Municipalities (DoM) exercises administrative supervision of the NPs. The 
Gujarat Municipal Finance Board (GMFB) is responsible for distributing funds to 
the NPs. The affairs of the NPs are governed by a body comprising of councilors 
elected by the people. Chief Officer appointed by the State Government is 
responsible for supervising the financial and executive administration of the NPs. 
As per census 2011, there are 162 NPs in the State. The NPs are classified into 
four categories65 on the basis of population.
An Audit was conducted to assess management of assets by the NPs which 
included creation of assets, execution of works, accounting, utilisation and 
maintenance of assets. Audit focused on 11 categories of assets 66 in the NPs for 
providing basic civic services to the residents. Audit test-checked (May 2016 
to October 2016) the records of UD&UHD, GMFB and 15 out of 162 NPs 67 
covering the period 2013-16. The NPs were selected from each region of the 
State by adopting Simple Random Sampling without Replacement Method. Joint 
field visits68 were also conducted in test-checked NPs to ascertain the status and

rent of each shop and auction the shops by giving wide publicity in the local

4.3.7 Disposal of assets
Disposal of asset is the third and last phase in the supply management cycle 
where the first two phases consist of procurement, and utilisation and 
maintenance. Disposal occurs when a piece of equipment or property can no 
longer provide efficient service or though still working, has been rendered useless 
due to obsolescence. Disposal proceedings should be immediately initiated to 
avoid further deterioration of the property and consequent depreciation in its 
value. A systematic and timely disposal would yield benefits in terms of, among 
others, a higher appraised value and by freeing storage areas for other purposes. 
Unserviceable/obsolete vehicles, equipment, machineries, dead stock, etc. are 
required to be disposed of by way of auctioning after fixing a minimum upset 
price by the competent authority.
Audit observed in 12 out of 15 test-checked NPs that there were 89 vehicles 83 
which were treated as unserviceable/obsolete and were lying idle for period 
ranging from one to 12 years. These vehicles had not been auctioned by the NPs 
for want of fixing a minimum upset price or holding of auction. Retention of 
these vehicles for a longer period would further deteriorate the condition of the 
vehicles and would reduce appraisable value.
During joint field visit, it was also observed in all test-checked NPs that huge 
volume of pipes, vehicle tyres, machineries, etc. which were not usable were 
lying un-disposed. None of the NPs had initiated any action to dispose of the 
same. 
The Chief Officers of NPs concerned stated (May to October 2016) that survey 
would be conducted to identify such assets and action for their disposal would be 
taken immediately.

4.4 Collection of Professional Tax by selected Nagarpalikas
4.4.1 Introduction
The State Government enacted (March 1976) The Gujarat (Panchayats, 
Municipalities, Municipal Corporations and State) Tax on Professions, Trades, 
Callings and Employments Act, 1976 (The Act). For enforcement of the Act, 
the State Government framed (April 1976) Gujarat State Tax on Profession, 
Trades, Callings and Employments Rules, 1976 (The Rules). The Act provided 
that every person engaged in any Profession, Trade, Calling or Employment and 
falling under one or the other classes mentioned in Schedule I of the Act shall be 
liable to pay the Professional Tax (PT). The PT shall be paid to the designated 
authority at such rate fixed by the State Government from time to time. Up 
to September 2006, PT was levied and collected by the State Government. 
Thereafter, the functions had been transferred to the Urban Local Bodies (ULBs) 
and Panchayati Raj Institutes (PRIs) except for class of persons specified under 
1A84 of the schedule. As per instructions issued by the State Government, the PT 
collected in respect of class of persons specified under 1B to 10 of the schedule 
shall be treated as own revenue of the PRIs and ULBs concerned. PT collected 
from class of persons specified under 1A of the schedule shall be credited into 
State Government account. Professional tax is a major source of revenue for the 
Nagarpalikas (NPs).

person liable to pay tax (other than a person earning salary or wages) shall obtain
