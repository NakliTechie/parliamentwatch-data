PREFACE 
This Report  of Comptroller and Auditor General of India  for the period ended 
March 2024 has been prepared for submission to Lieutenant Governor of Jammu and 
Kashmir under Section 72 of  the Jammu and Kashmir Reorganis ation Act, 2019 for 
laying in the Legislative Assembly. 
This Report contains two Chapters: Chapter-I of the Report presents significant audit 
findings noticed during Performance Audit on ‘Jal Jeevan Mission’ and Chapter-II 
presents significant audit findings noticed during Subject Specific Compliance  Audit 
on ‘Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Scheme’. 
The instances mentioned in this Report are those which came to the notice in the 
course of test audit of the period 2023-24 as well as those which came to notice in 
earlier years but could not be reported in the previous Audit Reports. Instances 
relating to the period subsequent to 202 3-24 have also been included, wherever 
necessary.  
The audit has been conducted in conformity with the Auditing Standards issued by the 
Comptroller and Auditor General of India.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY 
 
About the Report  
This Report covers matters arising out of the Performance Audit o n ‘Jal Jeevan 
Mission’ and Subject Specific Compliance Audit o n ‘Mahatma Gandhi  National Rural 
Employment Guarantee Scheme’. The primary purpose of this Report is to bring the 
important results of audit to the notice of the Legislature. The Report has been 
organised in two chapters as under:  
Chapter-I: Performance Audit on Jal Jeevan Mission  
Jal Jeevan Mission (JJM) is the flagship Scheme launched on 15 August 2019 with an 
aim to provide Functional Household Tap Connection (FHTC) to every rural 
household by December 2024  (extended to 2028).  Performance Audit of Jal Jeevan 
Mission (JJM)  covering the period from 2019-20 to 2023 -24 revealed that the 
institutional mechanism for successful implementation of programme  was not fully 
operational and effective. There were shortfalls in holding prescribed number of 
meetings of District Water Sanitation Mission, lack of bottom-up approach in 
preparation of Action Plans and non-preparation of District Annual Action Plans.  
There was shortfall ranging from 06 per cent to 93 per cent in achievement of targets 
for providing the FHTCs fixed under State Action Plans . As of   
31 March 2024, 14,43,515 (77.15 per cent) households were reported to have been 
provided with FHTCs . Despite sizeable allocations of funds, persistent 
underutilisation of funds during the initial years was observed. A ccumulation of idle 
balances, delayed execution of Schemes and delayed access of potable water to rural 
households were also seen . Due to d elay in utilisation of funds and inability to meet 
release conditions, the Government of Jammu & Kashmir could not receive the 
full allocation of Central Assistance. Only 44 per cent Schemes (1,440 out of 3 ,253 
total schemes)  taken up during 2019 -20 to 2023 -24 under the programme were 
completed as of December 2024. 
The release of funds for Support Activities ranged from 0.17 per cent to 3.30 per cent, 
falling short of the prescribed ceiling of five per cent.  While under WQM&S 
activities, it ranged from 0.08 per cent  to 1.07 per cent , against the provision of  
two per cent. Non-adherence to prescribed funding allocations and poor utilization 
under Support and WQM&S components indicated inadequate emphasis after  
2021-22 on critical activities like water quality surveillance, community awareness, 
and institutional capacity building.  
Out of 853 Schemes taken up under JJM in the sampled four districts (Kulgam, 
Anantnag, Kishtwar and Doda), only 429 Schemes had been physically completed as 
of November 2024. However, no O&M plan was prepared by the department for the 
upkeep of assets created under these Schemes, nor was any mechanism instituted to

ensure availability of trained manpower at the village level, as required under the 
guidelines. 
In the absence of a planned framework for maintenance and local capacity building, 
the sustainability of the completed Schemes remains at risk. The Department had also 
not initiated any action to assess the district -wise requirement of skilled manpower, 
nor had it engaged with P radhan Mantri Kaushal Vikas Yojana (PMKVY)  for

There was absence of a planned framework for maintenance , local capacity building

respectively, the prevalence of waterborne diseases was high with increasing trend 
over last five years. Although 63 per cent  JJM Schemes had been completed in 
Anantnag District, there is no significant decrease in the waterborne disease over the 
last five years. 
Recommendations 
The Government may: 
• Ensure bottom-up approach in preparation of various plans as per provisions 
of JJM operational guidelines.  
• Prioritise the completion of all the incomplete Schemes in a time -bound 
manner and devise a framework for allotment of works and procurement of 
material in a time-bound manner, and 
• Strengthen the water quality monitoring system and social audit mechanism.

Vacancies of Programme Officers, Accounts Assistants, Technical Assistants and

weaknesses in financial management, particularly in timely settlement of material 
procurement liabilities and wages to poor workers . Besides, there was absence of 
proper recording of events as per the guidelines. 
Out of a total 37,82,594 job card households ( HHs) who demanded job during

providing guaranteed employment. However, only 77,512 (two per cent) households 
were provided the minimum of 100 days of employment during 2019-24. 
The Scheme implementation suffered from lacunae in maintenance of basic records 
such as measurement books and muster rolls, thereby undermining transparency. 
Out of 27.14 lakh planned works to be taken up for execution during 2019 -20 to  
2023-24 in J&K,  only 20.16 lakh works (74 per cent) were taken up for execution. 
However, only 5.34 lakh (26 per cent ) of the works taken up for execution were 
completed during 2019-20 to 2023 -24. A significant number of 1 4.82 lakh works 
remained incomplete constituting about 74 per cent  of the works taken up for 
execution. The Department incurred an expenditure of ₹ 1,897.10 crore as of  
March 2024 on these incomplete works. Reasons for not completing 74 per cent of 
works were not forthcoming from records. 
The institutional arrangements/mechanism for planning and managing the 
convergence works with other programmes were not in place either at the District 
level or at the Block level in sampled districts.

Audit observed that 144 Anganwadi Centres were taken up for execution in four

payments.

JJM is based on a community approach  to water and includes extensive Information, 
Education and Communication as a key component of the mission.

1.7.1  State Water and Sanitation Mission at State Level 
Para 5.2 of the Operational Guidelines provided that SWSM headed by Chief Secretary 
should co-ordinate, converge and provide policy guidance at State level as a registered 
society. The SWSM would have two committees (i) Apex Committee and (ii) Executive 
Committee which were envisaged to have periodic meetings so as to ensure approval 
of State Action Plans, management of water quality and service standards, provide 
policy guidance, ensure financial discipline etc. There is also a State Level  Scheme 
Sanctioning Committee (SLSSC) for approval of Schemes. The functions, composition, 
number of meetings required and conducted by Apex and Executive committees and 
SLSSC during 2020-24 are given in Appendix-1.4. 
Audit observed that during the period 2020-24, though eight Apex Committee meetings 
were conducted as per guidelines, however, the Executive Committee which was 
entrusted with functions like review of physical and financial progress, monitoring of 
implementation, coordination with district -level agencies etc. held only one meeting 
against the required 48 meetings (as mentioned in the order for constitution) during the 
same period rendering the oversight process questionable.  
In reply, Department stated (August 2025) that 17 review meetings were also conducted 
under the chairmanship of Chief Secretary to review physical and financial progress. 
Further, minimum number of meetings to be conducted by the Executive Committee 
were not mentioned in Scheme Guidelines. Howev er, apart from one meeting of 
Executive Committee mentioned in Draft Report, 32 and 76 meetings  have been held 
under the chairmanship of Administrative Secretary and Mission Director respectively 
with the technical experts. It was added ( October 2025) that 108 meetings were held 
with Chief Engineers and technical experts under chairmanship of Mission Directorate.  
The reply could be seen in the light of the fact that the composition of Executive 
Committee comprised of stakeholders from various departments, while the meetings 
mentioned in the reply indicated discussions with specific personnel/experts only. In 
absence of all the stakeholders in a common forum deficiency in informed decision 
making cannot be ruled out.   
1.7.2  District Water and Sanitation Mission at District level 
Para 5.3 of the JJM guidelines envisages that at the district level, DWSM headed by the 
Deputy Commissioner is responsible for overall implementation of JJM. The functions, 
composition, and number of meetings required and conducted by DWSM during  
2020-24 in respect of four sampled districts are given in Appendix 1.5. 
Audit found that against the requisite 48 meetings of DWSM in each of four Districts, 
there was a shortfall of 41 (85 per cent) and 47 (98 per cent) meetings in Doda and 
Kishtwar Districts respectively. Further, no meeting was held in Anantnag and Kulgam 
Districts. The failure to conduct regular DWSM meetings undermined the institutional 
mechanism envisaged under JJM for effective planning, monitoring, and coordination 
at the district level.

1.8.1.2  District Action Plan 
As per Annexure X-B of JJM guidelines , DWSM is responsible for preparation and 
finalization of District Action Plan (DAP). The plan would be based on the aggregation 
of all Village Action Plans prepared in the district. The plan, apart from giving the road 
map to achieve the Functional Household Tap Connections by 2024, would also include 
the long-term drinking water security of the district. The DAP comprising of District 
water security, human resources required, requirement of Implementation Support 
Agencies (ISAs), Information, Education and Communication  (IEC) activities, 
retrofitting piped water supply Schemes, new Schemes, O&M at district level etc. 
Audit observed that the DAPs were not prepared in the prescribed format in any of the 
sampled districts. In four 9 sampled Districts, instead of a comprehensive planning 
document, only components related to retrofitting of piped water supply Schemes and 
new Schemes were considered in the form of Detailed Project Reports (DPRs). Critical 
elements such as district water  security planning, assessment of human resources, 
engagement of ISAs, and IEC activities as mandated in the prescribed DAP format were 
not incorporated. 
On being pointed out, the Department stated  (August 2025) that DAPs were 
strategically designed to ensure sustainable and comprehensive water access across all 
Districts and these DAPs were based on VAPs prepared and endorsed by VWSCs/ Pani 
Samitis. It was further added that each DAP includes 5 per cent  community 
mobilisation and 2 per cent water quality funds. However, supporting documents to 
substantiate the Departmental response were not shared with audit. 
1.8.1.3  State Action Plan 
Para 7.5 of the JJM guidelines envisages that every State has to prepare a five-year State 
Action Plan wherein annual targets of FHTCs and corresponding financial requirements 
will be projected.  
Further, Para 3.6.1 of operational guidelines  envisaged that State Action Plan (SAP) 
needs to be prepared with an objective of achieving overall State drinking water security

1.9.2  Central Assistance (CA)

by GoI to States/ UTs. The amount towards the first installment will be 50 per cent of

to the State/UT in the financial year and released in two tranches. The last date of

1.10.2  Planning for Operation and Maintenance and Skilled Human Resources 
in the Created Assets 
As per Chapter 9 of the Operational Guidelines  for implementation of the Jal Jeevan 
Mission (JJM), besides development of water supply infrastructure, there is a critical 
need to raise awareness about judicious use of water, and to build capacities of Gram 
Panchayats (GPs) and their sub -committees Village Water & Sanitation Committees 
(VWSCs), Pani Samitis, and user groups for effective planning, implementation, 
operation, and maintenance (O&M) of in-village infrastructure.  
Further, Para 9.4 of the guidelines stipulates  that to ensure long-term sustainability of 
Functional Household Tap Connections (FHTCs) to every rural household, skilled 
manpower such as masons, plumbers, fitters, and electricians will be required. It also 
mandates convergence with Pradhan Mantri Kaush al Vikas Yojana (PMKVY) for 
identifying district -wise requirements and training such human resources through 
certified training programmes in vernacular languages. 
Audit scrutiny revealed that  out of 853 Schemes taken up under JJM in the sampled 
four districts, only 42933 Schemes had been physically completed as of November 2024. 
However, no O&M plan was prepared by the department for the upkeep of assets 
created under these Schemes, nor was any mechanism instituted to ensure availability 
of trained manpower at the village level, as required under the guidelines. 
In the absence of a planned framework for maintenance and local capacity building, the 
sustainability of the completed Schemes remains at risk. The Department had also not 
initiated any action to assess the district-wise requirement of skilled manpower, nor had 
it engaged with PMKVY for training, certification, and deployment of local youth for 
O&M activities. 
While accepting the audit contention, the Department stated  (August 2025) that a 
comprehensive O&M policy was under finalisation. It was also stated that the 
Department was taking all feasible steps to ensure long term sustainability of the created 
assets.

2.1  Introduction 
The Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Scheme (MGNREGS) is 
a Centrally Sponsored Scheme implemented on a cost sharing basis 1 between GoI 
(90 per cent) and the State/ Union Territory (UT) Government (10 per cent) wherein at 
least 100 days of guaranteed wage employment, in a financial year, to every household 
whose adult members volunteer to do unskilled manual work is provided. The scheme 
aims to create durable assets to ensure a source of livelihood for the rura l population 
and to strengthen Panchayati Raj establishments across India.  
The UT Government also bears the total expenditure of delayed payment of the wages 
to the workers, unemployment allowance and administrative expenses  of the State 
Employment Guarantee Council (SEGC). The Scheme was implemented in all the 
20 districts of Union Territory of Jammu and Kashmir (J&K) during 2019 -20 to 
2023-24. 
India, as a member of United Nations, committed (September 2015) to the Sustainable 
Development Goals (SDG) -1 and 2 which seeks  to end poverty in all its forms 
everywhere (Goal 1) and to end hunger, achieve food security and improved nutrition 
and promote sustainable agriculture  (Goal 2). An overview of MGNREGS process is 
given in Chart 2.1.

2.3.1  Funding Pattern 
Central Share (CS) of funds under MGNREGA is normally released in two tranches. 
The release of 1 st tranche (including upfront money) of CS is based on proportionate 
fund requirement as per the agreed to LB to take care of requirement for the first six 
months of the financial year, subject to a maximum of 50 per cent of the total fund 
required for a whole year. The release of 2 nd tranche is based on unspent balances and 
actual performance against the agreed to LB during the year. 
The MGNREGS in the UT of J&K is having 100 per cent funding from the GoI from 
the year 2019 -20 onwards 20. The GoI allocates wages for unskilled/skilled and  
semi-skilled labour, material costs and administrative expenses to the GoJ&K in two 
tranches. GoJ&K releases these funds  to the State Employment Guarantee Fund 
(SEGF). The funds (wages) are then transferred by SEGF to the accounts of unskilled, 
skilled, semi-skilled workers and to the vendors/suppliers on account of payments for 
material and other administrative expenses. In May 2013, the Government constituted 
the State Employment Guarantee Authority (SEGA), J&K which is responsible for 
management and administration of SEGF. SEGA was registered (May 2013) as a 
society under J&K Registration of Societies Act, 1998.

2.4.3  Framework for Convergence between MGNREGA and Other 
Programmes/ Schemes 
One of the objectives of MGNREGA is the creation of durable assets and securing 
livelihood of rural households. This can be facilitated through convergence of 
MGNREGA works with resources of other Programmes/ Schemes available with 
Panchayats and other line departments. These resources are not limited to availability 
of funds but include technical expertise and know-how of the line departments. Further, 
Paragraph 15.3.1.3 (a) of the MGNREGA Operational Guidelines provide s that 
planning for works and managing the convergence would require institutional 
arrangements for proper coordination at District, Block and Village level. The 
institutional platform for such dovetailing will be the Gram Sabha at the Village level 
and then the Gram Panchayat. 
Audit, however, observed the following shortcomings during 2019-20 to 2023-24. The 
institutional arrangements/mechanism for planning and managing the convergence 
works with other programmes  were not in place either at the District level or at the 
Block level in sampled districts. Although, the Department of Rural 
Development (MGNREGS), GoI issued (November 2013) 41 circular regarding 
constitution of a convergence team consisting of senior officials of Pradhan Mantri 
Gram Sadak Yojna (PMGSY) and MGNREGS to operationalise convergence 
guidelines, no such convergence team had been constituted as of August 2025. There 
was nothing on record to indicate that the Department had achieved any 
inter-departmental convergence in sectors such as horticulture, fisheries, PMGSY, 
roads, agriculture, sericulture etc.  
According to the Department of Rural Development (MGNRES), GoI circular 
(November 2013), there are clear provisions for ensuring that roads constructed under 
PMGSY should be included in post -construction activities such as maintenance,  
road-side plantations and water harvesting structures under MGNREGA, for placing in 
ensuing Gram Sabha for approval and inclusion in shelf of projects.

2.4.3.1  Construction of Anganwadi Centres (AWCs) under MGNREGA in 
convergence with ICDS  
Ministry of Rural Development, Government of India (MGNREGA Division) 
Guidelines (February 2016) provide for construction of Anganwadi Centres  (AWCs) 
under MGNREGA in convergence with ICDS Scheme of Ministry of Women and Child 
Development. The guidelines aim at building a systematic convergence of ICDS, 
MGNREGS and resources of Panchayati Raj Institutions, especially 14 th Finance 
Commission grants so that every AWC is provided with own building.  
Under MGNREGA, expenditure up to ₹ 5 lakh will be allowed for construction of an 
AWC building. Beyond this, the expenditure of ₹ 2 lakh will be borne from the ICDS 
Scheme of the Ministry of Women and Child Development. Progress of construction 
of AWCs unde r MGNREGS shall be the responsibility of the District Programme  
Coordinator (DPC) and the agency implementing ICDS Scheme as mentioned in the 
respective guidelines. The completion period of each AWC building shall not be more 
than 11 months from the date of commencement of the work.

2.5.2 Monitoring Mechanism 
The MGNREGA Act, 2005 and MGNREGA Operational Guidelines, 2013 prescribe  
monitoring at National level, State level and District level through Vigilance and 
Monitoring Committees, District  Quality Monitors, State Quality Monitors, National 
level Monitors, State Employment Guarantee Council, and Social Audit by Social Audit 
Unit (SAU).  
2.5.2.1  State Employment Guarantee Council 
Section 12 of ‘Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act 2005’ 
envisages setting up of a State Employment Guarantee Council (SEGC) for regular 
monitoring and review of the implementation of the Act at the State/ UT level. 
Accordingly, the Department constituted (March 2006) “State Employment Guarantee 
Council” (SEGC) to advise the  UT Government on all matters relating the Scheme, 
including reviewing the monitoring and redressal mechanism, giving recommendations 
for improvement as well preparing the  annual report to be laid before the State/ UT 
Legislature. 
Audit observed that SEGC was required to meet at least twice a year  for regular 
monitoring and review of the implementation of the Scheme at the UT level. Against 
the requirement of 10 meetings held during 2019-24, the SEGC held only one meeting 
on 02 September 2022 during 2019-24.  Even the decision taken in the meeting held on 
02 September 2022,  regarding preparation of Annual Reports for placing before the 
Administrative Council, had not been fully implemented as of March 2024. The SEGC 
had fixed target for construction of 13.73 lakh soakage/ compost pits in registered HHs. 
However, these targets were not achieved fully as of March 2024,  as only 4.65 lakh  
(34 per cent) soakage/compost pits in individual HHs were taken -up for execution. Of 
these, only 2.31 lakh (50 per cent ) soakage/compost pits were completed as of  
March 2024. Reasons for not completing 50 per cent soakage/ compost pits were not on 
record. Thus, the envisaged monitoring and steering role of SEGC at the highest level

was not effectively discharged, thereby  weakening accountability  and oversight 
envisaged under MGNREGA. 
In reply, the Department while accepting the audit observations stated ( January 2025

the coming session. Further, the Department stated that they have actively worked 
towards the completion of SBM soakage and compost pits in accordance with the

2.5.3  Social Audit 
MGNREGA has institutionalised ‘Social Audit’ as a means of continuous public 
vigilance. The basic objective of social audit is to ensure public accountability in the 
implementation of projects, laws and policies. Section 17  of the Mahatma Gandhi 
National Rural Employment Guarantee Act, 2005 (MGNREG Act, 2005) states that the 
Gram Sabha would monitor the execution of works within the Gram  Panchayat and 
conduct regular social audits of all the projects under the Scheme taken up within the 
Gram Panchayat. 
2.5.3.1  Social Audit Setup 
The J&K Society for Social Audit Accountability and Transparency (JKSSAAT) conducts  
Social Audits of MGNREGS in J&K and was registered in 2018. The Director of Social 
Audit Unit (SAU) was appointed in January 2021. 
Audit examination disclosed that the Governing Body of the SAU was constituted in 
the year 2024, after a lapse of five years from registration of SAU as a Society in 2018.  
Auditing Standards 2120 (v) (a) issued by the Ministry of RDD,  GoI provides for 
designating a Grievance Redressal Officer for accepting complaints from citizens about 
the staff and practices of the SAU and the Grievance Redressal Officer shall report to 
the Governing Body. Paragraph 13.1.1 (ii) of  MGNREGA Operational Guidelines 2013 
stipulates that State/ UT Government shall facilitate conduct of social audit of works 
taken up under the  Act in every Gram Panchayat at least once in six months .  
However, contrary to these provisions, the SAU prepared an Annual Social Audit Plan, 
instead of the mandated six-monthly plan.  
Paragraph 13.4.3 of MGNREGA Operational Guidelines, 2013 provides that SAU,  an 
independent organisation, would facilitate conduct of Social Audit by Gram Sabhas and 
identify, train and deploy suitable Resource Persons (RP) at Village, Block, District and

2.5.5.1  Disposal of Complaints  
Section 23(6) of the MGNREG Act provides that the Programme Officer (PO)  shall 
enter every complaint in a complaint register maintained by him and shall dispose of 
the dispute and complaints within seven days of their receipt and in case complaints 
relate to matters to be resolved by any other authority shall be  forwarded to such 
authority under intimation to the complainant. Further, as required under  
Paragraph 10.3.9 of Operational Guidelines, 2013, complaint register should be 
maintained at GP, Block and District levels for monitoring the status of complaints.  
It was, however, noted that in five sampled districts  and in 40 sampled GPs of 
10 sampled blocks, no complaint register was maintained during 2019-20 to 2023-24.  
Concerned ACDs stated that complaints are registered on NREGASoft and are 
monitored there. Further, complaints are also submitted verbally/in writing to POs and 
district officials and are disposed accordingly.  
However, the maintenance of complaints on an online platform or informal receipt of 
complaints does not dispense with the requirement of maintaining the prescribed 
complaint registers, as mandated under Section 23(6) of the Act and Paragraph 10.3.9 
of the Operational Guidelines, 2013. Further, in the absence of the prescribed registers 
at the District, Block, and Gram Panchayat levels, the completeness, traceability, and 
timeliness of disposal of complaints cannot be verified. 
In reply, the Department stated (August 2025) that the complaint register shall be 
maintained properly in future and further stated that efforts are made to establish 
helpline for redressal of grievances at district level.

2.5.6  Citizen’s Charter 
As per Paragraph 13.12.1 of Operational Guidelines,  Citizens’ Charter shall cover all 
aspects of the duties of Panchayats and officials under the Act.  It should describe the 
specific steps involved in implementing the provisions of the Act  and lay down the 
minimum service levels mandated by these provisions on the  Panchayats and the  
officers concerned. 
Audit noted that no Citizens’ Charter was prepared by the Department during the period 
from 2019 -20 to 2023 -24. The absence of such a Charter  deprived beneficiaries of 
awareness regarding the roles and responsibilities of Panchayats and officials, as well 
as the minimum service standards envisaged in the MGNREG Act.  
In reply, the ACDs ( Kupwara, Anantnag and Jammu) while accepting the audit 
observation stated (September 2024, October 2024 and November 2024) that the Citizen 
Charter will be prepared as per the guidelines of the Scheme. The Additional Secretary, 
RDD stated (January 2025) that the Citizen Charter is already in the form of Annual 
Master Circulars.  
The reply is not satisfactory as Annual Master Circulars are internal administrative 
instructions and do not constitute a Citizens’ Charter, which is a public-facing document 
mandatorily required under Paragraph 13.12.1 of the MGNREGA Operational 
Guidelines, 2013 to specify service standards, timelines, and responsibilities for 
beneficiaries.  
2.5.7  Maintenance of Mandatory Records 
MGNREGA Operational  Guidelines, 2013 (Chapter-10) describes the mandatory 
records required to be maintained. Further, GoI issued instructions (November 2016) 
for proper maintenance of records to ensure effective implementation of Scheme. It 
provided simplified formats of seven Registers to ease the functioning of the field-level 
personnel and  reduce duplication of work, without compromising the quality of 
information especially those relating to entitlements of workers.  
Register II (Gram Sabha Register), Register III (Demand for work, allocation of work 
and payment of wages register) and Register V (Fixed Asset register) were required to 
be maintained manually. Further, Register I ( JC application , registration , issue and 
household employment reports), Register IV- (Work register), Register VI (Complaint 
register) and Register VII  (Material register) can be maintained partially manually and 
partially printed and pasting from MIS itself. 
Audit noted that in 40 sampled GPs  of 10 sampled blocks,  none of the prescribed 
registers was maintained during 2019 -24. Consequently, crucial data such as 
applications for work, allotment of  work, performance of work, wages or

be recorded or verified, as required under the Scheme guidelines.

2.5.8  Conclusion 
The Department did not assess the quantum and timing of labour demand as no  
door-to-door survey was conducted, despite this being mandated in the Operational 
Guidelines. Absence of District Perspective Plans indicate s that planning was largely 
input-driven rather than demand -driven. Non-constitution of District Level Technical 
Committees, absence of IEC Plans and non -establishment of the Human Resource 
Development and Capacity Building Division weakened technical scrut iny, awareness 
generation, and capacity building under the Scheme. 
Vacancies of Programme Officers, Accounts Assistants, Technical Assistants and Gram 
Rozgar Sahayaks , coupled with uneven distribution across districts, constrained 
effective supervision, record maintenance, and timely service delivery. 
Further, non -maintenance of essential records, inordinate delays in payments to 
suppliers and release of wages to workers without using Aadhaar Based Payment (ABP) 
methods reflected systemic weaknesses. 
There were also instances of delayed release of funds by the UT Government to the 
implementing Department, non -payment of unemployment allowances, delayed 
payment of wages and expenditure on inadmissible items. The Scheme implementation 
suffered from lacunae in maintenance of basic records such as measurement books and 
muster rolls, thereby undermining transparency. 
Monitoring and grievance redressal mechanisms were inadequate. The State 
Employment Guarantee Council (SEGC), envisaged as the apex steering and monitoring 
body, failed to discharge its role effectively, as its meetings were not held regularly and 
the decisions taken were not fully implemented. Further, there were shortcomings in 
conduct of social audits and settlement of objections raised in social audit reports. 
2.5.9  Recommendations 
The UT Government may consider: 
➢ Conducting door -to-door surveys, updating job cards, preparing 
development/perspective plans, stepping up IEC activities, and organising 
Rozgar Diwas on a regular basis so that a realistic assessment of local needs 
of employment be made.

➢ Ensuring timely release of funds by the UT Government, prompt payment of 
wages to workers, payment of unemployment allowances to eligible 
beneficiaries, and clearance of pending compensation for delayed wage 
payments. 
➢ Strict m onitoring of prescribed activities including the  concurrent social 
audit by constituting institutions of State/District Quality Monitors, 
Ombudsman and preparation of Citizen’s Charter.
