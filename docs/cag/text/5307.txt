Preface

This Report of the Comptroller and Auditor General of India contains the results
of Audit of Jammu district in Jammu and Kashmir. The Report has been prepared
for submission to the Governor of the State of Jammu and Kashmir under Article
151(2) of the Constitution of India.

The Audit involved a review of the significant socio-economic developmental
programmes implemented in the district during 2006-11. The audit was conducted
by a test-check of records of the Chief Planning Officer, the Deputy
Commissioner, Departmental Heads of Education, Health, Rural Development,
Urban Development, Forest, Public Health Engineering, Power, Roads and
Buildings, Irrigation, Tourism and Police Departments. Implementation of
developmental programmes subsequent to March 2011 has also been commented
upon wherever necessary.

The Audit has been conducted in conformity with the Auditing Standards issued
by the Comptroller and Auditor General of India.

Introduction

To provide an effective delivery system so that the benefits envisaged under various
programmes undertaken by the Government percolate down to the people, the
Government of India made public participation mandatory vide 74° Amendment Act,
1992 to the Constitution of India by empowering local governments at District, Block
and Gram Panchayat level so as to elicit their views in plan formulation by their
active participation and to inculcate in them a sense of ownership. As the Panchayati
Raj System has not been fully implemented in the State due to non-enactment of the
74" amendment to the Constitution of India by the State Legislature, the involvement
of these agencies is negligible and the decisions in the implementation of any
programme in the Districts is taken at the district level by District Development
Boards (DDB) headed by the Chief Minister of the State/ Senior Cabinet Minister
constituted for the purpose comprising the District Development Commissioner,
Additional Deputy Commissioner, Chief Planning Officer and elected representatives
of the district. It was in this context that district centric approach towards these
aspects was thought imperative to assess the impact of various socio-economic
developmental activities on various sectors undertaken in the district and to bring out
shortcomings in achievement of the laid down targets along with significant positives.

Substantial funds to the tune of € 2880.03 crore were allocated during 2006-2011 to
social, economic and general sectors by the State Government and also by the
Government of India (GOI) through flagship programmes (Centrally Sponsored.
Schemes-CSS). Most of the funds provided through CSS are released directly by the
GOI to the executing agencies instead of routing the same through the budget of the
State Government to avoid loss of time in release of funds. Audit was conducted
during August 2010 to April 2011 of different sectors like Education, Medical, Public
Health (PHE), Sanitation, Rural Development, Forest, Tourism etc. to ascertain the
impact of various programmes undertaken during 2006-07 to 2010-11 by State
Government and the Government of India (GOI). The audit findings have been
discussed in the succeeding paragraphs.

Planning

approach for development of the District had not been formulated with the result the
critical gaps in developmental activities remained unattended. The District
Administration plays little or no role in execution or monitoring of the centrally
sponsored schemes.

Acute shortage of doctors to the extent of 41 per cent (i.e., shortage of 52 against the

Water Supply and Sanitation

The department did not have any perspective or annual action plans for providing
potable water. Water supply schemes in rural areas were not prioritized and instances
of funding new schemes when old schemes were lying incomplete were rampant. Out
of 85 schemes taken up under Accelerated Rural Water Supply Programme (ARWSP)
during 2003-07 to be completed within the envisaged period of one year to three
years, 19 (22 per cent) schemes were incomplete as of March 2011. Taking up of
ineligible schemes and cost and time overruns in completion of schemes were issues
of great concern. Out of 133 schemes under execution during 2006-11, only 75 had
been completed during the period and the balance 58 schemes remained incomplete
due to insufficient funds. The faulty planning and poor controls over implementation
resulted in time-overrun of two to six years and cost-overrun of हैं 11.35 crore in 23
schemes. The department had not collected revenue on account of water charges
resulting in loss of revenue of € 0.63crore as of March 2011 to the public exchequer.
The delivery as well as the quality of public hygiene and sanitation services was
grossly inadequate.

(Paragraph: 5.3 and 5.4)

Recommendations

The department may prioritize water supply schemes based on proper assessment of
needs and execute them efficiently. Funds may be provided in such a manner that new
schemes are not taken up before the completion of ongoing schemes. Steps may be
taken to regularise or disconnect illegal electricity connections. The arrears in water
connection charges may be recovered immediately. Sanitation facilities may be
provided and upgraded through efficient utilization of funds available under various
schemes and an effective system of waste management in urban areas should be
ensured.

CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION

District Jammu derives its name from the city of Jammu which was founded by Raja
Jamboo Lochan in the 14" century BC. The District with an area of 3097 sq km is
bounded in the north and north east by the Tehsils of Reasi in Udhampur District, in
the east and south east partly by Tehsil Ramnagar of Udhampur District and partly by
Tehsil Billawar of Kathua District, in the south and south west by Kathua District and
Sialkote District of Rawalpindi (Pakistan) and in the north west by Tehsil Nowshara
of District Rajouri and parts of the District Bhimber now under the occupation of
Pakistan. The District falls in the Himalayas. As per Census 2011, Jammu District is
most populous District of the State with provisional population of 15,26,406 (12.16
per cent of the population of the State) with 53.15 per cent of them residing in rural
areas. The literacy rate in the District is 83.89 per cent which is higher than the State
average of 68.74 per cent and National average of 74.04 per cent. The District
comprises four Tehsils and eight blocks covering 297 panchayats with 771 inhabited
and 88 uninhabited villages (Census 2001).

CHAPTER 3: PLANNING

3.1 District Policy and Planning

The Constitution of India (74th Amendment Act, 1992) mandates establishment of a
District Planning Committee (DPC) to consolidate plans prepared by the Panchayats
and Municipalities in the District and to prepare a draft development plan for the
District. The chairman of the DPC is to forward the Development Plan, as
recommended by the DPC, to the State Government.

It was seen in audit that the District Planning Committee (DPC) had not been
constituted due to non-enactment of the 74" amendment of the Constitution of India
by the State Legislature. In the absence of the DPC, the developmental works in
various sectors under the District Plan had been identified by the concerned
departments on need-basis in consultation with the members of the DDB. The works
programmes prepared for inclusion in the District plans are approved by the DDB
headed by the Hon’ble Chief Minister.

The following points were observed.

e No any role was played by the District administration in the execution of
Centrally Sponsored Schemes and State Plan Schemes. These were planned and
finalised by the concerned Administrative Departments and executed by the
Sectoral Heads.

¢ No long-term perspective plan for the development of the District was found to
have been formulated.

Conclusion

A long term Perspective Plan based on inputs from stakeholders to have an integrated
approach for development of the District had not been formulated with the result the
critical gaps in developmental activities remained unattended. The District
Administration plays little or no role in execution or monitoring of the centrally
sponsored schemes.

Recommendations

e A long term and well integrated plan may be formulated for the effective
implementation of the developmental schemes in the District.

e District administration should be made responsible for monitoring the
implementation of programmes of the schemes and initiating mid course
corrections wherever necessary.

CHAPTER 5: SOCIAL SERVICES

Given the fact that human development indicators such as access to education, health,
provision of drinking water and sanitation facilities (rural and urban) etc. have a
strong link with eradication of poverty, the GOI and the State Government have
prioritised these sectors by providing large funds through Centrally Sponsored

5.1 HEALTH

The Chief Medical Officer (CMO) Jammu functioning under the Health and Family
Welfare Department is responsible for providing healthcare services to the people of
the District. To improve the health status of the people in the District, healthcare
services are provided through a network of seven Government hospitals and 26
private hospitals/nursing homes. In addition to this, healthcare services in the rural
areas of the District are delivered through a network of seven Community Health
Centres (CHCs), 29 Primary Health Centres (PHCs) and 194 Sub-Centres (SCs). To
ascertain the efficiency of delivery of services in the health sector, records of three
hospitals in Jammu city and three CHCs, 14 PHCs and 32 SCs of five blocks were
test-checked in audit. The important points noticed as a result thereof are discussed
below under Urban health-care and Rural health-care separately.

5.1.2. National Rural Health Mission (NRHM)

In order to provide accessible, affordable, accountable, effective and reliable
healthcare facilities in rural areas and reduce Infant/Maternal Mortality Rate, the GOL
launched National Rural Health Mission (NRHM) in April 2005. For the purpose,
tural areas of the District were divided into seven’ blocks. Records of five Block
Medical Officers (BMOs), three CHCs out of seven, 14 PHCs and 32 SCs out of 29
PHCs and 194 SCs respectively were examined in audit. Following points were
noticed:

Planning and Project Formulation

The NRHM envisages carrying out of preparatory studies viz. mapping of services,
household surveys and facility surveys at village, block and District levels. The
Mission (hereinafter) further envisages preparation of perspective plan as well as
Annual District Action Plans by aggregating the Block Health Action Plans prepared
in all blocks on the basis of household and facility surveys.

Records showed that the District level Perspective Plan (PP) had not been
implemented notwithstanding the fact that the same had been prepared (December
2007). As a result, the achievement of goals set in the plan had not been ensured.
Further, records showed that the District Annual Plans had never been prepared
except for the year 2010-11; instead, the Mission had been implemented through the
State annual plans prepared at the State level. Non-formulation of District level
annual plans defeated the basic objective of the Mission to decentralize planning and
implementation arrangement to ensure that need-based and community-owned
District health action plans become the basis for interventions in the sector.

The CMO stated (June 2011) that Perspective Plan was implemented as per the
directions of the GOI and annual plans were prepared at the District level after
consolidating Block Health action plans. The reply was not acceptable as the
Perspective Plan was not implemented as verified during audit and AAPs except for
2010-11 could not be produced before Audit when called for.

Financial Management

The Annual Plans had never been prepared at the District level during 2006-10, thus
the State Health Society (SHS) released funds without assessing the actual
requirement leading to large unspent balances ranging between 21 and 56 per cent
during 2006-11. The unspent balance as on March, 2011 had come down considerably
to 21 per cent due to preparation of District annual action plan for the year 2010-11. It
clearly indicated that the actual requirement of funds at the District level could not be
assessed due to non-formulation of need-based and community-owned District Health
Action Plans and due to release of funds on the basis of State Annual Plans prepared
at the state level.

children, etc. In the District, the programme, however, commenced from 2002-03.

Waste Management

As per Municipal Solid Wastes (Management and Handling) Rules, 2000, every
municipal authority, within its territorial area, is responsible for collection, storage,
segregation, transportation, processing and disposal of municipal solid wastes.

Collection

About 300 metric tonne of municipal waste/garbage generated daily in 71 municipal

Scrutiny of records revealed that no proper mechanism existed to handle and dispose
of the wastes in accordance with the provisions of the Solid Waste (Management and
Handling) Rules 2000 where under such waste are to be treated scientifically so as to
avoid threat to human health and surrounding environment. Waste collection
schedules for different areas had to be notified as required under rules, which was not
done by the Corporation.

Segregation/Storage/Transportation

required to establish and maintain storage facilities in such a way that these did not
create unhygienic and insanitary conditions. Storage bins were to be painted green for

Audit noticed that the Corporation had not made adequate arrangements in this regard.
The wastes were being dumped either in open spaces or common bins at a few places.
Most of the waste was not being put in the bins provided and instead had been found
dumped in open sites near public places along main road and streets. Also different
colour bins for biodegradable, recyclable and other wastes had not been provided. In
the absence of notified time schedules for clearance, the storage bins were found over-

Provisions of Prevention of Food Adulteration Act and rules made thereunder provide
for frequent inspection of units dealing with manufacture, storage or sale of food
articles, lifting of food samples by Food Inspector for subsequent analysis and
maintenance of records of all inspections made and action taken by him in

revealed that out of 1,174 food samples lifted during 2005-10, 156 (13 per cent) were

6.2.2. Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act

The main objective of the Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee
Act 2005 (MNREGA) is to enhance livelihood security in rural areas by providing at
least 100 days of guaranteed wage employment in a financial year to every household
whose adult members volunteer to do unskilled manual work. The scheme was
launched in the District during September 2007.

Planning

A key indicator of success of the scheme is time-bound implementation which
requires advance planning. The basic aim of the planning process is to ensure that the
District plan is prepared well in advance to offer productive employment on demand.
For this purpose, the guidelines envisage preparation of District perspective plan to
provide inputs for Annual Work Plan of the District. The aim is to identify all types of
MNREGA works that should be encouraged in the District and the potential linkages
between these works for providing long term employment generation and sustained
development.

Though the perspective plan of District had been prepared, yet the annual work plans
were prepared on the basis of the projected works/up-to-date inputs of the Block
Development Officers as approved by the District Development Commissioner,
Jammu. This implied that the annual plans did not flow from the perspective plan.
This not only defeated the preparation of perspective plan but was also violated the
guidelines.

Formulation of Annual Works Plan (AWP)

The guidelines envisage that while preparing the development plan, the number of
works/ projects in shelf in a village should be more than the estimated demand so that
employment could be provided to the workers for the time and duration they want for

Swarna Jayanti Shahri Rozgar Yojna (SJSRY)

Poverty alleviation is one of the major goals of economic planning. To achieve this
goal, the GOI launched (December 1997) ‘Swarna Jayanti Shahri Rozgar Yojna
(SJSRY)’ after subsuming the ongoing urban poverty alleviation schemes /
programmes. The main objective of the programme is to provide gainful employment
to the urban unemployed or under-employed through setting up of self-employment
ventures or provision of wage employment. Besides, the Prime Minister’s
Reconstruction Plan (2004-05) for J&K, inter alia, includes provision of self
employment opportunities for urban unemployed persons.

The two major components of SJSRY such as Urban Self Employment Programme
(USEP) and Urban Wage Employment Programme (UWEP) were reviewed in audit.
The major audit findings are discussed in the succeeding paragraphs.

6.8.1. Urban Self-Employment Programme (USEP)

The USEP focuses on providing assistance to individual urban poor beneficiaries for
setting up self employment ventures and micro-enterprises. The programme envisages
coverage of urban population below poverty line (BPL) as defined by the Planning
Commission from time to time. The scheme was introduced in the District in 1997-98.

Non-Framing of Grazing Policy

To promote natural regeneration and vegetative growth for restoration of forest cover,

it is essential to check unbridled grazing in the forest area. As the forests around

Jammu Division are not commercial forests, these are very important from ecological
considerations and as such require utmost attention for preservation, conservation and
afforestation. In the absence of any firm grazing policy on scientific lines, the biotic
pressure on these forests was very heavy not only by the local animals but by the large
number of sheep and goats of the Bakarwals (tribals), especially during the winter
season. This leads to scanty growth of these forests and the actual incidence of
grazing in these forests is far beyond the carrying capacity of these forests due to
settlement of Gujjars/Bakerwal in these forests during the period from October to
May every year. The un-restricted grazing not only damages the young growth but
also disrupts regeneration of the trees, shrubs and herbs during the Monsoon period. It
not only destroys seed source but disrupts the photosynthesis process, besides causing
surface soil erosion. To avoid this, a firm grazing policy is required which had not
been framed as of March 2011.

The rehabilitation of degraded forests envisages checking by plantation, construction
of check dams, water harvesting structures and natural regeneration. The major items
of works undertaken include fencing plantations and other engineering works. Despite
spending = 1.69 crore during 2006-10, there was no reduction of degraded area and it
remained static at 2,557 Ha.

As per the State Forest Conservation Act-1997, forests were to be raised over
degraded area equal to twice the area diverted for non-forest purpose. Further, rules

The performance of the departmental nurseries was dismal as the survival rate of

6.10 Tourism

The tourism activities at a particular place are directly related to the arrival of tourists
at that place. The more the arrival, the more economic activities get generated and
make impact on other sectors also. Tourism generates multiple effects on the service
sector such as Agriculture, Horticulture, Poultry and Handicrafts. Besides, tourism
provides direct and indirect employment to a large number of people. Jammu &
Kashmir is an important tourist destination of the country and has been a place of
attraction for tourists since centuries. All the three regions of the State viz; Kashmir,
Jammu and Ladakh regions attract a large number of tourists. Jammu is the Gateway
for tourists in the State as all the tourists coming to the State are to pass through
Jammu city. To accommodate the tourists, proper infrastructural facilities has to be in
place which is particularly essential in view of influx of a large number of pilgrims
who visit Mata Vaishno Devi Shrine at Katra.

Tourist influx

Data produced to audit by the department showed that while as the influx of pilgrims
to Jammu had increased from 61 lakh in 2004 to 87.49 lakh in 2010, the tourism had
gathered pace in Kashmir valley also which increased from 7.77 lakh tourists to 11.81
lakh during the corresponding period.
