This report has been prepared for submission to the Government of Assam
in accordance with the terms and conditions of the Technical Guidance and Support
(TGS) on the audit of accounts of Panchayati Raj Institutions (PRIs) and Urban Local
Bodies (ULBs) entrusted by the Government of Assam to the Comptroller & Auditor
General (C&AG) of India under Section 20 (1) of the C&AG’s (DPC) Act, 1971, in May
2002.

2. This Annual Technical Inspection Report for the year 2010-11 is a
consolidation of major audit findings arising out of audit of accounts of 42 PRIs (3 Zilla
Parishad, 13 Anchalik Panchayat and 26 Gaon Panchayat) and 5 ULBs (3 Municipal
Board and 2 Town Committee) conducted during 2010-11 (January to June 2011).

3. The Report contains three chapters. Section ‘A’ of Chapter I contains an
overview of the organization, finances, devolution and accountability framework and
Section ‘B’ contains the comments on financial reporting of PRIs and ULBs. Chapter II
has two sections viz, Section ‘A’ containing results of District Rural Sector Audit and
Section ‘B’ has the results of Audit of Transactions of PRIs. Chapter III contains results
of Audit of Transactions of ULBs for the year ended 31st March 2011.

4. The purpose of this report is to provide an overview of the functioning of
PRIs and ULBs in the State and draw the attention of the concerned Executive

Departments to take appropriate remedial action wherever necessary.

11 Introduction

Seventy Third and Seventy Fourth Constitutional amendments gave constitutional status
to the Panchayati Raj Institutions (PRIs) and Urban Local Bodies (ULBs) and established
a system of uniform structure, holding of regular elections and regular flow of funds
through Finance Commission etc. As a follow up, the states were required to equip and
entrust these bodies with such powers, functions and responsibility so as to enable them
to function as institutions of self government. The Constitutional amendments intended to
pave the way for smooth transition to fiscal decentralization at the grass root level. In
particular, the PRIs and ULBs were required to prepare plans and implement schemes for
economic development and social justice including those functions included in the

Eleventh and Twelfth Schedules of the Constitution.

Assam has a very strong historical background of local self government. Post ape

Constitutional amendment, the State enacted Assam Panchayat Act, 1994 and framed
Assam Panchayat (Administrative) Rules, 2002 and Assam Panchayat (Financial) Rules,
2002 to enable Local Bodies (LBs) to work as the third tier of government. The State has

also identified and amended other related laws to empower LBs.

There were 2407 PRIs and 89 ULBs in the State as on 31 March 2011.All the 2407 PRIs
were in General Areas as the Panchayati Raj system does not exist in Sixth Schedule
Areas. Local governance in Sixth Schedule Areas is vested with the Autonomous District
Councils (ADCs). Of the 89 ULBs, 72 were in General Areas and governed according to
the provisions of the Assam Municipal Act, 1956. The remaining 17 ULBs falling within
Sixth Schedule Areas were governed by the rules framed by respective ADCs.
Recommendations of Third Assam State Finance Commission (TASFC) did not cover the
ADCs.

The Guwahati Municipal Corporation (GMC) was constituted under the Guwahati
Municipal Corporation Act, 1971 and started functioning with effect from 15 February
1974.

1.2 State profile

Assam is situated in the North East (NE) part of India. According to 2011 census, the
State covers an area of 78,438 sq. km. with a population of 3.12 crore. The rural
population in the State was 2.68 crore (86 per cent) whereas urban population was 44
lakh (14 per cent). The percentage shares of area and population of the State to that of the
country are 2.4 and 2.58 respectively. The State has the highest population density among
NE States at 397 persons per sq. km. As against decadal growth of 17.6 per cent at
national level, the population of the State has grown by 16.85 per cent over the period
2001-11. The sex ratio of Assam at 954 females to 1000 males is higher than the national
average of 940. Female literacy rate of the State rose to 67.27 per cent from 51.85 per

cent in 2001. (source: ecostatassam.nic.in)
The State can be divided into three distinct geographical regions viz.

(i) The Brahmaputra Valley comprising of twenty two districts in plains with a total
area of 56194 sq.km;

(ii) | The Hill areas consisting of two districts with a total area of 15322 sq. km. and;

(iii) | The Barak Valley covering three districts with a total area of 6922 sq.km.

The districts are further subdivided into 56 swb-divisions and 145 revenue circles for the
convenience of administration and revenue collection. The economy of Assam continues
to be predominantly agrarian. The service sector along with tea industry dominates

Assam's economy.

As per Planning Commission and Ministry of Panchayati Raj (MoPR) report (2009-10)
the state has 11 backward districts out of 278 backward districts in India.

[ 1.4 Organizational Setup in State Government and Local Bodies

The Chief Secretary of the State is the overall in-charge of monitoring the functions and
activities of local bodies in the State. The Principal Secretary, Panchayat and Rural
Development Department is the administrative head of PRIs and is assisted by the
Commissioner, Panchayat and Rural Development in allocation of fund and in exercising

overall control and supervision of functions and schemes at the State level.

The Principal Secretary, Urban Development Department is the administrative head of
ULBs and is assisted by the Directors, Municipal Administration and Town & Country
Planning in allocation of funds and in exercising overall control and supervision of

functions and schemes at the State level.

The Secretary, Guwahati Development Department is the administrative head of

Guwahati Municipal Corporation (GMC).

The PRIs and ULBs are functioning under democratically elected bodies. PRI in each tier
is headed by a President. However, GP President is represented in AP and likewise AP
President is represented in ZP. As regards ULBs, a TC or MB is headed by a Chairman.
The only Municipal Corporation in the State is headed by a Mayor.

Sections 102 (2) and 103 (3) of AP Act, 1994 provide that Government shall appoint a
Chief Accounts Officer (CAO) and a Chief Planning Officer in each ZP. The CAO shall
advise the ZP on financial matters and shall be responsible for all matters relating to the
accounts of the ZP including preparation of annual accounts and the budget. The Chief
Planning Officer shall advise the ZP on plan formulation and shall be responsible for all

matters relating to the planning of the ZP.

However, the Government created only the post of CAO (October 2010) for Zilla
Parishad to which no appointment has so far been made by the State Government.
Continuing delay in appointment of CAO, even after creation of the post contributed

substantially to the occurrence of irregularities and deficiencies in the accounts of PRIs.

The executive function in the GMC is exercised by the Commissioner, GMC, under
section 34 of GMC Act, 1971. As per section 53 of Assam Municipal Act (AMA), 1956,
MBs may appoint Executive Officer with the approval of the Government. The Executive
Officer shall function under the overall control of the Board and under the direct
supervision of the Chairman. However, there is no executive officer in any MB or TC

where elected body is functioning. The Chairman exercises the executive function.

The following is observed in audit:

1.13 Database on LB Finances

Based on recommendation of the Eleventh Finance Commission (EFC), Comptroller and

PRIs. The database formats were prescribed with a view to having a consolidated position
of the sector-wise resources and application of funds by PRIs, details of works executed
by LBs and their physical progress etc. Though, the State Government accepted (August

(February 2012). Joint Director P&RDD stated (July 2011) that in the first phase all Zilla

1.15 Vigilance mechanism

1.15.1 Ombudsman

The ombudsman conducts investigation and enquires into instances of maladministration,
corruption, favoritism, nepotism, lack of integrity, excessive action, inaction, abuse of
position etc, on the part of officials and elected representatives of LBs. He can even
register cases, suo moto, if the instances of the above kind come to his notice. The State
Government under the Chairpersonship of the then Chief Secretary, Assam constituted a
committee (August 2010) for selection of Ombudsman and Deputy Ombudsman under
MGNREGA. The State Government in November 2011 selected 10 Ombudsmen and 2
Deputy Ombudsmen in 12 out of 27 districts in the State under section 27 (1) of the
MGNREG Act, 2005

There was, however, no provision in the AP Act and AM Act regarding setting up of
ombudsman for LBs.

1.15.2 Social Audit

The primary objective of social audit is to bring the activities of LBs under close

surveillance of public for which the later needed to have access to records and documents
of LBs. Such immediate access to information would facilitate transparency and
accountability in day-to-day functioning of LBs. The State Finance Department issued

guidelines (May 2009) for social audit which, infer alia, included the following:

> Use of Gaon Sabhas and Ward Committees as important vehicles for spread of
awareness about social audit.
Appointment of nodal officer at the level of Gaon Sabhas and Ward Committees

who would register complaints and fix the date for social auditing.

1.15.3 Lokayukta
The Assam Lokayukta and Upalokayukta Act 1985 (Assam Act No. XX of 1986) came

into effect in the State from December 12, 1986 for the purpose of conducting

investigation into grievances and allegation against ministers, legislators and other public
servants including those of PRIs and ULBs. However, the Lokayukta in Assam has not

been authorized to register a case suo moto as in some other states in the country.

The institution of Lokayukta was established in 1989 in the state. At present the
institution is headed by the Upalokayukta, while the post of Lokayukta has been lying
vacant for the last 16 years (since March 1995). The Upalokayukta had received a total of
1018 complaints till December 31, 2011. The Assam Upalokayukta has recently
submitted a proposal to the State Government for setting up Lokayukta cells in every
district and Sub-divisional headquarters, to create awareness about the ombudsman.
(Source: The Telegraph, dated 9 April 2012 & rightspeaks.blogspot.in/201 1/04/pathetic-

plight-of-assam-lokayukta.html).

There is a need to increase awareness among and periodic information to the people about
the existence and functioning of the anti-corruption mechanism to make it effective and
useful to the public and the Lokayukta should be empowered to register the cases suo

moto to strengthen the institution.

1.18 Legal framework

Transparent and timely financial reporting in LBs is an essential prerequisite for ensuring
accountability. Matters relating to drawal of fund, form of bills, incurring of expenditure
and maintenance of primary financial records are governed by the provisions of the AP
Act, AP (F) Rules, AM Act, GMC Act and AM (Accounts) Rules and other departmental

standing orders and instructions.

The PRIs maintain accounts on cash basis. Receipts and payments are required to be

incorporated in the accounts in order to make the accounts complete and accurate.

The AP Act and AP (F) Rules, however, did not contain any provision for preparation and

rendering of Annual Accounts by PRIs.

1.18.1 Deficiencies in maintenance of primary financial records

Regular maintenance of Cash Book, daily closing of Cash Book after due attestation by
the authorised officer, preparation of Properties and Asset Register, Register of
Assessment and Collection of Taxes etc., are some of the controls in the matter of

accounting. An effective internal control system gears up the supervisory organisation

and helps it to insulate itself from financial irregularities and instances of fraud.

Deficiencies noticed in the maintenance of primary financial records of PRIs during
2010-11 are detailed below:

a. Improper maintenance of records

Rule 8, sub rule 4 (a), (b) and (c) of AP (F) Rules 2002 stipulate that all moneys received
and payments made should be entered in the Cash Book which should be closed every
day. Monthly closing of Cash Book with physical verification of cash and reconciliation
of Cash Book balance with bank balance under proper authentication are to be done. Sub-
tule 4 (e) further stipulates that at the close of each month, the bank balance as reflected
in the Cash Book shall be reconciled with balances as per bank account. However, the

following is noticed in audit:

> Test check Cash Book of 29 PRIs for the period ranging from 2002-03 to 2010-

11 revealed that there were more than one Cash Book which were not closed daily



Financial Reporting
or monthly, analysis of closing balance and physical verification of cash was also

not done.

In addition to the operation of multiple Cash Books, funds received under
different schemes/ activities were parked in a number of bank accounts for each
scheme. Cash Book balances were not reconciled with bank balances in any PRI.
Instances of un-reconciled balances with differences ranging from 0.15 lakh to

26.91 crore in 5 PRIs as on March 2011 are indicated in Appendix 1.3.

There were short/non accountal of receipts and short receipts of 718.62 lakh in

six PRIs as shown in Appendix 1.4.

None of the PRIs furnished certificate to Audit declaring the total number of bank
accounts maintained. Audit could not, thus, ascertain the correctness of the bank

transactions recorded in the Cash Book.

Not only did the above indicate that PRIs needed to do a lot in the above specific critical
areas but also indicated failure of the DDOs of the PRIs to adhere to the provisions of
financial rules to ensure proper maintenance of Cash Book and subsidiary records.

Possibility of fraud and embezzlement of money could not, therefore, be ruled out.
b. Register of Advances

PRIs granted various advances to the members and officials. Advances were also granted
to the departmental officials, construction committees, suppliers etc., for execution of
works/ supplies etc. However, none of the 42 PRIs, test checked in audit during 2010-11,
maintained Advance Registers for accountal of advances and watching recovery/
adjustments thereof. Instances of advances remaining outstanding in 3 PRIs amounting to
227.96 lakh are indicated in Appendix 1.5.

The Assam Panchayat (Financial) Rules, 2002 {AP (F) Rules} neither provided for
maintenance of ‘Advance Register’ nor prescribed any mechanism for

recovery/adjustments of advances. In the absence of any provision in the Rules, Advance

Registers were stated to have not been maintained. PRIs, thus, failed to watch recovery/

adjustment of advances and accordingly, treated the amounts of advances as final

expenditure, which is fraught with the risk of possible fraud/embezzlement.

1.19 Annual Accounts and Budgetary control

1.19.1 Non preparation of Annual Accounts

The Government of Assam accepted (August 2004) the accounting formats prescribed by
C&AG of India for PRIs and accordingly amended the Assam Panchayat (Financial)
Rules 2002 in August 2004. However, neither the formats for preparation of Monthly and
Annual Accounts, as prescribed by the C&AG of India, were incorporated in the Assam
Panchayat (Financial) Rules 2002 nor any provision was made in the said Rules for

preparation and submission of monthly and annual accounts.

Assam Municipal (Account Rules) 1961 framed under Assam Municipal Act 1956,
provides for maintenance of accounts of municipalities on cash basis and did not
prescribe formats for preparation of annual accounts by ULBs. The State Government, in
the line of National Municipal Accounting Manual (NMAM), prepared the draft State
Municipal Accounting Manual (SMAM) in July 2010 which is based on accrual based
accounting system and amended the AM Act, 1956 in May 2011, to provide for
maintenance of accounts on accrual basis and preparation of Receipt and Payment

Account, Income and Expenditure Account and the Balance Sheet.

However, the accounts of ULBs continued to be maintained on cash basis due to late
amendment of the AM Act and thereby true and fair view of financial affairs of ULBs and

their assets and liabilities were not disclosed.

1.21 Internal control system at the level of LBs

Internal control mechanism is an integral function of an organization which helps it to
govern its activities effectively and achieve its objectives. It is intended to provide
reasonable assurance of proper enforcement of Acts, Rules and Bye-laws. Various
internal control measures in financial and operational activities when built into the
departmental rules and manuals and their strict adherence, would minimize the risk of
errors and irregularities. It is also designed to provide reasonable assurance to the effect
that the following general objectives are achieved.

Fulfilling accountability obligations ;

Compliance of applicable rules and regulations;

Implementation of programmes is carried out in an orderly, economical, efficient

and effective manner.

State Government. This is thus a systemic deficiency at the level of State Government.

Internal Audit is an important instrument to examine and evaluate the level of compliance

far been conducted (February 2012). The department has no Audit Manual of its own and

Section 128, Sub-sections (1) and (2) of the AP Act, 1994, provides for submission of
Annual Administrative Report of the preceding year of AP and ZP to the Government by
30 September every year. Report of the ZP together with a memorandum by the
Government reviewing the working of the ZP should be laid before the State Legislature
as per sub-section (3) of the section ibid. However, neither the PRIs prepared their
Annual Administrative Reports nor the State Government (P&RDD) called for Annual
Administrative Reports from PRIs for consolidation and submission to the State

Legislature.

Annual Administrative Report of GMC for the preceding year together with a statement

of receipts, disbursements and balance at credit of the Municipal Fund at the close of the

year is required to be submitted to the Government under Section 136 of GMC Act.

However, test check of records of GMC in August-September 2010 revealed that no such

report was submitted to the Government.

1.25 Conclusion

> Financial reporting is not effective and inadequate with no provision in PRI and
ULB Acts and Rules for preparation and rendering of annual accounts by Local
Bodies. Non preparation of annual accounts resulted in lack of transparency of
state of affairs as well as non-disclosure of financial assets and liabilities of Local
Bodies. There is no provision for reporting of misappropriation, fraud etc., to the

State Government in PRI Acts and Rules,

Internal control mechanism was weak as evidenced by non-maintenance of
Community Assets, Register of Advances and non-furnishing of utilization

certificates by LBs and deficiencies in maintenance of primary financial records at



all levels of PRIs and ULBs. There is no provision for internal audit in ULB Acts
and Rules. Asset Register was partially maintained. Except the assets created
under MGNREGS, details of assets created under schemes implemented by LBs
were not maintained. As a result, status of assets created under other flagship

schemes could not be assessed.

Appropriation of fund without preparation of budget led to unauthorized
expenditure in PRIs. Preparation of unrealistic budget resulted in over estimation

of revenue in PRIs.

There was poor response and delays in furnishing replies to audit observations

leading to accumulation of outstanding audit objections.

The primary financial records of LBs were deficient.

No vigilance mechanism such as constitution of Ombudsman for conducting

investigation and enquiries into instances of maladministration, corruption etc.

was adopted in the AP as well as AM Act.

Acute shortage of manpower at each level of PRIs hampered the enlarged

functional achievement of the objectives of decentralized governance in PRIs.

Internal control relating to financial management was almost non existent leading

to lapse of TFC grant.

2.1 Introduction

District Rural Sector Audit involved a review of the significant socio-economic
developmental schemes/programmes implemented in the District of Cachar during the
period 2006-11. The audit encompassed an appraisal of Social Sector programmes
relating to Rural Sector. In the economic sector, infrastructure development was reviewed
through an assessment of the projects and schemes implemented for improvement of road
connectivity in villages (MGNREGS, DDP and BRGF schemes), provision of

There is a standard procedure of withdrawal of funds for incurring any government
expenditure which is governed by the General Financial & Treasury Rules, Delegation of
Financial Powers Rules, and specific conditions attached with a given sanction for
withdrawal of funds by the competent authority for a given purpose. This procedure
enables the sanctioning authorities to administer fiscal resource transfer from the
government treasury down to the grassroots level of expenditure. It also enables the
implementing as well as the supervisory officers at all levels to monitor that fund flow
from the government accounts with due regard to efficiency, effectiveness, and economy.
Most importantly, this procedure is designed to keep the public expenditure in a well-

defined accountability loop and within the overall ambit of legislative financial control.

In most of the flagship schemes relating to the rural social development implemented by
the PRIs, the standard procedure had not been followed mainly due to complexity in
mechanisms for flow of fund and differing processes of utilisation governed by guidelines
of different schemes, especially, in schemes like MGNREGS, IAY, SGSY etc, funds are
released by GOI directly to DRDAs without routing the same through the State budget.
As a result, flow of fund and its utilization by the implementing agencies are not readily
reflected in the accounts of the State, which are left with no mechanism to monitor the
flow of fund and exercise legislative financial control on implementation of schemes.
Thus, the rural social development schemes were being implemented without ensuring
that alternative structures were in place for ensuring integrated financial monitoring and
accountability either at the district or at the state levels. This fact is further compounded

by the complexity of the administrative set up explained at paragraph no. 2.2.

There is no mechanism, however, at the district and the state levels to monitor fund flow,
deployment of fund, and expenditure of the social development schemes, much less their
eventual outcome in an aggregated manner at any given point of time. The state accounts
do not reflect the totality of government investments in social development schemes, as
fund for centrally sponsored schemes flow to the implementing agencies outside of

government accounts.

Financial reporting in any organization is a key element of accountability. The best
practices require preparation of General Purpose Financial Statements (GPFS) for each

accounting entity and government as a whole.

GPFS are accounting statements to communicate information about financial position and
performance and include notes and schedules that are needed to clarify items in the

statements, disclosures on assets and liabilities, and physical performance etc.

Rural social sector investment in Cachar has more than doubled in the last five years
(2006-2011). PRIs engaged in implementation of rural social sector schemes, however,
did not prepare GPFS. They are reporting the financial and physical progress of the
schemes dealt with by them to their Directorate either directly or through their next higher

authority. These reports do not contain critical information about assets, loss due to theft

or negligence, pending liabilities, etc. Even in preparation and submission of these

statements, there is no centrality of financial and physical reporting regarding total

investments made in the rural social sector in the district or in each Panchayat.

As recommended by EFC, PRIs ought to prepare annual accounts. However, the Assam
Panchayat (Financial) Rules 2002 did not include any provision for preparation of Annual
Accounts and its submission to any authority. P&RD Department, which was responsible
for monitoring of the activities of PRIs, had not taken any step to ensure that PRIs

2.8.2 Indira Awaas Yojana

2.8.2.1 Indira Awaas Yojana (IAY) is a housing scheme intended to provide houses to
the poor people in rural areas. IAY provides grant for construction of houses to members
of Scheduled Castes/Scheduled Tribes, freed bonded labourers and also to non SC/ST
rural poor who are below the poverty line subject to the condition that benefits to non-

SC/ST should not exceed 40 per cent of total IAY allocation during a financial year.

2.8.2.2 Identification of beneficiaries

DRDAs are to decide Panchayat-wise number of houses to be constructed under IAY,
during a particular financial year and intimate the same to the Gaon Panchayat.
Thereafter, the Gaon Sabha is to select beneficiaries restricting its number to the target
allotted from the list of eligible households, according to IAY guidelines and as per
priorities fixed. Selection by the Gaon Sabha is final and no approval of any higher

authority is required.

2.8.4 Backward Region Grant Fund (BRGF)

BRGF is designed to redress regional imbalances in development. The fund would
provide financial resources for supplementing and converging existing developmental

inflows into identified districts of backward regions so as to:

(a) Bridge critical gaps in local infrastructure and other development requirements that
are not being adequately met through existing inflows,

(b) Strengthen, to this end Panchayat and Municipality level governance with more
appropriate capacity building, facilitate participatory planning, decision making,
implementation and monitoring, to reflect local felt needs,

(c) Provide professional support to local bodies for planning, implementation and
monitoring their plans, and

(d) Improve the performance and delivery of critical functions assigned to Panchayats,

and counter possible efficiency and equity losses on account of inadequate local capacity.

2.15.1.2 Dibrugarh ZP took up a scheme (2009-10) namely, Emergent Management
Measures from Motholla to Oakland at an estimated cost of 1 crore under DDP in 2007-
08 to arrest the severe ongoing erosion in the area. The work was technically sanctioned
(June 2009) by the Additional Chief Engineer, Upper Assam Zone, Water Resource

Audit of Transactions

vouchers by the advance payees raised doubts regarding the bonafideness

Transaction Audit of Urban Local Bodies

Recommendation

Comprehensive data base of all tax payers may be maintained for identification of
defaulters and regular demand notice may be issued to them. State Government
should keep watch on the collection regime and issue necessary instructions to

ULBs for strengthening their own resource of revenues.

Necessary provisions may be made in respective scheme guidelines/grants to
penalize the officials concerned who are found responsible for diversion, or mis-
utilization of funds hampering the intended objectives of the respective schemes.
Internal control mechanism may be strengthened by deploying required trained

manpower and internal audit so as to prevent recurrence of irregularities.

Effective and proper planning for the execution of works needs to be done by the
ULB to enhance efficiency in utilization of funds and also to avoid blockade of
fund. State Government may issue suitable instructions to ULBs to get the

released amount utilized within a specific time frame, and follow up on the same.

Instructions may be issued immediately for maintenance of advance payment
tegisters by all ULBs. Importance of maintaining of advance payment registers
may be seriously conveyed to all ULBs. CPF contribution amount of employees

should be deposited into CPF account through a time bound action plan.
