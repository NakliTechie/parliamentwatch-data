Recommendation (Sl. No. 1) 
 
DIGITAL COMPETITION REGULATION 
 
1.5 The Committee had noted that since the early 1990s, India's economic 
liberalization has been driven by market-based mechanisms. The rapid adoption of 
digital technologies, however, introduced a new phase that presents both immense 
opportunities and significant challenges for competition regulation. The Committee 
had also note that the unique characteristics of digital markets, such as network effects 
and data advantage, have led to a concentration of economic power in a few large 
technology platforms that act as "gatekeepers." This necessitates a nuanced 
regulatory approach to balance innovation incentives with the imperative of 
maintaining fair competition. The Committee further noted that while a National 
Competition Policy (NCP) was drafted in 2011 to enhance market efficiency, it is yet 
to be implemented. The Committee observed that the Competition Act, 2002, with its 
traditional ex-post enforcement, is less effective in the fast-paced digital economy. The 
Committee also specifically observed the exclusion of Virtual Assistants from the Draft 
Digital Competition Bill, noting the need for their inclusion in line with global practices. 
Therefore, a fundamental shift from a reactive to a proactive, ex-ante regulatory 
framework is required to address the complexities posed by practices like self-
preferencing, predatory pricing, and tying and bundling. The Committee, therefore, 
recommended that a nuanced approach is adopted in the Digital Competition Bill 
(DCB), avoiding blanket prohibitions and allowing for context-specific assessments. 
Furthermore, the Committee emphasized that ongoing market studies on AI and other 
sectors should serve as foundational evidence for refining the DCB. The Committee 
stressed the urgency for the CCI to remain agile and continuously adapt its tools and 
strategies to keep pace with rapid technological advancements and ensure effective 
competition law enforcement in the evolving digital landscape. The Committee 
believed that NCP may act as a cross-sectoral framework to foster a unified 

3 
 
competition culture and harmonize various laws and policies across Central and State 
governments. The NCP should also be designed to empower the CCI, ensuring its 
autonomy and encouraging a cooperative relationship with other sectoral regulators 
to enhance coordination and ensure consistent enforcement. 
1.6 In their action taken reply, the Ministry of Corporate Affairs has furnished the 
following written submission: 
Through its timely enforcement actions against anti-competitive practices and 
quick regulation of mergers in all sectors including digital markets, CCI ensures that 
the markets remain competitive and contestable. The law is applied to new age 
markets in a nuanced manner with the endeavour to retain incentives to innovate and 
grow while addressing anti-competitive practices.  
 
Based on stakeholder comments and suggestions, it has been decided to adopt 
an evidence-based and adaptive approach in finalising the Draft Digital Competition 
Bill.  
Accordingly, the Ministry has decided to conduct a Market Study to assess 
appropriate qualitative and quantitative thresholds, review core digital services, and 
evaluate the impact of the proposed ex-ante framework on competition, innovation, 
and market entry. The objective of the study is:  
i) to review and evaluate the proposed qualitative and quantitative thresholds 
outlined in Clause 3 of the draft Digital Competition Bill,  
ii) to review the list of proposed core digital services included in Schedule I of 
the draft DCB,  
iii) to evaluate the implications of the draft Digital Competition Bill on these 
services and their stakeholders, including the potential impact on 
competition and market entry, and 
iv)  to assess the potential impact of the proposed ex- ante framework on 
smaller players including start-ups & MSME’s. 
        The outcomes of this study will guide the finalisation of a balanced and forward-
looking digital competition framework that ensures fairness, competitive neutrality, and 

4 
 
innovation, while equipping the Competition Commission of India with suitable tools to 
effectively regulate digital markets. 
The Request for Proposal (RFP) for engagement of agency for conducting the 
said study was floated on the GeM portal on 03.11.2025 and the last date for 
submission of bid for agency was 05.01.2026. 
With respect to NCP, it is stated that Cabinet Note along with National 
Competition Policy was sent to Cabinet Secretariat on 04.07.2014 seeking approval 
of the Cabinet on the National Competition Policy. The Cabinet considered the same 
in its meeting held on 09.07.2014 and deferred it. At present, there is no proposal 
under consideration for framing an entirely new National Competition Policy (NCP) or 
for revising the existing NCP. 
Nevertheless, based on the recommendations of CLRC, several of the 
amendments introduced to the Competition Act, 2002 through the Competition 
(Amendment) Act, 2023 to support the core objective of National Competition Policy 
namely promoting competition in markets so as to optimise efficiency and maximise 
consumer welfare. 
It is to mention that CCI has taken cognizance of alleged anti-competitive 
practices indulged in by digital players and ordered investigation(s) such as such as 
Google, Apple, Amazon/ Flipkart , Zomato/Swiggy etc.  In cases where an inquiry has 
been completed by the CCI, final orders have been issued imposing monetary 
penalties apart from issuing cease and desist order and remedies. 
 
  

5 
 
1.7 The Committee note that while the Ministry has initiated a critical Market 
Study via a Request for Proposal (RFP) in November, 2025, to refine 
Systemically Significant Digital Enterprise (SSDE) thresholds, the National 
Competition Policy remains in a state of indefinite deferment since 2014. The 
Committee observe that relying solely on the Competition (Amendment) Act, 
2023, maintains a reactive ex-post stance that is structurally inadequate to 
handle the "winner-takes-most" speed of digital gatekeepers, where 
investigations into giant corporates often conclude only after competitors have 
been forced out of the market. Furthermore, the continued exclusion of Virtual 
Assistants from the Draft Digital Competition Bill (DCB) creates a regulatory 
blind spot, diverging from international benchmarks such as the European 
Union’s Digital Markets Act. The Committee, therefore, recommend that the 
Ministry pivot from the stagnant concept of National Competition Policy towards 
the global best practice of a Competition Assessment Framework (CAF), 
institutionalizing a Competition Impact Assessment for all new regulations to 
prevent market distortions at the source, similar to the statutory rigour of an 
Environmental Impact Assessment. This should be coupled with the immediate 
finalization of the DCB—expanded to include Virtual Assistants and Cloud 
Services—and the issuance of a "Strategic Steer" to the CCI to prioritize the 
protection of MSMEs and startups. The Committee also urge the Ministry to fully 
empower and integrate the existing Digital Market Division (DMD) into this new 
regulatory framework, leveraging its established technical expertise and 
algorithmic auditors to ensure that ex-ante oversight and SSDE monitoring are 
seamlessly executed in an AI-driven economic landscape.

Recommendation (Sl. No. 4) 
 
ENFORCEMENT EFFECTIVENESS AND LITIGATION CHALLENGES 
1.8 The Committee noted that the CCI employs a dual approach of enforcement 
and advocacy to regulate businesses. While extensive advocacy efforts—385 events 
in 2022-23 and 365 in 2023-24—foster awareness, enforcement faces significant 
challenges, particularly in digital markets, where investigations are often stalled or 
orders are stayed by courts. Since its inception until January 10, 2025, the CCI has 
received 1,303 antitrust cases and 1,229 merger control notices, disposing of 1,168 
and 1,109 respectively. The average number of days to dispose of a combination 
notice was 17 in 2021-22, 21 in 2022-23, and 16 in 2023-24, highlighting the CCI's 
efficiency in merger control. 
The Committee observed that the CCI has taken significant enforcement 
actions against major digital players, imposing substantial penalties, such as Rs. 
135.86 crore in the Google search bias case and a provisional penalty of Rs. 1337.76 
crore in the Google Android matter. However, a detailed analysis of penalty realization 
reveals a significant challenge. While the CCI imposed Rs. 1,335.77 crore in penalties 
in FY 2021-22 (realizing Rs. 177.23 crore) and Rs. 2,672.48 crore in FY 2022-23 
(realizing Rs. 1,340.41 crore), a substantial portion of total imposed penalties has been 
effectively stalled by litigation. As of April 30, 2025, out of a total imposed penalty of 
Rs. 20,350.46 crore, a massive amount of Rs. 18,512.28 crore has been either stayed 
or dismissed by appellate courts. This makes the remaining amount of Rs. 1,838.19 
crore "realizable," of which the CCI has realized Rs. 1,823.57 crore, a high realization 
rate of 99.20%. This data indicates that while the CCI is effective at collecting penalties 
that are not under litigation, its overall enforcement is significantly undermined by legal 
challenges. The new provision mandating a 25% pre-deposit for appeals is a measure 
intended to address this problem. 
The Committee, therefore, recommended the CCI, in coordination with the 
Ministry of Corporate Affairs (MCA), should explore measures to reduce litigation 
delays and ensure the effective enforcement of its orders, particularly in complex 
digital market cases. This includes adopting robust legal defense strategies and 
continuously assessing the effectiveness of new provisions like the 25% pre-deposit 

7 
 
for appeals. Addressing these legal challenges is critical in ensuring that the CCI's 
enforcement actions translate into tangible deterrence and accountability. 
 
1.9 In their action taken reply, the Ministry of Corporate Affairs has furnished the 
following written submission: 
CCI is proactively defending litigations before various judicial fora, and, wherever necessary, filing caveats. CCI engages Senior Advocates of repute—including the Attorney General, Solicitor General, Additional Solicitor General(s), and other eminent Senior Counsels—to represent it in competition law matters before various fora/ courts, particularly in cases concerning the digital markets. CCI also takes proactive measures to seek early listing of cases, wherever warranted, through appropriate applications filed on a case-to-case basis. The Competition Act, 2002 was recently amended vide Competition (Amendment) Act 2023. These amendments inter alia introduced a provision for requirement for pre-deposit of 25% of the penalty imposed by the CCI prior to filing an appeal, with the objective of discouraging frivolous appeals or sham litigation intended to delay the payment of penalties imposed by the Commission for contraventions under the Act. The said provision came into force w.e.f. 18.05.2023. In this regard, no representations have been received in the Ministry from Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises (MSMEs)/ relatively small business with limited financial resources indicating that the pre-deposit requirement has caused undue hardship or has discouraged them from pursuing legitimate appeals. Based on the experience gained from the implementation of this provision, the Ministry will review the provision, if considered necessary. 
  

8 
 
1.10 The Committee note that while the 25% pre-deposit requirement was 
introduced to deter frivolous appeals and address the Rs.18,500 crore penalty 
realization gap, the Ministry of Corporate Affairs (MCA) has primarily maintained 
a reactive posture by relying on the absence of formal MSME grievances to 
justify the status quo. The Committee observe that under the Ease of Doing 
Business 2.0 and Business Reforms Action Plan (BRAP) 2025 frameworks, 
driven by Department for Promotion of Industry and International Trade (DPIIT) 
under the Ministry of Commerce and Industry, a lack of complaints often signals 
a "barrier to justice" rather than stakeholder satisfaction; for many MSMEs, the 
mandatory deposit may act as a prohibitive financial entry-fee that prevents 
them from even initiating an appeal. The Committee, therefore, recommend the 
Ministry to commission an independent impact study to ensure that the pursuit 
of deterrence does not inadvertently stifle the financial viability and "legal 
reach" of India’s small business ecosystem. Furthermore, the Ministry should 
collaborate with the Ministry of MSME to establish realistic tiered financial 
thresholds (e.g., 10% for Micro enterprises) to effectively distinguish between 
frivolous litigation by large tech platforms and genuine appeals by smaller 
entities currently blocked by capital constraints. While the pre-deposit 
addresses delayed realization, the Committee urge the Ministry to focus on the 
root cause by strengthening the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal 
(NCLAT) with dedicated competition benches to ensure that "stayed" penalties 
are adjudicated within a fixed statutory timeline.

Recommendation (Sl. No. 5) 
PROTECTION OF MSMES AND SMALL BUSINESSES 
 
1.11 The Committee noted that the digital revolution, while offering immense 
opportunities, also brings significant risks for Micro, Small, and Medium Enterprises 
(MSMEs). A key concern is the concentration of economic power in a few large 
technology platforms that act as intermediaries, which can stifle the growth and market 
access of smaller players. Practices like predatory pricing and deep discounting by 
major online platforms pose a direct threat to small retailers. The Committee observed 
that the Deal Value Threshold (DVT) of Rs. 2000 crore, introduced to capture strategic 
digital transactions, has raised concerns that it may allow large corporations to acquire 
MSMEs without regulatory scrutiny. The Ministry of Corporate Affairs (MCA) 
acknowledged these challenges and clarified that the CCI's enforcement actions are 
designed to protect MSMEs from anti-competitive practices. The CCI also conducts 
advocacy programs to help MSMEs understand their rights under the Competition Act 
and the concerns persisting regarding predatory pricing and deep discounting by e-
commerce platforms can be investigated under Section 3 or Section 4 of the 
Competition Act. The Ministry had emphasized that the ongoing market study will 
provide evidence-based solutions for balancing competition and innovation, especially 
for MSMEs and startups.  
 
The Committee, however, recommended that the Deal Value Threshold (DVT) 
of Rs. 2000 crore should be reviewed by the MCA and CCI. This reassessment is 
crucial to ensure the threshold does not inadvertently facilitate the acquisition of 
MSMEs by larger corporations without regulatory scrutiny, thereby preventing the 
creation of monopolies or duopolies that harm fair competition. A lower threshold for 
acquisitions involving MSMEs could be considered if market studies indicate so. 
Secondly, the CCI should continue its proactive investigations into predatory pricing 
and deep discounting by dominant online platforms to prevent these practices from 
harming small retailers. Specific guidelines could be developed to clarify when such 
practices become anti-competitive. Finally, mechanisms should be put in place to 

10 
 
ensure data access for smaller businesses, enabling them to compete effectively 
against large digital enterprises that control vast amounts of data. 
 
1.12 In their action taken reply, the Ministry of Corporate Affairs has furnished the 
following written submission: 
The Government of India has amended the Competition Act (Act) in 2023 and introduced a specific provision for Deal Value Threshold (DVT) as an additional notification criterion for regulation of M&A. It may be noted that that the existing thresholds for Deal Value Threshold i.e. DVT (of INR 2000 Crore) has been fixed and made applicable very recently (i.e. from 10th September 2024) after thorough deliberations, careful consideration, and stakeholder consultations subsequent to amendment in the Competition Act, 2002 [Competition (Amendment) Act, 2023].  Further, it is pertinent to mention that thresholds relating to assets/turnover for 5(a), 5 (b) and 5 (c) of the Competition Act, 2002 (Act) (as amended) have also been revised by the Ministry (MCA) in March 2024 in terms of provisions of Section 20(3) the Act. Section 20(3) of the Act reads,  “Notwithstanding anything contained in section 5, the Central Government shall, on the expiry of a period of two years from the date of commencement of this Act and thereafter every two years, in consultation with the Commission, enhance or reduce, by notification, or keep at the same level, on the basis of the wholesale price index or fluctuations is exchange rate of rupee or foreign currencies, or such factors that in its opinion are relevant in this matter, the value of assets or the value of turnover or value of transaction, for the purposes of that section.”  Furthermore, it is worth mentioning that the Act empowers the Central Government in consultation with the Commission, to enhance or reduce, by notification, or keep the thresholds including deal value, at the same level, on the basis of the wholesale price index or fluctuations is exchange rate of rupee or foreign currencies, or such factors that in its opinion are relevant in this matter.  

11 
 
A Market Study on E-commerce in India was conducted by CCI in 2019-20 which inter alia covered e-commerce in consumer goods. The study report presented the issues that may, directly or indirectly, have a bearing on competition, or may hinder realisation of the full pro-competitive potential of e-commerce. These included the issues of lack of platform neutrality, unfair platform-to-business contract terms, exclusive contracts between online marketplace platforms and sellers/service providers, platform price parity restrictions and deep discounts.   On the basis of the market study findings, the Commission recommended the e-commerce platforms to put in place transparency measures in the areas of search ranking, collection, use and sharing of data, user review and rating mechanism, revision in contract terms, and discount policy with a view to address concerns while maintaining the innovative potential of the sector.  Further, under its enforcement mandate, in cases of contravention of the Competition Act, 2002, the Commission directs appropriate remedies including those relating to data to address competition concerns.    In this regard, the Ministry has further clarified as under:-  
As reflected in the Preamble and Section 18 of the Competition Act, 2002 (the 
Act), the Competition Commission of India (the Commission) is entrusted with the 
mandate to prevent practices having an adverse effect on competition, promote and 
sustain competition in markets, protect the interests of consumers, and ensure 
freedom of trade carried on by other participants in markets in India. In furtherance of 
this statutory mandate, the Commission is, inter alia, responsible for the regulation of 
combinations—namely, acquisitions, mergers, and amalgamations—that meet the 
jurisdictional thresholds prescribed under the Act. 
The objective of merger control under the Act is to ensure that transactions 
likely to cause an appreciable adverse effect on competition in the relevant market are 
identified and addressed at the appropriate stage. The appreciable adverse effect on 
competition standard focuses on transactions that are likely to result in appreciable 

12 
 
adverse impact on the competitive structure of a market, as opposed to transactions 
having only marginal or inconsequential competitive effects. 
Pursuant to amendments to the Act, the Central Government notified the Deal 
Value Threshold (DVT) of ₹2,000 crore with effect from 10 September 2024, capturing 
certain transactions not meeting traditional asset or turnover-based thresholds. Since 
its operationalisation, thirty-nine combination notices have been filed under the DVT 
route. Examination of these transactions indicates that none raised competition 
concerns or posed an appreciable adverse effect on competition. Only a limited 
number of notified transactions pertained to the digital sector, for which the DVT was 
primarily intended. 
In this context, a further reduction of the extant thresholds, with the objective of 
bringing transactions involving MSMEs within the ambit of mandatory merger review, 
may not serve the intended purpose of merger control under the Act. Such a reduction 
is likely to result in a significant increase in the number of notifiable transactions 
involving enterprises of limited scale and market presence, operating predominantly 
in fragmented and non-concentrated markets, where the likelihood of competition 
concerns is minimal. This could also impose additional compliance and transactional 
costs on MSMEs and other small businesses. 
Accordingly, from a competition enforcement perspective, further lowering of 
thresholds is unlikely to meaningfully enhance the objectives of the Act. Merger control 
is designed to address structural competition concerns at the market level rather than 
to serve as a general protective mechanism for specific categories of enterprises. 
Issues relating to the protection, promotion, and safeguarding of MSME interests are 
more appropriately addressed through targeted policy instruments and sector-specific 
measures administered by the Ministry of MSME and other relevant authorities, rather 
than through the merger control framework under the Competition Act. 
  

13 
 
1.13 The Committee note that the digital revolution has introduced systemic 
risks for MSMEs, primarily through the concentration of economic power among 
a few "Systemically Significant Digital Enterprises" (SSDEs) that act as market 
gatekeepers. While the Deal Value Threshold (DVT) of Rs.2,000 crore (operative 
since September 2024) was intended to capture strategic "killer acquisitions," 
the Ministry of Corporate Affairs’ reactive reliance on the absence of formal 
grievances may overlook a systemic "barrier to entry" for innovative startups. 
The Committee observe that a "one-size-fits-all" DVT may fail to protect the 
digital ecosystem from gradual monopolization, as dominant platforms continue 
to leverage "invisible" market distortions—such as data silos and algorithmic 
self-preferencing—that are not currently addressed by a codified regulatory 
framework. The Committee, therefore, while reiterating their recommendation 
urge that the MCA and CCI must transition to a risk-based oversight model by 
initiating a sector-specific review of DVT levels, particularly for acquisitions in 
high-innovation and high-data-concentration markets to protect the interests of 
MSMEs. Simultaneously, the CCI should notify a formal E-commerce Code of 
Conduct to be administered by the Commission, which explicitly mandates 
platform neutrality, algorithmic transparency, and non-discriminatory data 
access for smaller business users. To ensure these protections are enforceable, 
the existing Digital Market Division (DMD) should be fully integrated into this 
framework to serve as a technical and procedural facilitator; by leveraging its 
algorithmic auditors to provide evidentiary support and overseeing mandatory 
mediation mechanisms, the DMD can bridge the technical asymmetry between 
small businesses and dominant platforms in line with global benchmarks like 
the EU’s Platform-to Business (P2B) Regulation.   

14 
 
1.14 The Committee further note that the Ministry, in its written reply, has 
remained silent on the recommendation regarding the development of specific 
guidelines to clarify when practices such as predatory pricing and deep 
discounting become anti-competitive and mechanisms should be put in place 
to ensure data access for smaller businesses, enabling them to compete 
effectively against large digital enterprises that control vast amounts of data. 
The Committee also take note of the growing concerns regarding the adverse 
impact of deep discounting, opaque pricing practices, the concentration of 
market power in a few dominant digital platforms, leading to the marginalisation 
of small retailers and weakening of local kirana ecosystems. The Committee are 
of the considered view that, in the absence of clear and well-defined regulatory 
benchmarks, such practices may persist without timely scrutiny, thereby 
undermining fair competition and limiting consumer choice. In this context, the 
Committee view the Ministry’s omission on this aspect with concern and, 
therefore, reiterate their recommendation and urge the Ministry to expeditiously 
develop specific guidelines to clearly delineate when such practices become 
anti-competitive, along with appropriate mechanisms to ensure equitable data 
access for smaller businesses, so as to promote certainty, transparency, and 
effective enforcement in digital markets.

Reply of the Government

EVOLUTION OF THE REGULATORY FRAMEWORK: EX-POST AND EX-ANTE 
 
The Committee note that the Competition Act, 2002, with its ex-post framework, 
is ill-equipped to handle the rapid concentration of power in digital markets. 
Recognizing this limitation, the Committee on Digital Competition Law (CDCL) 
proposed a separate ex-ante regulatory mechanism through a Draft Bill on Digital 
Competition Law (DCB).This framework aims to prevent anti-competitive conduct 
before it harms consumer interests, specifically targeting Systemically Significant 
Digital Enterprises (SSDEs) based on quantitative and qualitative criteria.The DCB, 
which is currently under public consultation, identifies nine Core Digital Services and 
imposes obligations on SSDEs to prevent anti-competitive practices.This move aligns 
with global trends, as seen in the EU's Digital Markets Act (DMA) and the UK's Digital 
Markets, Competition and Consumers (DMCC) Act 2024.However, stakeholders have 
raised concerns about the DCB's proposed thresholds potentially capturing Indian 
companies prematurely, the absence of a rebuttal mechanism for SSDE designation, 
and potential overlaps with existing laws like the DPDP Act. 
 
The Committee observe that while the DCB and the EU's DMA share the 
objective of regulating large digital enterprises, their approaches differ significantly. 
The DCB covers 9 Core Digital Services (CDS), one less than the DMA, and uses 
more inclusive definitions. A key difference is the absence of a rebuttal mechanism in 
the DCB, which is a feature of the DMA. Furthermore, the DCB's thresholds for SSDE 
designation are based on a "spread test" using either end-user or business-user 
thresholds, unlike the DMA which requires both. The DCB also grants the CCI flexibility 
to specify distinct conduct obligations for each CDS through subordinate legislation, a 
difference from the DMA's direct statutory obligations. To address stakeholder 
concerns, the Committee recommend that the Ministry refine the DCB's thresholds 
and designation mechanisms to prevent inadvertent capturing of fast-growing 
domestic firms. The Committee also recommend the introduction of a rebuttal 
mechanism in exceptional cases, for SSDE designation, similar to the EU's DMA, to 
ensure fairness and provide regulatory certainty.

ENFORCEMENT EFFECTIVENESS AND LITIGATION CHALLENGES 
The Committee note that the CCI employs a dual approach of enforcement and 
advocacy to regulate businesses. While extensive advocacy efforts—385 events in 
2022-23 and 365 in 2023-24—foster awareness, enforcement faces significant 
challenges, particularly in digital markets, where investigations are often stalled or 
orders are stayed by courts. Since its inception until January 10, 2025, the CCI has 
received 1,303 antitrust cases and 1,229 merger control notices, disposing of 1,168 
and 1,109 respectively. The average number of days to dispose of a combination 
notice was 17 in 2021-22, 21 in 2022-23, and 16 in 2023-24, highlighting the CCI's 
efficiency in merger control. 
The Committee observe that the CCI has taken significant enforcement actions 
against major digital players, imposing substantial penalties, such as Rs. 135.86 crore 
in the Google search bias case and a provisional penalty of Rs. 1337.76 crore in the 
Google Android matter. However, a detailed analysis of penalty realization reveals a 
significant challenge. While the CCI imposed Rs. 1,335.77 crore in penalties in FY 
2021-22 (realizing Rs. 177.23 crore) and Rs. 2,672.48 crore in FY 2022-23 (realizing 
Rs. 1,340.41 crore), a substantial portion of total imposed penalties has been 
effectively stalled by litigation. As of April 30, 2025, out of a total imposed penalty of 

24 
 
Rs. 20,350.46 crore, a massive amount of Rs. 18,512.28 crore has been either stayed 
or dismissed by appellate courts. This makes the remaining amount of Rs. 1,838.19 
crore "realizable," of which the CCI has realized Rs. 1,823.57 crore, a high realization 
rate of 99.20%. This data indicates that while the CCI is effective at collecting penalties 
that are not under litigation, its overall enforcement is significantly undermined by legal 
challenges. The new provision mandating a 25% pre-deposit for appeals is a measure 
intended to address this problem. 
The Committee, therefore, recommend the CCI, in coordination with the 
Ministry of Corporate Affairs (MCA), should explore measures to reduce litigation 
delays and ensure the effective enforcement of its orders, particularly in complex 
digital market cases. This includes adopting robust legal defense strategies and 
continuously assessing the effectiveness of new provisions like the 25% pre-deposit 
for appeals. Addressing these legal challenges is critical in ensuring that the CCI's 
enforcement actions translate into tangible deterrence and accountability.

CROSS-JURISDICTIONAL AND INTER-REGULATORY COORDINATION 
 
The Committee note that digital markets transcend national borders, requiring 
the CCI to collaborate with international competition authorities to address anti-
competitive practices that originate abroad but have local effects. Furthermore, the 
digital landscape involves overlapping issues, such as privacy and data protection, 
which fall under the purview of various sectoral regulators. Effective coordination 
between the CCI and these other regulatory bodies is crucial to ensure a 
comprehensive and coherent approach, avoiding regulatory chaos and meeting both 
competition and sectoral objectives. 
 
The Committee observe that the Competition Act, 2002, empowers the CCI to 
inquire into acts outside India that have an appreciable adverse effect on competition 
within the country (Section 32). The CCI has actively strengthened its international ties 
by signing 10 bilateral/multilateral Memoranda of Understanding (MoUs) with 
competition authorities from countries like Egypt, Mauritius, Japan, and Brazil, as well 
as with BRICS nations, the EU, and the US. The CCI also participates in multilateral 
forums like the ICN and OECD, becoming an ICN Steering Group member in October 
2023. Domestically, the Act provides a framework for inter-regulatory consultation 
through Sections 21 and 21A, and a proviso to Section 18, introduced in 2023, 
explicitly allows for MoUs with other statutory authorities. The CCI actively consults 

26 
 
with regulators like TRAI and SEBI. Global best practices, such as the UK's Digital 
Regulation Cooperation Forum, also emphasize the importance of cooperation 
mechanisms to overcome jurisdictional overlaps. 
 
In view of the above, the Committee would like to recommend that, firstly, the 
CCI and the MCA should prioritize enhancing inter-regulatory harmony. This involves 
proactively collaborating with other sectoral regulators, such as the Data Protection 
Authority and MeitY, to identify and address jurisdictional overlaps. To achieve this, 
they may formalize Memoranda of Understanding (MoUs) and establish clear 
protocols for information sharing and joint action. Secondly, to strengthen its 
international cooperation, the CCI should continue to deepen its engagement with 
global competition authorities through bilateral MoUs and multilateral forums. Such 
collaboration is vital for sharing enforcement experiences, aligning regulatory 
strategies, and effectively tackling the global anti-competitive practices of digital 
platforms. Foreign Jurisdictions are attempting to undercut regulatory oversight and 
remits by unilaterally trying to coerce regulations in other countries not to discharge 
their statutory responsibility. The CCI must be vigilant and mindful of such efforts.

CONCLUSION 
 
The Competition Commission of India (CCI) has been effective in promoting fair 
competition, even in the complex digital sector. The CCI has adopted new tools such 
as the Deal Value Threshold (DVT) and Settlement and Commitment Mechanisms to 
address emerging challenges. However, the CCI continues to face issues like 
technological complexity, cross-jurisdictional matters, and a lack of resources, 
including staffing and funding. To combat these, the CCI has initiated measures like 
the Digital Markets Division (DMD) and is proposing cadre restructuring. 
The Committee are of the strong view that while the inclusion of new provisions 
in the Digital Competition Bill (DCB) is vital for fostering fair competition and aligning 

30 
 
with global standards, its implementation must be carefully managed. The approach 
should be nuanced, phased, and evidence-based, taking into account the CCI's 
current capacity constraints. It is also crucial to address concerns from stakeholders 
regarding the potential impact on innovation and the compliance burden for emerging 
Indian tech companies and MSMEs.  By proactively addressing the challenges like 
structural dominance of super-apps, need for interoperability, regulating AI-based user 
interfaces etc by regular dialogue with the stakeholders and conducting studies of 
emerging issues the CCI can establish itself as an agile and effective regulator, 
capable of promoting a competitive, innovative, and inclusive digital landscape that 
benefits all stakeholders in India.

Further, the recommendation of the Committee has been duly noted and will be

PROTECTION OF MSMES AND SMALL BUSINESSES 
 
 The Committee note that the digital revolution, while offering immense 
opportunities, also brings significant risks for Micro, Small, and Medium Enterprises 
(MSMEs). A key concern is the concentration of economic power in a few large 
technology platforms that act as intermediaries, which can stifle the growth and market 
access of smaller players. Practices like predatory pricing and deep discounting by 
major online platforms pose a direct threat to small retailers. The Committee observes 
that the Deal Value Threshold (DVT) of Rs. 2000 crore, introduced to capture strategic 
digital transactions, has raised concerns that it may allow large corporations to acquire 
MSMEs without regulatory scrutiny. The Ministry of Corporate Affairs (MCA) 
acknowledged these challenges and clarified that the CCI's enforcement actions are 
designed to protect MSMEs from anti-competitive practices. The CCI also conducts 
advocacy programs to help MSMEs understand their rights under the Competition Act 
and the concerns persisting regarding predatory pricing and deep discounting by e-
commerce platforms can be investigated under Section 3 or Section 4 of the 
Competition Act. The Ministry has emphasized that the ongoing market study will 
provide evidence-based solutions for balancing competition and innovation, especially 
for MSMEs and startups.  
 
The Committee, however, recommend that the Deal Value Threshold (DVT) of                   
Rs. 2000 crore should be reviewed by the MCA and CCI. This reassessment is crucial 
to ensure the threshold does not inadvertently facilitate the acquisition of MSMEs by 
larger corporations without regulatory scrutiny, thereby preventing the creation of 
monopolies or duopolies that harm fair competition. A lower threshold for acquisitions 

33 
 
involving MSMEs could be considered if market studies indicate so. Secondly, the CCI 
should continue its proactive investigations into predatory pricing and deep 
discounting by dominant online platforms to prevent these practices from harming 
small retailers. Specific guidelines could be developed to clarify when such practices 
become anti-competitive. Finally, mechanisms should be put in place to ensure data 
access for smaller businesses, enabling them to compete effectively against large 
digital enterprises that control vast amounts of data.

The study report presented the issues that may, directly or indirectly, have a bearing on competition, or may hinder realisation of the full pro-competitive potential of e-commerce. These included the issues of lack of platform neutrality, unfair platform-to-business contract terms, exclusive contracts between online marketplace platforms and sellers/service providers, platform price parity restrictions and deep discounts.   On the basis of the market study findings, the Commission recommended the e-commerce platforms to put in place transparency measures in the areas of search ranking, collection, use and sharing of data, user review and rating mechanism, revision in contract terms, and discount policy with a view to address concerns while maintaining the innovative potential of the sector.  Further, under its enforcement mandate, in cases of contravention of the Competition Act, 2002, the Commission directs appropriate remedies including those relating to data to address competition concerns.    In this regard, the Ministry has further clarified as under:-  
As reflected in the Preamble and Section 18 of the Competition Act, 2002 (the 
Act), the Competition Commission of India (the Commission) is entrusted with the 
mandate to prevent practices having an adverse effect on competition, promote and 
sustain competition in markets, protect the interests of consumers, and ensure 
freedom of trade carried on by other participants in markets in India. In furtherance of 
this statutory mandate, the Commission is, inter alia, responsible for the regulation of 

35 
 
combinations—namely, acquisitions, mergers, and amalgamations—that meet the 
jurisdictional thresholds prescribed under the Act. 
The objective of merger control under the Act is to ensure that transactions 
likely to cause an appreciable adverse effect on competition in the relevant market are 
identified and addressed at the appropriate stage. The appreciable adverse effect on 
competition standard focuses on transactions that are likely to result in appreciable 
adverse impact on the competitive structure of a market, as opposed to transactions 
having only marginal or inconsequential competitive effects. 
Pursuant to amendments to the Act, the Central Government notified the Deal 
Value Threshold (DVT) of ₹2,000 crore with effect from 10 September 2024, capturing 
certain transactions not meeting traditional asset or turnover-based thresholds. Since 
its operationalisation, thirty-nine combination notices have been filed under the DVT 
route. Examination of these transactions indicates that none raised competition 
concerns or posed an appreciable adverse effect on competition. Only a limited 
number of notified transactions pertained to the digital sector, for which the DVT was 
primarily intended. 
In this context, a further reduction of the extant thresholds, with the objective of 
bringing transactions involving MSMEs within the ambit of mandatory merger review, 
may not serve the intended purpose of merger control under the Act. Such a reduction 
is likely to result in a significant increase in the number of notifiable transactions 
involving enterprises of limited scale and market presence, operating predominantly 
in fragmented and non-concentrated markets, where the likelihood of competition 
concerns is minimal. This could also impose additional compliance and transactional 
costs on MSMEs and other small businesses. 
Accordingly, from a competition enforcement perspective, further lowering of 
thresholds is unlikely to meaningfully enhance the objectives of the Act. Merger control 
is designed to address structural competition concerns at the market level rather than 
to serve as a general protective mechanism for specific categories of enterprises. 
Issues relating to the protection, promotion, and safeguarding of MSME interests are 
more appropriately addressed through targeted policy instruments and sector-specific 

36 
 
measures administered by the Ministry of MSME and other relevant authorities, rather 
than through the merger control framework under the Competition Act.
