1.2 Institutional Credit flow to agriculture has received major focus
of the policy makers after independence. Yet, institutional agricultural
credit disbursement could not substitute informal sources of credit
totally. An overwhelming majority of farmers are still dependent on
informal sources for meeting their credit needs to a large extent.

(ii) Expert Groups and Committees on Agricultural Credit
1.5 Various Committees and Expert Groups have been appointed
from time to time to examine institutional credit availability of farmers
and suggest measures to include more and more farmers in the
mainstream of institutional credit, prominent among them being All
India Rural Credit Review Committee, 1966, High level Committee on
agricultural Credit, 1997, Expert Committee on Rural credit, 2000,
National Commission on Farmers, 2004 and Expert Group on
Agricultural Indebtedness, 2007. They have made in-depth analysis of
the problems of rural credit and recommended measures to rectify
them. However, the preponderance of non-institutional credit still
persists causing perpetual indebtedness of farmers.

3
(a)  Expert Group on Agricultural indebtedness
1.6 The Government, in August, 2006 constituted an Expert Group
under the Chairmanship of Prof. R. Radha Krishna to look into the
problems of Agricultural indebtedness in its totality and suggest
measures to provide relief to farmers across the Country. The Expert
Group, which submitted its report in July, 2007 examined, inter alia ,
the sources of credit and found that 40 per cent of the loans taken by
farmers came from money lenders, traders and such other non-
institutional sources which charged interest rates of over 30 per cent.
The Expert Group, therefore, felt that there was a need to relieve the
farmers from private debt carrying high interest rate by transferring it
to institutional agencies. The Group also found that farmers in States
which recorded the highest rates of suicide borrowed money for
productive purposes i.e. to buy seeds, fertilizers, pesticides etc.
Therefore, the Group said that policy of debt relief must be flexible to
take into account regional variations and requirements.
1.7 Other credit measures suggested by this Group include,
re-schedulement of loans to farmers and waiver of interest in case of
second consecutive failure. It has suggested strengthening of credit
counselling mechanism, simplification of procedures of mortgage,
computerization of land records, strengthening the resource base of
NABARD, promotion of farmers’ self-help groups and federations, risk
mitigation measures, such as, strengthening of National Agricultural
Insurance Scheme, creation of a Price Risk Mitigation Fund, introduction
of Weather Insurance, use of Satellite Imagery for Crop Surveillance
and promotion of off-farm employment. The major issues relating to
agricultural credit identified by the Expert Group on Agricultural
indebtedness include, poor performance of credit cooperatives and
regional rural banks, inability of commercial banks to meet targets for
agricultural lending and high cost of rural banking.
(b) Technical Group to review legislations and money lending
1.8 In May, 2006, the Reserve Bank constituted a Technical Group
under the Chairmanship of Shri S.C. Gupta to review the efficacy of
the existing legislative framework that governs money lending as well
as the enforcement machinery in different states and make
recommendations for its improvement. The technical group, in their
report recommended a model legislation to regulate money lending,
fixing maximum interest rate and registration of money lenders. It
also proposed appointing individuals or accredited loan providers who
would lend funds to borrowers in competition with money lenders,
preventing them from charging arbitrary interest rates and treating the

4
advances made by institutional creditors to accredited loan providers
as priority sector lending.
(c) Committee on Financial Inclusion
1.9 The Committee on Financial Inclusion, chaired by
Dr. C. Rangarajan, which submitted its report in January, 2008 observed,
inter alia :
“NSSO data (59th Round) reveal that 45.9 million farmer
households in the country (51.4%), out of a total of 89.3 million
households do not access credit, either from institutional or non-
institutional sources. Further, despite the vast network of bank
branches, only 27% of total farm households are indebted to formal
sources (of which one-third also borrow from informal sources).”
1.10 The Rangarajan Committee has also indicated that 64.95
million cultivator households and 46.6 million non-cultivator households
(agricultural labourers, artisans and others) do not have access to formal
financial services. According to the Committee on Financial Inclusion,
financial inclusion must be taken up in a mission mode as a National
Rural Financial Inclusion Plan (NRFIP). The target for NRFIP could be
to provide access to comprehensive financial services to at least 50%
(55.77 million) of the excluded rural cultivator and non-cultivator
households, across different States by 2012 through rural/semi urban
branches of Commercial Banks and RRBs. The remaining households,
with such shifts as may occur in the rural/urban population, have to
be covered by 2015.
(iii) Measures to improve flow of credit
1.11 As indicated in a written note furnished to the Committee by
the Ministry of Finance (Department of Financial Services), the measures
taken by the Government of India and RBI in the recent period for
improving flow of credit to agriculture include:-
(a) As recommended by the Advisory Committee on Flow of
Credit to Agriculture and Related Activities from the Banking
System (Vyas Committee), in order to improve the flow of
credit to small and marginal farmers (which form a part of
the weaker sections), the Public Sector Banks (PSBs) were
advised in 2004 to make efforts to increase their
disbursements to small and marginal farmers to 40 per cent
of their direct disbursements under the Special Agricultural
Credit Plan (SACP) by March 2007. The PSBs have disbursed

5
Rs. 40,965 crore during 2006-07, which works out to 44.68%
of direct credit under SACP .
(b) With a view to bringing distressed persons into the fold of
formal financial system, banks have been advised that they
may grant loans to distressed persons to prepay their debt
to non-institutional lenders, against appropriate collateral or
group security . Such loans are eligible to be classified under
Weaker Section category within the priority sector.
(c) One Time Settlement (OTS) Scheme for Small and Marginal
Farmers:
Banks were advised to formulate guidelines, with the
approval of their Boards of Directors, on one-time settlement
for small and marginal farmers, who have been declared as
defaulters and have become ineligible for fresh credit. On
settlement, these farmers would become eligible for fresh
finance.
(d) As a part of monitoring/review mechanism of the
performance of banks under Government Sponsored
Schemes, RBI has been advising all Scheduled Commercial
Banks to implement the schemes in right earnest and achieve
the targets set under the schemes.”
1.12 Other agricultural credit measures initiated include:
(a) Farm credit package announced on 18.6.2004
As per the Government of India package announced on 18th June,
2004, credit flow to agriculture sector had to be doubled over the
position of 2003-04 in next three years viz. 2004-05, 2005-06 and
2006-07. The credit flow got doubled in a period of two years. The
package also stipulated relief to farmers in distress by restructuring
loans to farmers who suffered production and income losses on account
of natural calamities for two successive years, relief to farmers in arrears
by restructuring of loans to farmers whose dues to the banking system
were in arrears and who became ineligible for fresh credit and One
Time Settlement for small and marginal farmers to settle their NPA
accounts. To mitigate the distress, farmers face due to the heavy debt
from non-institutional lenders (e.g. moneylenders), banks were enabled,
subject to the guidelines to be approved by their Boards of Directors,
to advance loans to such farmers, against appropriate collateral or
group security. Under this Scheme, the banks have provided Rs. 307.30
crore (data provisional) of credit to farmers against 71,103 accounts.

6
From the year 2008-09, banks have been advised to earmark around
3% of the target for agriculture credit in 2008-09 for giving loans under
the Debt Swap Scheme.
(b) Special package relating to Agriculture Credit for Debt Stressed
Districts
Keeping in view the conditions of the farmers in the 31 districts
in the four States of Maharashtra (06), Andhra Pradesh (16), Karnataka
(06) and Kerala (03), where a large number of farmer suicides had
been reported, a Relief Package to mitigate the distress of farmers has
been implemented. The package involved waiver of the entire interest
on overdue loans as on 1.7.2006, reschedulement of these overdue
loans over a period of 3-5 years with a one year moratorium and
fresh credit flow to these districts. The burden of waiver of overdue
interest was shared equally by the State Governments (50%) and by
the Central Government (50%). The share of Government of India is
about Rs. 1814 crore.
(c) Interest Subvention to farmers
As per the announcement in the Union Budget 2006-07,
Government is providing interest subvention of 2% per annum to Public
Sector Banks, Regional Rural Banks and Cooperative Banks on their

Regional Rural Banks. Under this Scheme, farmers are able to get
short term credit at 7% rate of interest with an upper limit of Rs. 3
lakhs on the principal amount since 2006-07. The scheme of interest
subvention was continued in 2007-08. The estimated commitments for
2006-07 and 2007-08 are Rs. 1856 crore and Rs. 2472 crore respectively.
(d) Announcements in the Budget 2008-09 relating to agricultural

during 2008-09.
(ii) Interest subvention scheme has been continued for the year
2008-09 also.
(iii) Budget 2008-09 envisages constitution of a National Rural

2. SHORTFALL IN AGRICULTURAL LENDING BY BANKS
2.1 The need to channelise the flow of credit to certain sectors of
the economy, known as the priority sector was first expressed in RBI’s
credit policy for the year 1967-68. The description of the priority sectors
was formalized in 1972 and in November, 1974, public sector banks
were advised that their priority sector lending should reach a level of
not less than one-third of the outstanding credit by March 1979.
Similarly, in November, 1978, private sector banks were also advised
to lend a minimum of 33
1/3 per cent of their total advances to priority
sector by the end of March, 1980. Subsequently , the target of 40 per
cent for priority sector was fixed in which sub targets of 18 and 10
per cent for agriculture and weaker sections were stipulated.
2.2 RBI has mandated domestic scheduled commercial banks (both
in public and private sector) to provide 18 per cent of Adjusted Net
Bank Credit (ANBC) to agriculture sector, of which 13.5 per cent is to
be disbursed as direct credit and 4.5 per cent towards indirect credit
to agriculture.
2.3 Direct Agricultural advances refer to advances given by banks
directly to farmers for agricultural purposes, such as crop loans. In
addition, it also includes following advances:
(i) Advances upto Rs. 10 lakh to farmers against pledge/
hypothecation of agricultural produce (including warehouse
receipts) for a period not exceeding 12 months irrespective
of whether, where the farmers were given crop loans for
raising the produce or not.
(ii) Medium and long-term loans (provided directly to farmers
for financing production and development needs) such as
Purchase of agricultural implements and machinery ,
Development of irrigation potential, Reclamation and Land
Development Schemes, Construction of farm buildings and
structures, etc.
(iii) Loans to plantations, development of allied activities such
as fishery, poultry etc.
(iv) Establishment of bio-gas plants.
(v) Purchase of land for agricultural purposes by small and
marginal farmers.
(vi) Loans to agri-clinics and agri-business centres.

10
2.4 Indirect agricultural finance on the other hand covers advances
provided by banks to farmers indirectly, i.e., through other agencies.
Important items included under indirect finance to agriculture are as
under :
(i) Credit for financing the distribution of fertilisers, pesticides,
seeds, etc.
(ii) Loans upto Rs. 25 lakhs granted for financing distribution
of inputs for the allied activities such as, cattle feed, poultry
feed, etc.
(iii) Loans to Electricity Boards for reimbursing the expenditure
already incurred by them for providing low tension
connection from step-down point to individual farmers for
energising their wells.
(iv) Loans to State Electricity Boards for Systems Improvement
Scheme under Special Project Agriculture (SI-SPA).
(v) Deposits held by the banks in Rural Infrastructure
Development Fund (RIDF) maintained with NABARD.
(vi) Subscription to bonds issued by Rural Electrification
Corporation (REC) exclusively for financing pump-set
energisation programme in rural and semi-urban areas and
also for financing System Improvement Programme (SI-SPA).
(vii) Subscriptions to bonds issued by NABARD with the
objective of financing agriculture/allied activities.
(viii) Finance extended to dealers in drip irrigation/sprinkler
irrigation system/agricultural machinery, subject to the
following conditions:
(a) The dealer should be located in the rural/semi-urban
areas.
(b) He should be dealing exclusively in such items or if
dealing in other products, should be maintaining
separate and distinct records in respect of such items.
(c) A ceiling of upto Rs. 20 lakhs per dealer should be
observed.
(ix) Loans to Arthias (commission agents in rural/semi-urban
areas) for meeting their working capital requirements on
account of credit extended to farmers for supply of inputs.
(x) Lending to Non-Banking Financial Companies (NBFCs) for
on-lending to agriculture.

3. RURAL INFRASTRUCTURE DEVELOPMENT FUND
3.1 Rural Infrastructure Development Fund (RIDF) was set up with
NABARD under the initiative of the Central Government in 1995-96
to provide loans to the State Governments for financing rural
infrastructure projects. Since then, fourteen tranches of allocations have
been made towards the fund. Commercial banks make contributions
to the fund to the extent of shortfalls in their agriculture and/or priority
sector lending. Since 1999-2000, the scope of RIDF has been widened
to enable utilisation of loans by Panchayat Raj institutions (PRIs), Self-
Help Groups (SHGs), and Non Government Organisations (NGOs),
among others. In pursuance to the announcement made by the Finance
Minister, a separate window was opened under RIDF XII for funding
rural road component of Bharat Nirman Programme with an allocation
of Rs.4,000 crore during 2006-07. At present, the system of allocation
under RIDF to banks as revised by RBI since 2nd July, 2007 is as
follows:
“50% of the corpus shall be allocated among the domestic
commercial banks having shortfall in lending to priority sector
target of 40% of ANBC or credit equivalent amount of Off-Balance
Sheet Exposure, whichever, is higher, on a pro-rota basis. The
balance 50% of the corpus shall be allocated among the banks
having shortfall in lending to agriculture target of 18% of ANBC
or credit equivalent amount of Off-Balance Sheet Exposure,
whichever, is higher, on a pro-rota basis. The amount of
contribution by banks to a particular tranche of RIDF will be
decided in the beginning of the financial year”.
3.2 Presently, the rate of interest payable to banks on RIDF deposits
ranges from 3 to 6%.
3.3 Questioned about the criteria followed for determining the
extent of contribution and details of the tranche-wise deposits made
to RIDF by scheduled commercial banks, the Ministry of Finance
(Department of Financial Services) have, in a written reply, furnished
the following information:
“The corpus of a particular tranche of RIDF is decided by
Government of India every year.
Further, the bank-wise allocation of RIDF is decided by RBI based
on the shortfall in the lending to the priority sector and/or

14
agriculture sector by the Domestic Scheduled Commercial Banks,
in the public as well as private sector, as on the last reporting
Friday of March, of the preceding year. The percentage of shortfall

4. INTEREST RATE ON AGRICULTURAL CREDIT
4.1 From kharif 2006-07, it was decided that the farmers would
receive crop loans up to a principal amount of Rs. 3 lakh at 7 per cent
rate of interest and the Government of India would provide necessary
interest subvention to NABARD and banks for this purpose. This policy
has been continued in 2007-08 also, for which the Government has
made a provision of Rs. 1,677 crore in the Union Budget 2007-08.
4.2 The Committee had, in their report on the Demands for Grants
(2007-08) of the Ministry of Finance (Departments of Economic Affairs,
Expenditure and Disinvestment) observed that though the scheme of
providing interest subvention to banks to the extent of 2 percent was
formulated to enable extending short-term production credit to farmers
at 7 percent interest rate, the requirements of the farming community
demanded an affordable rate of the interest. The Committee, therefore,
desired that the suggestions of the National Commission on Farmers,
which included, reducing the rate of interest on crop loans, were acted
upon so as to enable the farmers to access institutional credit on large
scale. The Committee also recommended that the Government/RBI
should initiate steps to further reduce the rate of interest on long term
agricultural loans as well, so as to make it affordable for farmers.
4.3 In response to the observations of the Committee on the interest
rate applicable on agricultural credit, the action taken note furnished
by the Ministry of Finance (Department of Financial Services) states as
under:—
“Banks fix the rates of interest taking into account their cost of
fund, transaction cost and risk. With the deregulation of interest
rates by the Reserve Bank of India (RBI), Banks are free to fix the
rate of interest on loans above Rs. 2 lakh provided by them to
farmers. However, interest rates for loans up to Rs. 2 lakh should
not exceed the Benchmark Prime Lending Rate (BPLR) in order to
benefit small borrowers including small and marginal farmers.
Banks have been providing crop loans to the farmers @7% with
an upper limit of Rs. 3 lakh on the principal amount w.e.f. Kharif
2006-07. This has happened on account of the interest subvention
provided by the Government to Public Sector Banks, Cooperative
Banks and Regional Rural Banks (RRBs) on the involvement of
their own funds and concessional refinance to RRBs and
Cooperative banks through NABARD…
The loans provided for investment purposes such as farm
mechanization, equipments etc. are for longer periods and are
related to the size of the project. The rates of interest charged on

21
such investment loans are related to cost of borrowings, transaction
and risk cost of the lending bank. Any reduction in the rate of
interest for such purposes as in the case of crop loans may not be
financially viable for the lending banks. Bank are taking steps to
reduce their transaction costs through better use of information
Technology, Banking Correspondents/Business Facilitaters models,
etc. Risk premium can come down by introduction of appropriate
insurance products, positive credit information from credit bureaus,
better market facilities, proposed credit guarantee scheme for the
distressed farmers, etc. With these measures, the rates of interest
are likely to come down”.
4.4 The National Commission on Farmers, in their final report,
has recommended as follows on the issue of interest rate on agriculture
credit:—
“The need is to improve the outreach and efficiency of the rural
banking system. The financial services must reach all its users
effectively; the credit must be in time, in required quantities and
at appropriate interest rate. The interest rate should be as low as
possible. It should be possible to bring about a reduction in
transaction cost by eliminating all forms of waste and inefficiency
in the operation of the banking system. The inefficiencies of the
delivery system should not be loaded on the interest charged. The
delivery system has to be proactive and should respond to the
financial needs of clients in the rural areas. The banking system
needs to explore the large unmet credit potential needed to raise
agriculture to higher thresholds, and for the growth of rural and
agri-business enterprises and employment.
The State has a responsibility towards improving the credit
absorptive capacity of the farmers and to support the banking
system by creating favourable environment for expanding and
deepening of financial services.
NABARD as the leader of agriculture and rural credit should
ensure, convergence between credit availability and credit
absorptive capacity of the farmers and other rural borrowers, and
an efficient credit delivery system. NABARD should actively involve
itself in institution building and provide backup support through
research and development initiatives. NABARD should function
like a National Bank for Farmers. There is a need for a fresh look
at its mandate, role and business model after about 25 years of its
establishment and vastly changed circumstances.
The Reserve Bank and Government of India have to broadly assign
the roles and responsibilities to different agencies in the multi-
agency system and ensure implementation of their policies and
programmes. There is a need for an Agriculture Credit Policy”.

5. ROLE OF COOPERATIVE BANKS
5.1 Cooperative movement in India started in 1900 when the
Cooperative Societies Act was passed in 1904. RBI played a major role
in developing cooperative banks.
5.2 Co-operative Banks have two main categories, viz., urban co-
operative banks with single tier structure and rural co-operative credit
institutions with multi tier structure. Rural cooperative credit institutions
have two distinct categories, viz., the Short-term co-operative credit
structure (STCCS) and the Long-term Co-operative credit structure
(LTCCS). Within the STCCS, primary agricultural credit societies (PACS)
are at the village level, District Central Co-operative Banks (DCCBs)
are placed at the intermediate level, and the State Co-operative banks
(STCBs) at the apex level. The long-term structure of rural Co-operatives
comprises State Co-operative agriculture and rural development banks
(SCARDBs) at the State level, and primary co-operative agriculture
and rural development banks (PCARDBs) at the decentralised district
or block level. The structure of rural Co-operative banks varies from
State to State. Some States have a unitary structure with the State
level banks operating through their own branches, while others have
a mixed structure incorporating both unitary and federal systems.
5.3 According to the Expert Group on Agricultural Indebtedness,
‘with the entry of commercial banks in rural credit the share of
cooperative credit in total agricultural credit had gone down from 70.8
per cent in 1975-76 to only 21.8 per cent in 2005-06. While the share
of cooperatives in short term credit in the form of crop loans declined
from 74.9 per cent to 33.2 per cent; its share in long-term credit declined
steeply from 61.2 per cent to 6.0 per cent’.

to September, 2007-08, the total number of accounts financed by

5.6 As indicated in the Report of the Committee on Financial
Inclusion, the problem of sickness in the Indian rural cooperative
movement became evident as early as in 1930s. The All India Rural
Credit Survey, was set up in the Third Phase (1950-1990) of Co-
operative Movement, which recommended State partnership in equity ,
governance and management to revive the cooperatives. However, as
per the report, attempts such as these failed to resolve the persistent
problems of poor governance, management and weak financial health
and the Co-operative banks are continuing to struggle with problems
of low resource base, high dependence on external sources of funding,
excessive control, huge accumulated losses, imbalances, poor business
diversification, low recovery etc.
5.7 In order to look into the problems of co-operative credit
societies and suggest remedial measures, a Task Force on Revival of
Rural Cooperative Credit Institutions (Vaidyanathan Committee) was
set up in 2004. The Task Force observed that the Co-operative Credit
structure (CCS) was impaired in governance both on managerial and
financial fronts and, hence, needed to be revived and restructured. It
favoured restoration of the autonomous character of credit co-operatives
by bringing down the control and interference of State Governments
through suitable amendments to the State Cooperative Societies Acts
and the Banking Regulation Act, 1949. It also recommended provision
of required financial assistance for recapitalisation of the Short-term
Co-operative Credit Structure (STCCS) for funding the accumulated
losses of the structure, evolving a common accounting system,
management information system (MIS), computerisation and human
resource development (HRD) initiatives. Financial assistance was
recommended to be back ended, subject to the introduction of
institutional, legal and regulatory reforms.
5.8 For the revival of the STCCS, the Vaidyanathan Committee
Report has suggested an implementable Action Plan with substantial
financial assistance for recapitalization subject to introduction of strict

24
legal and institutional reforms together with technical assistance for
Human Resource Development(HRD), establishment of a common
accounting system and computerization.
5.9 The recommendations of the Task Force have been accepted in
principle by the Government. Based on the recommendations of the
Task Force, the Central Government finalised a Revival Package
encompassing the financial assistance as well as legal and institutional
reforms. It envisages a total outlay of Rs. 13,596 crore to be shared by
Government of India, State Governments and the Cooperative Credit
Structure in the ratio of 68:28:4. The assistance is available for (a)
wiping out accumulated losses, (b) covering invoked but unpaid
guarantees given by State Governments, (c) increasing the capital to a
minimum level of 7% and (d) technical assistance (including cost of
special audit, training, computerization etc.). As informed to the
Committee, so far, seventeen States have executed the MoU with
NABARD and Government for implementing the Package, and five
more States have given consent to sign the MoU. Till date, Rs. 3470
crore has been released by the Government as grants under the
package.
5.10 As seen from the report on Trend and Progress of Banking,
the implementing agency of the revival package of Cooperative Credit
Institutions, NABARD, devised field tested formats for special audit of
Primary Agricultural Co-operative Societies (PACS) to arrive at the
exact extent of accumulated losses. In conformity with the reform
measures envisaged under the Revival Package, the State Governments
of Andhra Pradesh and Haryana have reportedly brought in
amendments to their respective Co-operative Societies Acts, and several
other State Governments are in the process of effecting similar
amendments.
5.11 Further, as informed to the Committee, in accordance with

6. EXPANSION OF REGIONAL RURAL BANKS (RRBS)
6.1 Regional Rural Banks (RRBs) came into existence in 1976 with
the promulgation of the Regional Rural Banks Act for establishing a
set of regionally oriented rural banks, which would combine the local
feel and familiarity of rural problems characteristic of cooperatives
with the professionalism and large resource base of commercial banks.
The equity of RRBs is held by the Central Government, concerned
State Government and the Sponsor Bank in the proportion of 50:15:35.
The number of RRBs went up from 6 in December, 1975 to 196 in
2004.
6.2 According to the report of the Expert Group on Agricultural
Indebtedness, the performance of RRBs deteriorated during the 1990s
owing to factors such as low recovery rates, low earning capacity due
to limits imposed on interest rate charged by them, increase in wages
and salaries and competition with sponsoring banks in their area of
operation. However, beginning with 2002, the performance of RRBs is
stated to have improved.
6.3 The main problem confronted by RRBs being their financial
health, a number of Committees have gone into the problem of their
financial viability viz. Committee to Review Arrangements for
Institutional Credit for Agriculture and Rural Development
(CRAFICARD,1981) Working Group on RRBs (Kelkar Committee, 1984),
Agricultural Credit Review Committee (Khusro Committee, 1989),
Committee on Restructuring of RRBs (Bhandari Committee, 1994),
Commtitee on Revamping of RRBs (Basu Committee, 1996), Expert
Group on RRBs (Thingalaya Committee, 1997), Expert Committee on
Rural Credit (Vyas Committee I, 2001), Chalpati Rao Committee, 2003,
Group of CMDs of Select Public Sector Banks, 2004 (Purwar Committee)
Advisory Committee on Flow of Credit to Agriculture and Related
Activities etc. The suggestions of these varied from vertical merger
(with sponsor banks), horizontal merger (amongst RRBs operating in
a particular region) and liquidation etc. to make RRBs financially viable.
6.4 The Working Group under the Chairmanship of A.V Sardesai
revisited the issue of restructuring the RRBs in 2005. This Group held
that to improve the operational viability of RRBs and take advantage
of the economies of scale, the route of merger/amalgamation of RRBs
may be considered taking into account the views of the various
stakeholders. Merger of RRBs with the sponsor bank is not provided
in the RRB Act 1976. The Committee was of the view that a change

26
in sponsor banks may , in some cases help in improving the
performance of RRBs. Various other measures suggested by this Group
include balance sheet strengthening, maintaining minimum capital,
governance and management issues, regulatory and supervisory aspects
and methods for improving profitability of RRBs.
6.5 The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) in September, 2006, constituted
a Task Force on Empowering the RRB Boards for Operational Efficiency
(Chairman: Dr. K.G. Karmakar) to suggest ways for giving more power
and flexibility to the Boards of RRBs in decision making. In its report
submitted on January 31, 2007, the Task Force, made several
recommendations relating to operational flexibility of RRBs. While some
of the recommendations of the Task Force have reportedly been
implemented, others are under examination.
6.6 In order to reposition RRBs as an effective instrument of credit
delivery in the Indian financial system, the Government of India, after
consultation with NABARD, the concerned State Governments and the
sponsor banks initiated State-level sponsor bank-wise amalgamation of
RRBs in September 2005 to overcome the deficiencies prevailing in
RRBs and making them viable and profitable units.
6.7 According to the Annual Report 2007-08 of NABARD, following
the amalgamation of RRBs (2005-06 onwards), the number of RRBs
has been reduced from 196 to 96 with an operating network of 14,520
branches covering 534 districts in 26 States as on 31 March 2007. Over
a period of three years (2005-07), aggregate reserves of RRBs increased
significantly (28%), while the deposits and investments increased by
34 and 24 percent, respectively. During the same period, loans and
advances outstanding increased by 47 percent while loans issued
increased by 57 percent.
6.8 During the year, 81 of 96 RRBs attained current viability and
showed improvement by way of increase in profits, reduction in losses
or by transcending from loss to profit by end-March 2007. The
remaining 15 RRBs continued to be loss-making entities. The amount
of net profit posted by RRBs as a group increased to Rs. 625.15 crore
during 2006-07 as against Rs. 617.13 crore in 2005-06. The aggregated
reserves of 56 RRBs that had wiped off their accumulated losses and
attained sustainable viability was Rs. 4,901.54 crore at end-March 2007.
Net worth of RRBs as a whole stood at Rs. 4,526.48 crore as on
31 March 2007.
6.9 As per the announcement of the Union Budget 2007-08, among
other measures, the RRBs, which have a negative net worth, would be
recapitalised in a phased manner. Modalities for recapitalisation are

8. CREDIT PENETRATION—ROLE OF SELF-HELP GROUPS AND
MICRO FINANCE INSTITUTIONS IN CREDIT DELIVERY
8.1 Inspite of nationalization of banks, empirical studies in 1980s
showed that a large section of the poorest of the poor could not access
formal banking system. In order to expand banking network to reach
this segment as well as with a view to developing a supplementary
credit delivery system that is cost effective and user friendly for both
banks and the poor, micro finance institutions were encouraged in
India. Micro financing was introduced through two models viz. Self-
Help Group – Bank Linkage Programme and micro finance institutions.
8.2 The SHG-Bank Linkage Programme was started as an action
research project in 1989. The findings of the project led to the launching
of the pilot project by NABARD in 1992 with policy support from the
Reserve Bank. The pilot project was designed as a partnership model
between three agencies, viz., the SHGs, banks and non-government
organisations (NGOs). The SHGs were expected to facilitate collective
decision making by the poor and provide ‘doorstep banking’. The
banks as wholesalers of credit, were to provide the resources, while
the NGOs were to act as agencies to organise the poor, build their
capacities and facilitate the process of empowering them.
8.3 The programme has since come a long way from the pilot
project of financing 500 SHGs across the country. It has proved its
efficacy as a mainstream programme for banking with the poor who
mainly comprise the marginal farmers, landless labourers, artisans and
craftsmen and others engaged in small businesses such as hawking
and vending in the rural areas. The main advantages of the programme
are timely repayment of loans to banks, reduction in transaction costs
both to the poor and the banks, doorstep “saving and credit” facility
for the poor and exploitation of the untapped business potential of
the rural areas. The programme, which started as an outreach
programme has not only aimed at promoting thrift and credit, but
also contributed immensely towards the empowerment of the rural
women.
8.4 During 2005-06, a focussed and location-specific micro-enterprise
development programme (MEDP) on skill upgradation and
development for sustainable livelihoods for members of the matured
SHGs was launched by NABARD.

36
8.5 The Finance Minister in the Budget Speech for 2005-06 had
indicated that the Government intended to promote MFIs in a big
way. Accordingly the ‘Micro-Finance Development Fund’ placed with
NABARD was re-designated as ‘Micro-Finance Development and Equity
Fund’ and its corpus was enhanced to Rs.200 crore from Rs.100 crore.
Further, measures are being taken to promote linkage of MFIs with
banks.
8.6 During 2006-07, 686,408 new SHGs were credit linked with
banks, taking the cumulative number of SHGs credit linked to 2.92
million. Bank loans disbursed to SHGs during 2006-07 amounted to
Rs.6,643 crore taking the cumulative bank loan disbursed to SHGs up
to March 2007 to Rs.18,041 crore.
8.7 During 2006-07, NABARD intensified the implementation of
the programme in the 13 identified priority States, some of which
account for the bulk of the rural poor, viz., Uttar Pradesh, Orissa,
West Bengal, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Gujarat, Rajasthan,
Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, Bihar, Assam, Himachal Pradesh and
Uttrakhand. Accordingly, the programme spread rapidly in these States
indicating a marked shift from its initial localisation in the Southern
region. Non-Southern region registered a share of 48 per cent at end
March, 2007, as against 29 per cent at the end of March, 2001.
8.8 In terms of relative shares of different agencies, commercial
banks continue to maintain their lead both in terms of numbers of
SHGs credit linked and loan disbursed. Though RRBs take the second
position after banks, their share in the total has declined in recent
years. The share of co-operative banks remained unchanged at
14 per cent in terms of number of SHGs credit linked and their share
in loan disbursed dropped during the year.
8.9 As per the Economic Survey (2007-08) i n all, 498 banks (50
commercial banks, 96 amalgamated RRBs and 352 cooperative banks)
are actively participating in the programme. The average bank loan
availed of per SHG and per family amounted to Rs. 65,924 and
Rs. 4,708, respectively. The outreach of the programme is said to have
enabled an estimated 427 lakh poor households to gain access to micro-
finance from the formal banking system.
8.10 In spite of all these measures, micro finance has not been able
to become a primary source of credit disbursal for poor, since a number
of problems still persist viz. regional imbalance, good quality SHGs,
graduation from credit to enterprise, promotion of SHG federation,
high cost of delivery, widening of scope and outreach of SHGs etc.

37
8.11 On Self Help Groups (SHGs) as a means of helping the poor
in getting integrated with the mainstream, the Expert Group on
Financial Inclusion has observed inter alia :
“The basic benefit conferred by the SHGs on the poor is the
opportunity to come together for collective action to improve their
livelihood, protect their interest and develop together for collective
action to improve their livelihoods, protect their interest and
develop the capacity to participate in mainstream activities on fair
terms, with much enhanced bargaining power and negotiation skills
with markets and state institutions. What cannot be achieved as
individuals can be achieved collectively by creating alternative
institutions. Problems of missing markets and malfunctioning
markets can be effectively tackled by peoples’ (farmers’)
institutions.”
8.12 Emphasising on State intervention and support in formation

“The State has to intervene actively and support the formation of
such institutions to empower the disempowered farmers by
promoting vigorously farmers’ collective institutions.”
8.13 Also, underlying the need for mitigating the burden of farmers

“Panchayati Raj Institutions (PRIs), civil society organizations like

banking facilities. As on May 10, 2006, 96 credit counseling centers

PART II
RECOMMENDATIONS/OBSERVATIONS
OF THE COMMITTEE
1. Credit is an essential requirement for revitalizing agriculture.
Various Committees and Expert Groups have gone into the problems
of rural credit and non-availability of institutional credit to farmers.
Though numerous measures have been taken by the Government
from time to time to address these problems on the basis of the
recommendations of these Committees, the Committee are dismayed
to note that even now vast majority of farmer households do not
have access to institutional credit. According to the recent report of
the Committee on Financial Inclusion, headed by Dr. C. Rangarajan,
64.95 million (73%), out of 89.3 million farmer households and
46.6 million (78.2%) out of 59.6 million non-cultivator households
(Agricultural labourers, artisans and others) do not have access to
formal sources of credit. The Committee hardly need to point out
that interest burden becomes a heavy liability to farmers, vast
majority of whom take loans from non-institutional sources, like
money lenders at high rates of interest due to non-availability of
institutional credit. The Committee would therefore, urge that as
recommended by the Committee on Financial Inclusion, at least 50%
(55.77 million) of the excluded cultivator and non-cultivator
households should be provided access to credit through commercial
banks and RRBs by the year 2012 and the remaining households by
the year 2015. The Committee would like to be informed of the
specific steps taken by the Government to implement these
recommendations and details of annual targets, fixed in this regard.
2. The Committee  regret to note that commercial banks, despite
having a vast chain of branches in rural areas have continued to fail
to reach the mandated level of disbursing 18% credit to agriculture.
The shortfall in bank lending to agriculture in the year, 2007-08 was
to the tune of as much as Rs. 21,818 crore. More disturbing is the
fact that banks appear to be averse to disburse credit to farmers
despite the fact that the recovery of direct agricultural advances has
been impressive and has been improving from 74.5% in 2004 to
78.6% in 2005 and to 80.1% in 2006 in the case of public sector
banks. The lower disbursements to agriculture are attributed to non-
completion of formalities by borrowers, lack of co-ordination between

39
banks and sponsoring agencies, bunching of applications, etc. The
Committee feel that these are matters which could be managed by
a proper co-ordination mechanism and awareness campaigns by
banks. The Committee urge that suitable measures be taken to
address these problems and to ensure that the targeted level of credit
reaches farmers.
3.  The Expert Group (headed by Prof. R. Radha Krishna) which
submitted its report on 3 July, 2007 has highlighted that farmers in
States which recorded the highest rates of suicide borrowed money
for productive purposes i.e. to buy seeds, fertilizers, pesticides, etc.
As brought out in a succeeding paragraph, farmers are debarred
from availing institutional credit if they default under the Kisan
Credit Card Scheme. The Ministry of Finance have stated that the
debt waiver and debt relief scheme announced in the budget, 2008
is expected to de-clog the line of credit to farmers by bringing a
sizable number of indebted farmers to access fresh institutional loans,
and strengthen the rural financial institutions. The Committee feel
that concerted efforts and result oriented strategies are needed to
address the root cause of the malady of deep rooted indebtedness in
the agrarian society. The Committee wish to be apprised of the
specific measures evolved in this regard and also details of the extent
to which implementation of the debt waiver and debt relief scheme
has contributed in helping the debarred farmers in accessing
institutional credit and in strengthening the rural credit delivery
system.
4. The Committee find that the Rural Infrastructure Development
Fund (RIDF) set up in 1995-96, for banks to deposit to the Fund in
compensation for shortfall in lending to priority sector has failed to
serve as an effective disincentive. From the data made available, the
Committee are surprised to note that while the corpus under RIDF
tranches X to XIII (2004-05 to 2007-08) totals to Rs. 46,000 crore, the
amounts deposited by banks upto 31.01.2008 add upto only
Rs. 13,896.69 crore, with the gap in utilization being to the extent of
as much as Rs. 32,103.31 crore. The Committee have been informed
that four more funds are being created requiring banks to deposit
the whole amount of shortfall in priority sector lending, in pursuance
of the recommendations of the Expert Group on Agricultural
Indebtedness. The Committee find that the corpus of all the new
four funds adds up to just Rs. 10,200 crore whereas the total shortfall
of the banks under the priority sector lending during the year 2007-
08 alone was of the order of Rs. 22,000 crore. In order to address
this situation, the Committee feel that an effective mechanism should

40
be evolved to ensure that banks reach out to the farmers and meet
the agricultural lending targets. The Committee would suggest that
it may be considered whether it would be desirable to introduce
any punitive measures in this regard.
5. Government provides interest subvention of 2% to public sector
banks, Regional Rural Banks and Cooperative Banks to enable them
to extend short-term production credit to farmers at 7% interest. The
Committee agree with the National Commission on Farmers that
inefficiencies of the delivery system should not be loaded on the
interest charged. The Committee recommend that in order to make
credit affordable and as proposed by the National Commission on
Farmers and as stressed by the Committee in their 51st report
(14th Lok Sabha), interest rates on agricultural credit should be
brought down to 4% if necessary by extending the scheme of interest
subvention. This will improve further credit absorption by farmers.
The Committee would also like to know the cost cutting measures
introduced by banks, the extent of their implementation, adoption
of new modes of delivery viz . banking facilitators/banking
correspondents, etc. and impact of these measures on interest rates
and further deepening the credit absorption by farmers.
6. The Committee are disappointed to note that credit growth by
the co-operatives to the agricultural sector has declined from 25% in
2005-06 to 8% in 2006-07. The number of accounts financed by co-
operatives has also declined from 225 lakh in 2004-05 to 189 lakh in
2006-07. Co-operative societies are most potent means for widening
and deepening access to institutional credit to farmers. Their
revitalization is therefore vital. Though the Government has taken
up measures for recapitalizing cooperative credit institutions in
accordance with the suggestions of Task Force on Revival of Rural
Cooperative Credit Insitution, it is not clear as to by when this
exercise is expected to be completed. The Task Force had
recommended amendments to State Co-operative Societies Act with
a view to restoring the autonomous character of credit co-operatives.
Two States (Andhra Pradesh and Haryana) have so far amended
their respective Cooperative Societies Acts. The Committee are, of
the view that in order to make cooperative institutions a more
effective channel of disbursing rural credit, it is necessary to pursue
a time bound approach for reviving them. More importantly, State
Governments must be persuaded to bring in legislations amending
their respective Cooperative Societies Act without further delay.

7. The Committee observe that out of 96 Regional Rural Banks
(RRBs) as on 31 March, 2007, 15 were incurring losses. The poor
performance of RRBs is attributed to poor recoveries, low earning
capacity, hike in expenditure on account of higher wages and salaries
and competition from sponsoring banks in the area of operation.
With a view to improving the performance of RRBs, a number of
measures have reportedly been taken, which include, amalgamation
of RRBs, infusion of funds to strengthen the capital base and
allowing these banks to tap deposits from new sources and to enter
into new business areas. It has been stated that there are 27 RRBs

According to the Ministry of Finance (Department of Financial
Services), the number of uncovered districts by RRBs has now been
reduced to 37. The Committee urge that RRBs network should be
expanded to cover the remaining districts at the earliest.
9. The Committee regret to note that though the Kisan Credit
Card (KCC) scheme has been extended to cover all categories of
loans viz. short and long term loans and productive and non-
productive loans, the loans sanctioned under the scheme has declined
from Rs. 47,601 crore in 2005-06 to 40,300 crore in 2006-07. Similarly,
it is observed that out of 685 lakh KCC holders, only about 397 lakh
farmers (60%) have availed agricultural credit during 2006-07.
Ineligibility to avail fresh loans by defaulters is stated to be the
main reason in this regard. Consequently, as many as 40% of KCC
holders are ineligible for getting credit from the institutional sources
and are driven to money lenders. It appears that the scheme has led
to unintended consequences. The Committee feel that it is high time,
the scheme is critically reviewed with a view to plugging the short
comings and to ensure that non-wilful defaulters are not put to any
difficulty in accessing institutional credit.
10. The Committee find that the progress in implementation of
KCC Scheme is tardy in Goa, Himachal Pradesh, Jammu & Kashmir,
Sikkim and North Eastern States, which are still under-banked.
Action needs to be taken to remedy the situation in these States.

11. The Committee find it surprising to note that the data
reporting system of the nodal agency (NABARD) for implementation
of the KCC Scheme does not include information on total amount
of credit availed by KCC holders and recovery performance under
the scheme. Such information is vital to assess the performance of
the scheme and to overcome its weaknesses/shortcomings. The
Committee, therefore, desire that the data reporting system of
NABARD is geared up to include this information of vital importance
to enable assessment of the successfulness of the KCC Scheme.
12. The Committee find that a number of shortcomings and
weaknesses have been identified in the KCC scheme by the study
undertaken by National Council of Applied Economic Research
(NCAER). The weaknesses include, issuing of KCCs only to
pensioners by some banks, inadequacy of credit limits in many cases,
fixing of minimum credit limit by some banks and variations in
service charges. The Committee desire that remedial action should
be taken immediately to remove the weaknesses in order to ensure
that the scheme serves as an effective means of meeting the credit
requirements of farmers. The Committee hope that the Government
will also take necessary action to implement the recommendations
of the Expert Group on Agricultural Indebtedness particularly the

Indebtedness has also recommended the creation of a Money Lenders
Debt Redemption Fund with a corpus of Rs. 100 crore contributed
by Central and State Governments. In view of the continued and
increasing dependence of the rural households on money lenders
for meeting credit requirements, as evident from the NSSO’s survey
data on rural indebtedness, the Committee expect the Government
to implement the recommendation of the Expert Group without delay
and create the proposed fund, which would facilitate in the swapping
of debt.
