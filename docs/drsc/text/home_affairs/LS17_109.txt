REPORT
The Ministry of Home Affairs has been entrusted broadly with the following functions:
(a) To deal with all matters relating to internal security;
(b) To deal with all matters relating to Centre-State relations and Inter-State relations;
(c) Implementation of the provisions of the Constitution relating to Official Language and
the provisions of the Official Language Act, 1963;
(d) To discharge certain basic functions like notification of assumption of office by the
President and the Vice-President; notification of appointment of the Prime Minister
and other Ministers; notification of appointment, resignation and removal of
Governors and Lt. Governors, appointment of Lt. Governors, Chief Commissioners
and Administrative Officers in Union Territories;
(e) To deal with matters like citizenship and naturalisation, census of population, National
Anthem, National Flag, etc.; and
(f) To deal with all constitutional provisions with respect to the State of Jammu and
Kashmir and all other matters relating to the State of Jammu and Kashmir excluding
those with which the Ministry of External Affairs is concerned.
2. To discharge the above functions, the Ministry has been divided into six Departments:
(i) Department of Internal Security – to deal with Police, Law and Order and
rehabilitation;
(ii) Department of States – to deal with Centre-State relations, inter-State relations, Union
Territories and Freedom Fighters’ Pension;
(iii) Dep artment of Official Language – to deal with the implementation of the provisions
of the Constitution relating to Official Language and provisions of the Official
Language Act, 1963;
(iv) Department of Home – to deal with notification of assumption of office by the
President and Vice-President, notification of appointment of the Prime Minister and
other Ministers, etc.;
(v) Department of Jammu and Kashmir Affairs – to deal with all constitutional provisions
with respect to the State of Jammu and Kashmir and all other matters relating to the
State of Jammu and Kashmir excluding those with which the Ministry of External
Affairs is concerned; and
(vi) Department of Border Management to deal with management of borders including
coastal borders.

12. Demand Nos. 95, 96, 97, 98 and 99 which are given in V olume-II of the Demands for
Grants of the Ministry of Home Affairs, relate to Union Territories (without Legislature), namely
Andaman and Nicobar Islands, Chandigarh, Dadra and Nagar Haveli, Daman and Diu and
Lakshadweep, respectively. The entire budget of the Union Territories (without Legislature) on the
Plan as well as Non-Plan side is reflected in the Demands for Grants of the Ministry. Though the
budget of these Union Territories forms part of Ministry of Home Affairs, being the nodal Ministry,
it, however, does not dilute the responsibilities of the other Ministries of the Central Government
from scrutinising and sanctioning the schemes under their purview. The factual position regarding
budgetary procedure in this regard is that the entire budget requirement for Plan and Non-Plan,
revised estimates for current year and Non-Plan budget estimates for the ensuing year is discussed
by the Ministry of Finance in the pre-budget meetings with the representatives of the concerned
Union Territories and Ministry of Home Affairs. An overall ceiling is fixed in respect of each Union
Territory. The Planning Commission decides annual Plan allocations for the ensuing year. The Union
Territory Administrations prepare budgetary break-up showing budget provisions according to their
respective budgetary heads and also showing the allocation against Ministries/Departments of the
Central Government administratively concerned. This budgetary break-up is included in the
Demands for Grants of the Ministry of Home Affairs.

Centre (SMAC) in five major cities for operational level and participation of State Governments
through Inter-State Intelligence Support Team. Similarly TECHINT, i.e. Technical Intelligence,
included upgrading the Technical capabilities of IB and establishing of INMARSAT monitoring
system.

14.8 W ith regard to augmentation and Modernisation of Central Police Forces, (CPFs), the
Secretary stated that the CPFs continued to play a major role in maintenance of internal security and
also guarding India’s borders in peace time. He mentioned of the steps taken to augment their
strength, induct modern equipment, and ensure better quality training and also better service
conditions for their personnel. He than dwelt upon the new raisings of CPFs and stated that in the
Post Kargil situation, sanction was accorded to raise 209 battalions in Central Police Forces, Rashtriya
Rifles, and India Reserve battalions over a period of five years. With the raising of battalions in CRPF
it would become possible to release more units of the BSF, ITBP and SSB from their commitment
on internal security duties for reversion to their primary role of border guarding. As per original
sanction all battalions were to be raised by 2004-05. However, after review, Inter Ministerial Group
recommended that 25 battalions of CRPF scheduled for raising in 2004-05 would be phased over two

CROSS-BORDER INFILTRATION AND EXFILTRATION IN JAMMU AND KASHMIR
23. The ongoing militancy and terrorism in Jammu and Kashmir have all along been influenced
by the activities going on across the border of Jammu and Kashmir including the Line of Control
(LoC). This influence is largely manifested in the form of unabated and steady flow of trained,
well-armed and well-equipped militants infiltrating into the State from across the border as also the
exfiltration of misguided persons especially the youth from the State for receiving training in the
militant training camps located across the border.
23.1 The process of infiltration and exfiltration was expected to come to a halt when India and
Pakistan agreed to have ceasefire along LoC in November, 2003 as well as the international border.
But as a matter of fact both infiltration as well as exfiltration continue unabated though there has
been reduction in the number of such incidents ever since the ceasefire came into force.

19
23.2 In this context, it would be pertinent to mention the work going on for the last few years
to fence and floodlight the LoC and the international border to reduce their porosity with a view
to check infiltration and exfiltration. The continuous shelling from across the border has always
hampered this work. However, after the enforcement of the ceasefire the pace of the fencing and
floodlighting work has appreciably improved. The Ministry of Home Affairs has informed that along
the international border in Jammu and Kashmir fencing has been completed in respect of 143 kms.
and only a stretch of 37 kms. remains to be fenced. Similarly, along LoC fencing has been
completed to the extent of 580 kms. and a balance of 90 kms. remains to be completed. The
ongoing project for fencing of international border and LoC is now expected to be completed
by 2006. The Ministry has further informed that the substantial increase in the allocation in
BE 2004-05 over RE 2003-04 under the head “Indo-Pak Border Works” is to speed up the ongoing
fencing and floodlighting work.
23.3 While touching upon the issue of infiltration and violence in Jammu and Kashmir, the Home
Secretary presented figures showing the trends in infiltration and violence in the State in the last
three years as well as the current year as shown in Table No.5 supra.
23.4 These figures clearly indicate that there has been sharp decline in the number of infiltration
incidents, estimated number of infiltration, number of infiltration bids foiled and number of
terrorists killed in the foiled bids. In reply to a query in relation to these figures, the Director-
General, BSF explained the position and stated that the decline in the numbers was partly due to
the ceasefire along the border and partly due to the fencing and improved means of surveillance.
On the one hand there had been marked decline in the figures pertaining to infiltration and violence
in the State after the ceasefire between India and Pakistan while on the other, there had been
increase in violence in last three months of the current year as submitted by the Home Secretary.
23.5 Thus, it may be summed up that ceasefire along the border, fencing and better surveillance
have had a visible impact on the infiltration from across the border into Jammu and Kashmir. But
at the same time an increasing trend in terrorist activities is also noticeable despite the presence
of the factors responsible for their decline since November, 2003 when ceasefire between Indian
and Pakistani forces came into force. The rising trend in terrorist activities, if looked against the
backdrop of the fact that necessary infrastructure still exists across the border to aid and abet
terrorist activities in Jammu and Kashmir, makes it amply clear that menace of cross border
terrorism and infiltration will continue to pose a serious challenge to India in the times to come.
Therefore, it is imperative that the Government of India should be wary of this reality.
23.6 Against this backdrop, the Committee is of the opinion that our security forces
should intensify their fight against cross border terrorism and infiltration. A decline in the
figures relating to this menace should not be construed as a positive change in the
intentions of the militant organisations as well as their sponsors. While cautioning the
Government about the dangers and challenges that it may have to grapple with in the near
future in Jammu and Kashmir, the Committee once again reiterates its earlier viewpoint
that unless we reduce the porosity of our borders and make it extremely difficult for
infiltrators to sneak into Indian territory we will not be able to put effective checks on
terrorist activities taking place in Jammu and Kashmir. The need for enhanced surveillance
and effective border guarding both along the international border and LoC becomes all the
more essential keeping in view the fact that BSF being the border guarding force, is now
being withdrawn from counter-insurgency operations and therefore can be deployed along
the border to check infiltration and exfiltration.
FIDAYEEN ATTACKS ON SECURITY FORCES IN JAMMU AND KASHMIR
24. Our security forces have been making the supreme sacrifice at the altar of duty by laying

20
precious lives to fight separatist elements in Jammu and Kashmir for more than a decade. The
security forces, despite immense stress and strain, have been doing a commendable job and have
so far maintained an upper hand over the militants and foreign mercenaries. The constant hot
pursuit of the militants by the security forces have always kept them on the run. It is because of
this constant pressure maintained by the security forces that the militants and their mentors have
been evolving and using innovative methods to inflict lethal damages on our forces. One such
method is the fidayeen attack i.e. suicide attack which is proving more lethal than any other
method employed by the militants so far. The most obvious target of this kind of attacks is the
security forces and their installations. In the past, such attacks have been mounted on moving
convoys of security forces, company headquarters of the forces, army cantonments and their
residential quarters, etc. The attacks on moving convoys, like the one on a BSF convoy in
April, 2004 in which thirty-one persons lost their lives, always have an element of surprise leading
to high casualties. But the attacks on the installations and quarters of the security forces are the
matter of real concern. In the past also, this Committee has expressed its concern over this
disturbing fact. The entry of fidayeen squads in the installations of security forces is an indicator
to the fact that something is lacking so far as the fortification of the installations and the security
in and around them is concerned. The recent attacks on CRPF personnel in July-August, 2004
leading to the killings of fourteen of them once again highlights the fact as to how vulnerable these
installations have become to the fidayeen attacks. In one of the incidents, the militants mingled with
the CRPF personnel taking advantage of the darkness in the evening and took on the forces right
in their compound. All this goes on to prove that the security cover in and around the installations
of the security forces especially the command headquarters and bases which have heavy
concentration of forces needs to be strengthened and made foolproof. Because the main idea behind
the action of the militants is to target those places which are heavily concentrated by the security
personnel so that maximum collateral damage is inflicted on them.
24.1 The Committee recommends that the Ministry in consultation with the field
commanders of the forces deployed in Jammu and Kashmir, should devise an effective
strategy to provide foolproof security cover to the installations of the forces with a view to
deter and defeat the designs of fidayeen squads. The Ministry should also ensure that no
budgetary constraints are allowed to come in the way of providing necessary personnel and
material support to the forces in their endeavour to insulate themselves against such
attacks as such attacks have a direct bearing on the morale of the forces.
NAXALISM
25. The menace of Naxalism- a manifestation of extremism continues to expand its area of
influence and now its tentacles are spread over as many as 55 districts of States which are highly
affected by it. Besides, 18 districts come under the medium category; another 57 under low
category and 24 districts fall in the category of targeted districts. In all, 149 districts are affected
by this kind of extremism with variable degree and intensity. During 2003 as many as 1594 violent
incidents took place in which 515 civilians, 105 policemen and 211 extremists lost their lives. The
number of arms looted by the extremists from the security forces was 245 which has gone up
to 650 upto July, 2004. Thus, a disturbing and increasing trend is visible in the violence being
perpetrated by the extremists.
25.1 Keeping in view the serious proportions that this problem is assuming especially over the
last few years, the need for finding a solution to it should be the topmost priority of the
Government. The strategy being adopted by the Government to deal with this problem was also
commented upon by the Committee in its 98th Report on Demands for Grants (2003-04) of the
Ministry. Although the Government has taken steps like inclusion of 55 naxalite affected districts
under the Backward Districts Initiative of the Planning Commission under Rashtriya Sam Vikas

21
Yojana and provision of Rs.37.5 crore under Prime Minister Gram Sadak Yojana (PMGSY). The
good work of any such attempt is likely to get lost unless normalcy is restored.
25.2 The latest attempt of Government of Andhra Pradesh and the PWG to initiate peace dialogue
is a welcome step in the direction of a peaceful solution to the problem. While responding to the
initiation for talks extended by the Government of Andhra Pradesh, the PWG suggested for
announcement of ceasefire, formation of a Monitoring Committee and talks with mediators by the
Government. The Government of Andhra Pradesh accepted the suggestions in June, 2004 leading
to a reciprocal acceptance of ceasefire for three months by the PWG on 16 June, 2004. Both sides
announced their mediators for talks on 29 June, 2004 and talks got underway. The Home Minister
of Government of Andhra Pradesh held preliminary round of talks with PWG in July, 2004. Thus,
a positive beginning has been made for finding a solution to the problem.
25.3 In the context of initiating a peace dialogue, it may be pointed out that in response to this
Committee’s reiteration of its earlier recommendation made in its 88th Report on Demands for
Grants (2002-03) of the Ministry, the Government, in its ATR on the recommendations contained
in Committee’s 98th Report on Demands for Grants (2003-04) of the Ministry, inter alia stated that
as regards peace dialogue affected States had been requested to offer their views on the need to
hold a peace dialogue with the extremist outfits and whether the Central Government should have
any direct involvement in any such peace dialogue.
25.4 The well considered view of this Committee has been that the Government of India
should come forward to hold a broad, comprehensive and meaningful dialogue with all the
extremist organizations who abjure the path of violence to find a peaceful and lasting
solution to the problem.
INDIA RESERVE BATTALIONS
26. As per the recommendations of the Inter-ministerial Group set up in the light of the Report
of the Group of Ministers on National Security, 50 India Reserve (IR) battalions were proposed
to be raised at the rate of 10 battalions per year over a period of five years beginning 2000-01.
The Ministry, in its Performance Budget 2004-05, has stated that as per the recommendations of
Inter-ministerial Group, the raisings of IR battalions are being scheduled accordingly i.e.
10 battalions per year. It has stated that during 2003-04, 10 battalions had been sanctioned to
different States.
26.1 Here it would be pertinent to have a brief look at the mechanism for raising IR battalions.
As per the Performance Budget 2004-05 of the Ministry, the IR Battalion scheme was formulated
to overcome the ever-increasing demand from the States for deployment of CPMFs on internal
security duties. These battalions are raised as Armed Police Battalions of the States. The scheme
envisages reimbursement of initial raising cost as one time expenditure subject to the ceiling as
fixed by the Central Government from time to time. The Central Government reimburses to the
States such expenditure as 50% Grant-in-aid and 50% long-term loan.
26.2 Coming back to the issue of raisings of IR battalions, attention may be invited to the reply
of the Ministry of Home Affairs in respect of a query as to what was the status of the raisings
of 127 battalions of CPMFs including IR battalions sanctioned upto the year 2002-03. The Ministry
has informed that out of 30 IR battalions sanctioned during a period of three years
from 2000-01 to 2002-03, only 7 battalions could be raised. As regards the raisings sanctioned in
2003-04, the Ministry has furnished two contradictory statements. On the one hand the
Performance Budget of the Ministry states that 10 battalions have been sanctioned during 2003-04
while on the other in reply to a query referred to above in this paragraph, the Ministry has
informed that 8 battalions were sanctioned during 2003-04. So far as the actual raising is
concerned, it is nil during 2003-04.

22
26.3 Thus it becomes amply clear that the programme for raising IR battalions has not made
much headway despite there being adequate budgetary allocations earmarked for the implementation
of the programme. The Ministry, on its part, has performed the annual ritual of sanctioning about
10 battalions from 2000-01 onwards but it seems to have not ensured that States leave no effort
on their part to raise the battalions in a time bound manner. If the tardy pace with which IR
battalions are being raised is compared with the simultaneous raisings of battalions in CPMFs
during the same period, it becomes sufficiently clear that the programme for raising IR battalions
has not been followed with full commitment and a sense of urgency. This delay in the actual
raisings is a testimony to the lackadaisical approach of the Ministry of Home Affairs. This
programme being centrally funded programme, should have received adequate attention of the
Ministry. The Ministry’s role should not have been confined merely to the sanctioning of the
battalions. Rather it should have played a proactive role in seeing that the sanctioned battalions were
actually raised within the stipulated time period.
26.4 The Committee while noting the manner in which the programme for raising the
additional IR battalions has been pursued by the Ministry of Home Affairs, strongly
recommends that the Ministry should take urgent steps to ensure that all the battalions
sanctioned by it hitherto, should be raised expeditiously without any loss of time. The
Ministry should also set a time-frame for the States to which these battalions have been
sanctioned within which they should be required to raise the sanctioned battalions. As for
the battalions to be sanctioned during the current year, the Ministry should make it clear
before hand to the concerned States that the battalions sanctioned for them should be raised
in a time bound manner failing which any future demand on their part for additional
battalions may not be entertained except for some exceptional circumstances. The Ministry
should impress upon the States that when it comes to maintenance of internal security it
is the joint responsibility of both the Union as well as the State Governments. Therefore,
both Governments should make efforts in tandem to see that necessary infrastructure is in
place to meet the challenges on the internal security front. Both should also keep in mind
that if the additional IR battalions are not raised in time, the very purpose behind raisings
gets defeated and the States continue to depend on the Centre for their requirements of
additional forces.
HEADQUARTERS AND GROUP CENTRES FOR BATTALIONS OF CPMFS
27. As part of its endeavour to modernise and augment CPMFs, the Ministry of Home Affairs
has been implementing a five-year perspective plan with an outlay of Rs.3740.71 crore which
inter alia includes the raising of additional battalions of CPMFs from 2003 to 2008. Out of these
209 battalions, 127 battalions were sanctioned by 2002-03 and 36 more battalions have been
sanctioned for 2003-04. Thus, a total of 163 battalions have been sanctioned by 2003-04, out of

27.1 Insofar as raising the battalions of CPMFs is concerned, efforts are on to raise requisite

of necessary infrastructure to support the battalions. This concern was expressed by the
Committee in its 98th Report on Demands for Grants (2003-04) of the Ministry as well. In this
context, a query was made to the Ministry as to how many battalions of all the CPMFs were not
having Headquarters and Group Centres of their own. In response to the query, the Ministry
informed that as many as 77 battalions out of a total of 416 battalions of CPMFs did not have

27.2 The fact that a considerable number of battalions do not have their own necessary
infrastructure arouses the concern of the Committee. This goes on to prove that the emphasis of
the Ministry is more on raising the number of battalions. The Ministry should also bear in mind

23
the requirement of necessary support infrastructure for the growing number of battalions. As a
matter of fact, the programme for additional raisings as well as the provision of support
paraphernalia should go hand in hand. Provision of necessary support infrastructure is as much
important as the raising of additional battalions. One cannot be ignored at the expense of the other.
The efficiency and performance of the forces hinges on how well they are equipped and
accommodated. The forces which are fighting for the very survival of this nation should not be
starved of their basic requirements. It is the primary responsibility of the Ministry of Home Affairs
to ensure that our security forces are not forced to live on a shoestring.
27.3 The Committee is of the strong view that the Ministry of Home Affairs should chalk
out a detailed action plan for providing necessary support infrastructure to all the battalions

MODERNIZATION OF STATE POLICE FORCES
28. In its 98th Report on Demands for Grants (2003-04) of Ministry of Home Affairs, the
Committee had highlighted the fact as to how State Governments were found wanting on their part
vis-a-vis the implementation of the Union Government’s scheme for modernization of State police
forces. Apparently, the reason why State Governments have not shown keen interest in the
implementation of the scheme was lack of funds with them as the scheme required them to provide
matching funds. Keeping in view the financial constraints of the States, the Committee endorsed
the switch over from 50:50 pattern of funding to that of 75:25 pattern as recommended by the
Review Committee set up under the Chairmanship of the Special Secretary in the Ministry of Home
Affairs. In response to a query as to whether the Government had switched over to the new
funding pattern suggested by the Review Committee, the Ministry has replied that the Government
has switched over to the new funding pattern form 2003-04. The Review Committee formed to
suggest measures for improved implementation of the Scheme for Modernization of State Police
Forces (MPF Scheme) had recommended for a change in the funding pattern. On the basis of the
Review Committee Report and also on the suggestion of the Ministry of Finance, a proposal was
considered and approved by the Cabinet Committee on Security (CCS) in its meeting held on
22-10-2003. The revised scheme includes change in the funding pattern after grouping the States
into 3 categories, A, Bl and B2- mainly on the basis of level of threat from insurgency/naxalite
militancy/cross border terrorism being faced by the States. Central funding to the States falling
in the categories A, Bl and B2 has been approved as 100%, 75% and 60% respectively from
2003-04. The States falling under these categories are as under:—
Category A : (100% Central funding) : Jammu and Kashmir, Assam, Manipur and Tripura.
Category Bl : (75% Central funding) : Arunachal Pradesh, Bihar, Nagaland, Meghalaya,
‘Mizoram, Sikkim, Andhra Pradesh, Chhattisgarh and Jharkhand.
Category B2 : (60% Central funding)  : Gujarat, Rajasthan, Punjab, Himachal Pradesh,
Uttaranchal, Uttar Pradesh, West Bengal, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Orissa, Goa, Haryana,
Karnataka, Kerala and Tamil Nadu.
28.1 Earlier, the Ministry, in its detailed explanatory note on Demands for Grants (2004-05), had
stated that under this scheme, assistance was provided to the State Governments in the form of
50% Grants-in-aid and 50% loan to be utilized for expenditure of non-recurring nature on purchase
of vehicles, wireless equipment, computers and other sophisticated equipment. The provision under
the scheme in BE 2003-2004 was Rs.650.00 crore (Rs.325.00 crore for Grants-in-aid and
Rs.325.00 crore as Loans and Advances). During the year, a review of the scheme was undertaken

24
and it was decided to give the entire assistance in the form of Grants-in-aid. The loan portion of
Rs.314.50 crore, which remained undisbursed upto December, 2003, was converted into Grants-
in-aid to make the total provision of Rs.739.50 crore. For the year 2004-05, the provision has been
enhanced to Rs.800.00 crore based on the progress in expenditure by State Governments. Out of
this an amount of Rs.300.00 crore has been earmarked for giving Grants-in-Aid in Kind.
28.2 A performance appraisal of the implementation of the scheme during 2000-03 reveals that
there have been variations in the utilization of funds by the States. While some States have done
exceedingly well others have not done so well. Now that the funding pattern of the scheme
has been modified and the conversion of loan component into grant-in-aid from the financial year
2003-04 has been effected, it is hoped that States would strive for 100% utilization of the allocated
fund under the scheme. Besides, the provision of grant-in-aid in kind roughly to the extent of 40%
i.e. Rs.300 crore out of the total funds of Rs.800 crore earmarked for 2004-05, will also improve
the percentage of the utilization of funds.
28.3 The Committee while appreciating the efforts being made by the Ministry of Home
Affairs to make the scheme a success, would like to impress upon the Government that
instead of dividing the States into three categories as mentioned in the preceding
paragraphs, it should divide them into only two categories. The States mentioned in category
B1 should also be clubbed with category ‘A’ States and category ‘B2’ States should be raised
to the level of Category ‘B1’ by changing it to Category ‘B’. Thus there will only be two
Categories A&B. Category ‘A’ (having 100% Central Funding) and Category ‘B’ (having
75% Central Funding) such a revision in the categories of States will place the States
affected by insurgency/naxalite militancy/cross-border terrorism in one category and the
remaining States in the other category.
28.4 As for the funds earmarked for the scheme for 2004-05, the Committee recommends
that the allocation of Rs.800 crore should be raised to the level of Rs.1000 crore as decided
in 2000-01 for the next ten years. The Committee hopes that with the change in funding
pattern, conversion of loan component into grant-in-aid and revision in categories of States,
the utilization percentage should improve drastically. The Committee would like to
recommend that the Ministry of Home Affairs should also evolve a mechanism whereby the
States which have achieved 100% utilization level should be given an additional tranche of
funds subject to a prescribed ceiling in order to meet their further requirements.
28.5 As regards the monitoring of the implementation of the scheme by the State, the
Committee in its 98th Report had recommended that BPR&D should be designated as the
nodal agency for the implementation of the scheme. However, in reply to a query as to
whether there is any technical hitch in appointing BPR&D as the nodal agency to oversee
the implementation of the scheme, the Ministry has stated that BPR&D has already been
nominated as an independent agency to assess the impact of the scheme in all the States.
The Committee expresses its concern over the manner in which its well-considered
recommendation has been dealt with by the Ministry. The Committee in its wisdom had
come to the conclusion that given the BPR&D’s expertise in the field of police research &
development and it being an organization having first hand knowledge vis-a-vis the
modernisational needs of police forces acquired by it on the basis of various studies
conducted by it, it was the most suitable organization to be designated as the nodal agency.
28.6 The Committee while reiterating its recommendation made in its 98th Report,
strongly urges upon the Ministry of Home Affairs not to lose any more time in designating
BPR&D as the nodal agency to oversee the implementation of the scheme for the
modernization of State Police Forces. However, the Ministry may send its representatives
along with BPR&D’s teams on their study visits to the States.

CRIMINAL JUSTICE SYSTEM
29. Crime has taken deep routes in the society and in the last two decades has grown out of
bounds. In order to contain it and to make the society livable mass reforms are required in the
Criminal Justice System. This is an area, which has attracted the attention of intellectuals from
every field. The Government too came into action by constituting a Committee under the
Chairmanship of Dr. (Justice) V .S. Malimath to recommend the measures for revamping the
Criminal Justice System, which submitted its Report on 21 April, 2003. However as yet no reforms
have been made in the area.
29.1 The Ministry, replying to a question, has stated that the report of the Malimath Committee
on reforms in the Criminal Justice System was forwarded to the State Governments/Union
Territories on October 08, 2003 to obtain their comments on the recommendations made by the
Committee. So far the comments of only a few States and Union Territories have been received.
The State Governments are being reminded time and again to forward their comments urgently.
Last reminder was sent from Home Secretary on 1st June, 2004. Some recommendations of the
Malimath Committee relating to amendment of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 and the Indian
Penal Code, 1860 have already been included in the Criminal Law (Amendment) Bill, 2003
introduced in the Rajya Sabha on 22.08.2003. These recommendations are:—
(i) Recommendation No 35 relating to amendment of Section 162 of Cr. PC to require
that the statement should then be read over and got signed by the maker of the
Statement and a copy furnished to him.
(ii) Recommendation No.87 regarding amendment to Section 344 of the Code to require
the court to try the case summarily for perjury and enhancement of punishment
awarded consequent to such summary trial.
(iii) Recommendation No. 106 relating to implementation of 142nd and 154th Reports of
the Law Commission of India in regard to settlement of cases without trial, i.e.,
introduction of the concept of plea bargaining.
(iv) Recommendation No. 118 regarding amendment of Cr.PC to make the offence under
Section 498A of the IPC, bailable and compoundable.
29.2 The Ministry has further stated that the recommendations of the Malimath Committee to
improve the functioning of the institutions of the Criminal Investigation and Prosecution have
recently been forwarded to State Governments on 15th June, 2004.
29.3 Regarding fixing a time-frame for implementing recommendations of the Malimath
Committee, it has been stated by the Home Minister that since Criminal Law and Criminal
Procedure are on the Concurrent List of the Seventh Schedule to the Constitution of India and they
are administered by the State Governments, for effecting major changes to these laws, consultation
with State Governments is essential. Therefore, no specific time frame can be prescribed.
29.4 In the light of its recommendation in its 98th Report to expedite the reform in
Criminal Justice system, the Committee impresses upon the Government to take concrete
steps to get the responses of the States/UTs on priority, may be by calling for a meeting
of Chief Secretaries/Directors General or by some other method, as the situation cannot
wait for the slow response of the State Governments. It also welcomes the Government’s
initiative to introduce the Criminal Law (Amendment) Bill, 2003 for incorporating some of
the recommendations of the Malimath Committee.
DELHI POLICE
30. The implementation of BPR&D’s Study Report on the manpower requirements and

26
modernizational needs of Delhi Police has been engaging the attention of this Committee ever since
BPR&D submitted its Report to the Ministry of Home Affairs on 19 October, 2000. The
Committee, in its 73rd Report (2001-02), 88th Report (2002-03) and 98th Report (2003-04) on
Demands for Grants of Ministry of Home Affairs, has expressed its concern over the manner in
which the Ministry is going about so far as the implementation of this report is concerned.
30.1 Modernization of a police force and augmentation of its manpower is an ongoing process
which enable it to keep pace with the changing security scenario. This applies equally to a CPMF
as also to a civilian police force. The point which the Committee intends to drive back home is
that what is good for a CPMF can also be equally good for a civilian police force. But this basic
principle does not seem to have found favour with the Ministry of Home Affairs. Both CPMFs as
well as Delhi Police fall within the administrative purview of the Ministry and hence should receive
equal attention of it. But as a matter of fact the Ministry adopts double standards when it comes
to the Modernization and augmentation of these two forces. The Committee in its previous Reports
has highlighted this dualistic approach of the Ministry. It is primarily because of this dualistic
approach that on the one hand the Ministry prefers to prepare a five year perspective plan with
a concrete financial commitment duly approved by the Cabinet in case of the modernisation and
augmentation of CPMFs and on the other, the same Ministry asks the BPR&D to prepare a
blueprint for the modernisation of Delhi Police and after receiving its Report moves half-heartedly
when it comes to the implementation of the Report. Instead of chalking out a plan of action to
implement the Report in totality and in a definite time-frame, the Ministry prefers to adopt a
piecemeal approach under the garb of budgetary constraints. What is beyond the comprehension
of this Committee is why no budgetary constraints were allowed to come in the way of the
implementation of the modernization plans of CPMFs and why the pretext of budgetary constraints
is easily taken recourse to by the Ministry in case of Delhi Police? In support of this dualistic
approach, an argument can always be advanced by the Ministry that it has to prioritise its areas
of concern within the available budgetary resources. Presuming this argument on the part of the
Ministry, the Committee wonders if modernisation of Delhi Police is less important than the
modernisation of CPMFs. Given the approach adopted by the Ministry vis-a-vis the implementation
of BPR&D’s Report, it becomes amply clear that it figures at the lowest rung in the scheme of
things of the Ministry. Had this not been so, the Ministry would have gone about the
implementation of the Report in a wholehearted manner.
30.2 Without going into the nitty-gritty of the outcome of the BPRD’s Report on
modernisational needs and manpower requirements of Delhi Police after its submission to
the Ministry of Home Affairs, as it has gone into the details in its 73rd, 88th and 98th
Reports quite exhaustively, the Committee str ongly reiterates its earlier recommendation
that the Ministry of Home Affairs should chalk out a perspective plan with concrete
budgetary allocations for time bound implementation of BPR&D’s Report.
31. Delhi Police being the police force of the capital city of the country should be the model
for the police forces of the rest of the country. For that to happen, Delhi Police should not only
set highest standards of excellence but should also be well trained and well equipped with full
infrastructural support. Thus, apart from the need for better weaponry, sophisticated equipment and
sufficient manpower, there is also an equally essential and important need and that is to provide
well-constructed office buildings for proper and efficient functioning of the police force.
31.1 In this context, the Committee’s attention has been drawn to the fact that out of 127 police
stations of Delhi Police as many as 41 police stations operate from tents/temporary structures. Out
of these 41 police stations, in case of 28 police stations land has been allotted for construction
of their own buildings. As for the construction on the allotted land, there are no indications in the
information furnished to the Committee by the Ministry.

27
31.2 First of all it is really pathetic to know that a large number of police stations of the police

of providing buildings to them, only land has been allotted to them. When construction would begin

that the provision of buildings to the police stations operating from tented/temporary structures is
a matter of least priority for the Ministry of Home Affairs. Evidently, the Ministry seems least
concerned about providing necessary infrastructure to the personnel of Delhi Police.

32. The worsening traffic scenario of Delhi has also been a matter of concern for this

authority. In view of this reply of the Government of NCT of Delhi, the Ministry of Home Affairs
has put the matter on backburner by stating that the matter is being examined by the Government.
32.1 In its 98th Report on Demands for Grants (2003-04) of Ministry of Home Affairs, the

was on it as Delhi was yet to attain full statehood. In its ATR pertaining to this recommendation,
the Ministry again repeated the reply that the matter was under consideration of the Government.
In response to a query based on the ATR furnished by the Ministry as to whether the Government

32.3 The two contradictory replies furnished by the Ministry clearly prove that there is

DISASTER MANAGEMENT

setting up of National Emergency Management Authority would be submitted to the Cabinet for

OBSERV ANCE OF HUMAN RIGHTS IN JAMMU AND KASHMIR
35. Replying to a query of a Member about the observance of Human Rights in J&K, the
Ministry has stated that the Indian security forces were operating strictly within the confines of
the law while dealing with the activities of militants/mercenaries and various Pak-based militant
Tanzeems in J&K. Indian security forces were scrupulously abiding by the operating procedures laid
down for such situations, which were designed to ensure fun respect to the Human Rights
concerns. In addition, First Information Reports were registered by the concerned Police Stations
in respect of every reported case of alleged Human Rights violations and investigations were being
carried out to their logical conclusion. It has also stated that since 1990, till the end of June, 2004,
92 Army/BSF/CRPF personnel were given imprisonment in military custody ranging from 3 months
to 12 years. 5 Army personnel were given imprisonment in military custody ranging from 28 days
to three months. 25 Security Forces were either dismissed or removed form service or
compulsorily retired. 17 Security Forces were reduced in rank and 71 others were awarded other
departmental penalties. In 76 cases suspensions/enquiries/court martial proceedings were pending.
Only in 30 cases security forces’ personnel were exonerated from charges. Recently a court martial
convicted an officer of Captain’s rank to seven year’s Rigorous Imprisonment in a rape case.
35.1 The Ministry has also stated that strict instructions had been issued and reiterated to all
concerned from time to time including the Army and Para-Military Forces to ensure that they
conduct themselves in a humane manner and that alleged cases of excesses were taken serious note
of, inquiries made promptly and firm action taken against those found guilty. Considerable emphasis
was also being laid on police training. In a recently held high level meeting in Ministry of Home
Affairs on 14.02.2004 attended by Chief of Army Staff, Heads of CPMFs and DGP, J&K the
instructions were reiterated to the security forces.

PROTECTION OF MINORITIES IN JAMMU AND KASHMIR
37. On being asked about the measures suggested by the Special Group for Protection of
Minorities, scattered and remote population in Jammu and Kashmir, the Ministry has stated that
terrorism affected districts had mixed population. In these districts minority pockets had been the
targets of terrorists with the objective to create panic, force the minorities and scattered population
to flee to safer places and thereby giving the terrorists space for carrying out their activities
unhindered/unreported, and also to attract international publicity. Though terrorists have targeted
both the majority and minority communities but one of the aims of the Pak sponsored terrorists
has been to try to create a communal divide by indulging in mass killings of the people form
minority community so as to provoke people on communal lines and also to create a sense of fear.
In the past they have resorted to mass killings of members of minority community particularly in
Districts of Doda and Udhampur.
37.1 The M inistry has further stated that the protection of minorities and preventing their further
exodus had assumed vital significance. The Ministry feels that the minorities should not only be
accorded total protection but also be provided with a psychological sense of security. For
protection of minorities, scattered and remote population, the Special Group Report suggested that
any information pertaining to terrorist movements or their concentration in minority-inhabited areas

31
should be expeditiously dealt with. Follow up action after incidents of attacks on the minorities
should be prompt, determined and visibly effective. V olunteer Village Defence Committees should
be encouraged amongst the minorities Regular patrolling of minority inhabited remote areas needed
to be ensured. The security force nearest to a minority hamlet should have a wireless link with
it so that if terrorists strike, then the news reaches the security forces quickly and they could in
keeping with the prescribed SOPs (Standard Operating Procedures) rush to the concerned village.
Villagers in minority hamlets might need to be provided weapons for self-defence on a selective
basis.
37.2 The Ministry has also stated that the Report suggested to further strengthen measures to
inculcate a sense of confidence amongst Members of the minority communities. The Special Group
Report had been accepted recently and the concerned agencies were taking steps to implement
measures suggested above.
37.3 The Committee while applauding the measures being taken by the Government for
the protection of minorities in J&K, recommends that the recommendations as contained
in the Special Group Report should be implemented.

SECURITY OF SENIOR CITIZENS IN DELHI
40. On being asked as to whether Delhi Police was satisfied with the security arrangements for
senior citizens in the city, the Ministry has stated that security of senior citizens had been a cause
of serious concern for Delhi Police. A number of steps were taken in the past to sensitize the
public and focus attention on this problem. However, despite the vigil and alertness maintained by

32
Delhi Police, the problem continued and the vulnerability of the senior citizens was being exploited
by the servants, part time household workers and criminals. The number of senior citizens killed
was 21 in 2002, 18 in 2003 and 13 in 2004.
40.1 The Ministry informed the Committee about the measures taken by Delhi Police to check
crime against senior citizens in Delhi such as:
(a) A senior citizens’ security scheme is in vogue under which senior citizens were
identified;
(b) Beat staff of the police stations visit these senior citizens to reassure them and to
offer them advice on security measures that should be taken by them;
(c) During beat and division patrolling, special vigil maintained around the residences of
senior citizens;
(d) The antecedents of servants and others working with old persons being verified;
(e) The chowkidars of the area had been briefed to keep an eye on the residences of
senior citizens; and
(f) Guidelines had been issued by Delhi Police prescribing Do’s and Don’ts to be
followed by senior citizens.
40.2 Besides, recently more measures were proposed by the Commissioner of Police in
consultation with the Lt. Governor of Delhi which were as follows:
(a) On the pattern of Crime Against Women Cell, a Senior Citizens Cell would be created
at the Police Headquarters under the Additional Commissioner of Police (Crime)
which would be closely supervised by Joint Commissioner of Police (Crime). Its
primary function would be to coordinate and monitor the safety and security of
senior citizens.
(b) A telephone helpline for senior citizens would be available on a round-the-clock basis
in the PCR.
(c) Senior citizens would be re-sensitized through the media to contact police in case
their names do not figure already in the list of senior citizens.
(d) Apart form the usual servant verification, which needed to be undertaken, the
employers would be requested to ask on their own, for at least two prior references
which could be checked on telephone before employing anyone.
40.3 The Committee expresses its deep concern over the rise in the killings of senior
citizens in Delhi and recommends that foolproof measures should be taken by Delhi Police
to provide security to the senior citizens.

OBSERV ATIONS/CONCLUSIONS/RECOMMENDATIONS OF THE COMMITTEE —
AT A GLANCE
CROSS-BORDER INFILTRATION & EXFILTRATION IN JAMMU AND KASHMIR
Against this backdrop, the Committee is of the opinion that our security forces
should intensify their fight against cross border terrorism and infiltration. A decline in the
figures relating to this menace should not be construed as a positive change in the
intentions of the militant organisations as well as their sponsors. While cautioning the
Government about the dangers and challenges that it may have to grapple with in the near
future in Jammu and Kashmir, the Committee once again reiterates its earlier viewpoint
that unless we reduce the porosity of our borders and make it extremely difficult for
infiltrators to sneak into Indian territory we will not be able to put effective checks on
terrorist activities taking place in Jammu and Kashmir. The need for enhanced surveillance
and effective border guarding both along the international border and LoC becomes all the
more essential keeping in view the fact that BSF being the border guarding force, is now
being withdrawn from counter-insurgency operations and therefore can be deployed along
the border to check infiltration and exfiltration. (Para 23.6)
FIDAYEEN ATTACKS ON SECURITY FORCES IN JAMMU AND KASHMIR
The Committee recommends that the Ministry in consultation with the field
commanders of the forces deployed in Jammu and Kashmir, should devise an effective
strategy to provide foolproof security cover to the installations of the forces with a view to
deter and defeat the designs of fidayeen squads. The Ministry should also ensure that no
budgetary constraints are allowed to come in the way of providing necessary personnel and
material support to the forces in their endeavour to insulate themselves against such
attacks as such attacks have a direct bearing on the morale of the forces. (Para 24.1)
NAXALISM
The well considered view of this Committee has been that the Government of India
should come forward to hold a broad, comprehensive and meaningful dialogue with all the
extremist organizations who abjure the path of violence to find a peaceful and lasting
solution to the problem. (Para 25.4)
INDIA RESERVE BATTALIONS
The Committee while noting the manner in which the programme for raising the
additional IR battalions has been pursued by the Ministry of Home Affairs, strongly
recommends that the Ministry should take urgent steps to ensure that all the battalions
sanctioned by it hitherto, should be raised expeditiously without any loss of time. The
Ministry should also set a time-frame for the States to which these battalions have been
sanctioned within which they should be required to raise the sanctioned battalions. As for
the battalions to be sanctioned during the current year, the Ministry should make it clear
before hand to the concerned States that the battalions sanctioned for them should be raised
in a time bound manner failing which any future demand on their part for additional
battalions may not be entertained except for some exceptional circumstances. The Ministry
should impress upon the States that when it comes to maintenance of internal security it

both Governments should make efforts in tandem to see that necessary infrastructure is in

gets defeated and the States continue to depend on the Centre for their requirements of
additional forces. (Para 26.4)

The Committee while appreciating the efforts being made by the Ministry of Home
Affairs to make the scheme a success, would like to impress upon the Government that
instead of dividing the States into three categories as mentioned in the preceding
paragraphs, it should divide them into only two categories. The States mentioned in category

to the level of Category ‘B1’ by changing it to Category ‘B’. Thus there will only be two
