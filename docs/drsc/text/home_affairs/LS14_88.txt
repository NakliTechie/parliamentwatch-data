REPORT
 
The Ministry of Home Affairs has been entrusted broadly with the following functions:
            (a)        To deal with all matters relating to internal security;
(b)        To deal with all matters relating to Centre-State relations and Inter-State relations;
(c)        Implementation of the provisions of the Constitution relating to Official Language and the provisions of
the Official Language Act, 1963;
(d)        To discharge certain basic functions like notification of assumption of office by the President and the
Vice-President; notification of appointment of the Prime Minister and other Ministers; notification of
appointment, resignation and removal of Governors and Lt. Governors, appointment of Lt. Governors,
Chief Commissioners and Administrative Officers in Union Territories;
(e)        To deal with matters like citizenship and naturalisation, census of population, National Anthem, National
Flag, etc.; and
(f)         To deal with all constitutional provisions with respect to the State of Jammu and Kashmir and all other
matters relating to the State of Jammu and Kashmir excluding those with which the Ministry of External
Affairs is concerned.
2.         To discharge the above functions, the Ministry has been divided into five Departments:
(i)         Department of Internal Security – to deal with Police, Law and Order and rehabilitation;
(ii)        Department of States – to deal with Centre-State relations, inter-State relations, Union Territories and
Freedom Fighters’ Pension;
(iii)       Department of Official Language – to deal with the implementation of the provisions of the Constitution
relating to Official Language and provisions of the Official Language Act, 1963;
(iv)       Department of Home – to deal with notification of assumption of office by the President and Vice-
President, notification of appointment of the Prime Minister and other Ministers, etc.; and
(v)        Department of Jammu and Kashmir Affairs – to deal with all constitutional provisions with respect to the
State of Jammu and Kashmir and all other matters relating to the State of Jammu and Kashmir excluding
those with which the Ministry of External Affairs is concerned.
3.         On behalf of the Ministry, ten Demands have been presented to Parliament.
4.         In Vol. I of the Demands for Grants, there are five Demands namely, (i) Demand No. 45– Ministry of Home
Affairs; (ii) Demand No. 46 – Cabinet; (iii) Demand No. 47 – Police; (iv) Demand No. 48 – Other Expenditure of the
Ministry of Home Affairs; and (v) Demand No. 49 – Transfers to Union Territory Governments.
5.         Vol. II of the Demands for Grants relates to Union Territories (without Legislature), namely, Demand No. 98 –
Andaman and Nicobar Islands; Demand No. 99 – Chandigarh; Demand No. 100 – Dadra and Nagar Haveli; Demand No.
101 – Daman and Diu and Demand No. 102 – Lakshadweep. The entire Budget of the Union Territories (without
Legislature) on the Plan as well as Non-Plan side is reflected in the Demands for Grants of the Ministry of Home
Affairs.

12.       Demand Nos. 98, 99, 100, 101 and 102 which are given in Volume-II of the Demands for Grants of the Ministry
of Home Affairs, relate to Union Territories (without Legislature), namely Andaman and Nicobar Islands, Chandigarh,
Dadra & Nagar Haveli, Daman & Diu and Lakshadweep, respectively. The entire budget of the Union Territories
(without Legislature) on the Plan as well as Non-Plan side is reflected in the Demands for Grants of the Ministry. Though
the budget of these Union Territories forms part of the Ministry of Home Affairs, being the nodal Ministry, it, however,
does not dilute the responsibilities of the Ministries of the Central Government from scrutinising and sanctioning the
schemes under their purview. The factual position regarding budgetary procedure in this regard is that the entire budget
requirement for Plan and Non-Plan, revised estimates for current year and Non-Plan budget estimates for the ensuing year
is discussed by the Ministry of Finance in the pre-budget meetings with the representatives of the concerned Union
Territories and Ministry of Home Affairs. An overall ceiling is fixed in respect of each Union Territory. The Planning
Commission decides annual Plan allocations for the ensuing year. The Union Territory Administrations prepare budgetary
break-up showing budget provisions according to their respective budgetary heads and also showing the allocation against
Ministries/Departments of the Central Government administratively concerned. This budgetary break-up is included in
the Demands for Grants of the Ministry of Home Affairs.
 
PRESENTATION OF HOME SECRETARY ON DEMANDS FOR GRANTS (2002-2003)
13.       The Committee considered the Demands for Grants of the Ministry in its meetings held on 2,3,4 and 5 April
2002.  The Home Secretary made a presentation before the Committee on 5 April  2002 with the help of audio-visual
means.
14.       The Home Secretary began his presentation with an overview of internal security situation in the country.  He
apprised the Committee about the progress made on various fronts and the steps and measures taken last year.  He stated
that there were three high points on the graph of terrorism during the year 2001 namely, 11
th September attack on World
Trade Centre and Pentagon in the USA, 1 st October attack on Jammu & Kashmir Legislative Assembly and 13 th
December attack on the Parliament House. He stated that before 11 th September, India’s position regarding Pakistan
sponsored terrorism was accepted in the international forums grudgingly or with skepticism. However, this single event
not only globalized the fight against terrorism but had found India partners in its over decade-old struggle. He further
stated that the attacks on the Parliament House and the Jammu & Kashmir Legislative Assembly were not ordinary
terrorist assaults. These were in the nature of spectacular strikes on the two hubs of Indian democracy, throwing a direct
challenge to the Government of India. The events of the last year had also revived the threat of the likely use of weapons
of mass destruction against the civilized world. He informed the Committee that it was reliably known that certain
terrorist outfits  already possessed some of these weapons or were capable of acquiring them. As a result of which the
threats to internal security from terrorism of various forms – conventional, cyber, nuclear, biological and chemical – were
real and grave and it would not be prudent to take these threats lightly or casually. It would, therefore, be advisable to
take precautions and be ready for all contingencies. He apprised the Committee that in accordance with such threat
perceptions, a total review of the existing disaster prevention and management systems was under way, in consultation
with the State Governments, to locate gaps and deficiencies therein and to initiate a process of filling the gaps and
correcting the deficiencies so identified. He stated that it had already been decided to transfer the subject of disaster
management – except management of droughts and famines – from the Ministry of Agriculture to the Ministry of Home
Affairs. In this regard, a detailed proposal defining structures required for disaster prevention, management and relief was
being prepared and would be submitted shortly for the approval of the Cabinet. He touched upon the minimum action
programme suggested to the States for revamping their machinery for disaster management.
14.1    The Home Secretary, while referring to the communal violence in Gujarat, stated that the visage of the year 2002
got scarred by the unprecedented communal violence in Gujarat which left nearly 760 persons dead and nearly 1900

injured. The number of persons rendered homeless was around 98000. He stated that there was, however, a silver lining
that while incidents of communal clashes continued in Gujarat, bandhs organized in various other States passed off
almost peacefully so did the Shiladaan ceremony at Ayodhya on 15 March 2002. 
15.       Thereafter, the Home Secretary gave a detailed description of the internal security trends in vulnerable and
sensitive parts of the country.
16.       With regard to the situation in Jammu & Kashmir, the Home Secretary stated that between 1990 and 2001 the
proxy war unleashed by Pakistan in Jammu & Kashmir had consumed nearly 27500 lives in 52000 incidents. Out of
27500 deaths, 10000 were civilians, 3000 security personnel and 14500 terrorists. During this period of 12 years,
terrorists destroyed 633 educational buildings, 333 bridges, 11 hospitals, 1134 Government buildings, 10263 private
houses and 1932 shops. The graph of terrorism in Jammu & Kashmir showed an increasing trend between 1990 and 1996.
After that it started decreasing which continued till 2000. He further submitted that last year there was an upsurge in
terrorist activities registering 4522 incidents compared to 3074 incidents in 2000. This upsurge could be partly ascribed to
the one-sided cease fire declared by the Government of India in November, 2000 and partly to the fact that the terrorists
were getting desperate under pressure of the Pakistani establishment on the one-side and of the Indian security forces on
the other. As a result, 919 civilians and 536 security personnel lost their lives during 2001. He stated that the Ramzan
peace initiative announced on 27 November 2000 by the Government of India had to be withdrawn on 31 May 2001 after
a patient and testing wait of six months. He further elaborated that during 2001 two features of terrorism in Jammu &
Kashmir became particularly noticeable. One, the attacks were more focused on the security forces and their
establishments and second, that the number of suicide attacks was on the increase. Last year there were 25 fidayeen
(suicide) attacks. This apart, terrorists had also tried to provoke communal feelings by holding up in mosques  hoping that
people would react if security forces attacked the mosques to dislodge the terrorists. However, such tactics had failed. He
informed that there was attack on Amarnath Yatris for second consecutive year. 
16.1     The Home Secretary informed that in addition to six districts of Kashmir Division, all districts of Jammu Division
were declared as ‘disturbed’ areas in 2001 by the Government of Jammu & Kashmir.  He stated that terrorist groups not
only continued to operate from Pakistani soil but also continued to get assistance from the ISI and the Pakistani army.
Contrary to its promises, Pakistan had done nothing to effectively restrict the movement, funding, training or recruitment
of terrorism. All important terrorist organizations, including those which were banned by Pakistan, continued to have
offices not only in Pakistan occupied Kashmir (PoK) but also in Pakistan. In order to deceive the international
community, the banned terrorist organizations had been re-christened   like Lashkar-e-Toiba was now known as Pasban-
e-Ahle Hadith and Jaish-e-Mohammed as Tehrik-ul-Furkan. However, within Jammu & Kashmir, they continued to use
their original names. Referring to the Government’s present strategy in Jammu & Kashmir, he stated that besides taking
on the terrorist outfits in a direct combat, it was focused on further deepening of democratic process and sustenance of
developmental process over a period of time so that public opinion and energy were mobilized against terrorism.
17.       While apprising the Committee of the situation in North-East, the Home Secretary stated that the level of violence
in the region had declined, though the internal security situation particularly in the States of Assam, Manipur and Tripura
continued to be disturbed. There were also disturbing signs of an upswing in violence in Meghalaya and Arunachal
Pradesh. During the year 2001, there were 1339 incidents of violence compared with 1963 incidents in 2000. During
2001, 600 civilians and 175 security personnel were killed in terrorist violence as against the figures of 907 civilians and
164 security personnel during 2000. Last year 572 militants were killed compared to 584 killed the year before. Arms
looted by militants were 111 in 2001 as compared to 216 in 2000. The number of arms recovered was 577 in 2001 as
compared to 574 in 2000. He stated that most serious incidents of violence took place in Manipur in the aftermath of the
announcement of extension of ceasefire agreement between the Government of India and the NSCN (IM) without
territorial limits in the North-East. The announcement evoked widespread resentment in Manipur as also in Assam and
Arunachal Pradesh. In deference to the public sentiments, the Government announced restoration of status quo ante which
led to the return of normalcy. The cause of such unprecedented violence lay in the apprehension in the minds of the
people of Manipur that the extension of ceasefire to their areas might lead to a territorial division of this State at some
point of time in future. Referring to the talks with NSCN (IM), the Home Secretary informed that the talks on substantive
issues had already begun with NSCN (IM). A set of demands received from the NSCN (IM) was under consideration of
the Government. He further stated that the Government of India entered into a formal ceasefire with the NSCN (K) for a
period of one year w.e.f. 28 April 2001 on persistent demands of the Government of Nagaland, NGOs and Church
leaders. He hoped that the signing of the ceasefire agreement would enlarge the area of peace in Nagaland. As regards the
talks with Bodo Liberation Tigers (BLT) in Assam, he stated that talks with that group were continuing and the level of
violence attributable to this militant outfit had come down substantially.

17.1     He informed that an amount of Rs. 139.77 crore  had been released as Security Related Expenditure (SRE) during
the financial year 2001-2002. Sharing his anxiety, the Home Secretary stated that an understanding and nexus was
emerging among the militant outfits active in Assam, Meghalaya, Nagaland and Tripura. This was partly on account of
the compulsion of these outfits for alternative sanctuaries and camps and partly on account of their need for weapons and
training. He assured the Committee that the Government had taken note of this development for being factored into its
strategy for the North-East.
18.       With regard to left wing extremism, the Home Secretary stated that the magnitude as well as the intensity of left
wing extremist violence registered an upsurge for the first time since 1996 with 1208 incidents and 564 deaths reported
during 2001. In 2000, the number of incidents was 1179 and number of lives lost 550. States of Andhra Pradesh,
Jharkhand and Chhattisgarh witnessed a spurt in naxalite violence while there was a comparative decline in Bihar. There
was also a significant increase in the level of naxalite violence in States of Madhya Pradesh and Orrisa. He submitted that
there were sure signs that left wing extremism was spreading its tentacles in the States of Uttar Pradesh and West
Bengal.   He informed that Andhra Pradesh accounted for a larger proportion of naxalite violence as compared to Bihar

26.1     He informed the Committee that the report of the Group of Ministers (GoM) on National Security was considered
and accepted by the Government in May 2001.  The process of implementation of the recommendations of the GoM had
been set in motion and was being closely monitored at the level of Cabinet Secretary.    The recommendations pertained

apparatus’ included the establishment of an Intelligence Coordination Group (ICG) and a Technology Coordination
Group (TCG) working in close tandem with National Technical Facility Organisation (NTFO).  Giving details of each of

apex level and inter alia deal with (i) annual tasking for intelligence collection; (ii) overseeing the functioning of

facilities and equipment by the intelligence agencies.  It would generally oversee their TECHINT capabilities and also
examine issues relating to the allocations of fund for this purpose.  NTFO would inter alia plan, design, set up and operate
new strategic and expensive TECHINT facilities as approved by TCG keeping in view the rapid convergence now taking
place among hitherto different technologies.  The Home Secretary further informed that the GoM had recommended
constitution of Joint Task Force on Intelligence and a Multi-Agency Centre in the Intelligence Bureau Headquarters as
well as Inter-State Intelligence Support Teams and Multi-Agency Centres in the States also under the aegis of the
Intelligence Bureau, to ensure better coordination between the Central and the State intelligence agencies.

LEFT WING EXTREMISM
43.       Left wing extremism has been posing one of the biggest challenges on the internal security front.  Several States,
namely, Andhra Pradesh, Bihar, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Orissa, Uttar Pradesh, West Bengal and two of newly
created States of Jharkhand and Chhattisgarh have been bearing the brunt of violence unleashed by the protagonists of
this kind of extremism viz. CPML-PW and MCC.  These groups have been making concerted efforts to militarise their
cadres through formation of special guerilla squads and extend their activities over larger areas of the country.  Not just
that, they are now forging links with Communist Party of Nepal (Maoist).  These groups have even envisaged a Compact
Revolutionary Zone (CRZ) by linking up their strongholds spread across Telangana, Dandkaranya, Bihar, Jharkhand and
Chhattisgarh in India and areas of influence of Communist Party of Nepal (CPN-Maoist).  The violence unleashed by
these extremist groups has seen an upsurge in 2001 after having been in doldrums since 1996.  The Ministry of Home
Affairs has informed that some of the violent incidents occurred in 2001, were due to the protest actions of PWG and
MCC for declaring them as terrorist organizations under the Prevention of Terrorism Ordinance, 2001. 
43.1     In reply to a query as to what measures had been taken to quell the spurt in the violence, the Ministry of Home
Affairs replied that ‘Public Order’ and ‘Police’ being State subjects, it was for the concerned State Governments to devise
various methods and take concrete steps to curb left wing extremist activities in the States.  However, keeping in view the
overall dimensions of left wing extremism, a Coordination Centre headed by the Union Home Secretary with Chief
Secretaries and Directors General of Police of the seriously affected States, as its members, regularly reviewed and
coordinated steps taken by the States to check left wing extremist activities, monitored the plan of action in respect of
each State and made recommendations both on development and security aspects of the problem.  Several important
decisions such as providing financial support from the Central Government for combating left wing extremist activities,
preparation of plans for development of affected areas, to tackle the problem in a holistic manner, sharing of intelligence
inputs, and providing assistance of para military forces on need basis, etc. had been taken by the Coordination Centre and
effectively followed-up.  CPML-Peoples War (PW) and Maoist Communist Centre (MCC) and their formations and front
organizations had been notified as Terrorist Organisations under the provisions of the Prevention of Terrorism (Second)
Ordinance 2001 to enable the States to take effective action against them.  In order to curb Maoist activities on the Indian
soil along Indo-Nepal Border, Special Service Bureau (SSB) personnel had been deployed in the border States of
Uttaranchal, Uttar Pradesh, Bihar and West Bengal covering the districts of Pithoragarh, Champawat, Udham Singh
Nagar of Uttaranchal, Pilibhit and part of Lakhimpur Kheri of Uttar Pradesh, Purbi Champaran, Madhubani, Piprakoti,
Kishanganj, Sitamarhi, Supaul of Bihar and Darjeeling in West Bengal.  State Governments had also been advised to gear
up their intelligence machinery for collection and sharing of intelligence from the field, particularly with regard to
possible plans of sympathizers of CPN (M) to hold meetings, raise funds and organize logistic support.  States had also
been advised that any attempts of Maoist elements to seek sanctuary would have to be firmly deterred and a close watch
kept on movement of arms, ammunition and explosives to and from Nepal.
43.2     The Home Secretary, in his presentation before the Committee, also informed that the magnitude as well as the
intensity of left wing extremist violence registered an upsurge for the first time since 1996 with 1208 incidents and 564
deaths reported during 2001.

MONSTER OF COMMUNALISM
45.       Communalism has been the bane of modern India.  It acted as a midwife in the partition of India and even after
that it continues to plague the social fabric of the country.  It has acquired different forms and dimensions over the years. 
However, in the recent years it is rearing its ugly head in an organized manner much in the same way as it did in the years
preceding the partition.  There are several forces which work tirelessly to ensure that it does not lose it momentum.  These
forces are the real anti-national elements who are bent upon tearing the social fabric of the nation apart.  In their zeal to
gain foothold in their respective communities, these forces are stoking communal fire.  Their actions are spreading venom
in the society which threatens to be divided along communal lines.  These attempts to create a communal divide in the
society are one of the biggest challenges before the Indian polity.  Therefore, it needs to be nipped in the bud before it
grows too large to be controlled.  It is the considered view of this Committee that the Government must make honest and
sincere efforts to maintain the pluralistic character of the society and bring all right thinking elements of all communities
together to try and find ways to check the growing divide between them.
POLITICISATION OF POLICE FORCES
46.       Indian police system is a relic of the British colonialism.  Various Commissions and Committees set up from time
to time to suggest the measures necessary to purge the system have come out with a plethora of suggestions and
recommendations.  These recommendations have not at all been implemented  by some States and where they have been
implemented their letter and spirit has not been followed.  As a result of that, Indian police system continues to be
plagued by several ills.  Some of these ills were inherited by it from its colonial masters while some have crept into it as it
has evolved in the post-colonial period.  In the post-colonial period, it has been made to subserve the ruling elite.  This
has   raised  a  big  question mark over the  objectivity and
impartiality of the police.  Today we have a police which is politicized and politically polarized.  For it has become a
pawn in the hands of its masters.  In return, policemen get political patronage which has become essential for their
survival.  These days police personnel are found to be divided in camps with each camp having its own political leanings. 
This connivance of the police with the powers that be is giving rise to cynicism among people who are not only losing
faith in the police but also in the democratic principles of our polity.  These are, by all means, very dangerous signs for
the continuance and survival of democracy.  What can be more dangerous than a situation when people are losing faith in
as vital an institution as the police?
47.       In this backdrop, the Committee feels that there is need for a serious introspection by all concerned.  The
Government must act fast to stem the rot which has set in.  If immediate steps are not taken to set the house in order, there
are dangers of the system falling prey to these degenerating developments.  The Committee, therefore, recommends that
the Government should make earnest efforts to depoliticise the institution of police before it becomes too late to retrieve it
from the morass of degeneration.  It must be ensured that police remains accountable only to the law of the land.

REVAMP OF INTELLIGENCE APPARATUS
51.       Large scale intrusion of foreign mercenaries and Pak army regulars in Kargil in 1999 woke up the Indian
establishment to the need for a systemic overhaul of the intelligence set up.  Intrusions in Kargil proved a watershed
development for the overhaul of intelligence apparatus.  In the aftermath of Kargil war, a Group of Ministers (GoM) on
National Security was constituted to deliberate upon various issues and questions relating to the national security in a
comprehensive manner.  The report of the GoM was considered and accepted by the Government in May 2001.  The
Report focused on the areas of intelligence apparatus, internal security, border management and defence.  As regards
intelligence apparatus, the GOM has recommended the setting up of an Intelligence Coordination Group (ICG), a
Technology Coordination Group (TCG) and a National Technical Facility Organisation (NTFO).  The establishment of
these groups is aimed at ensuring better flow of intelligence inputs and providing strategic facilities and equipment.  In
addition to this, the GoM has also recommended the constitution of a Joint Task Force on Intelligence and a Multi-
Agency Centre in the Intelligence Bureau Headquarters as well as Inter-State Intelligence Support Teams and Multi-
Agency Centres in the States under the aegis of the Intelligence Bureau.  This entire paraphernalia is being put in place to
see that intelligence apparatus of the country is fully geared up, equipped and vigilant to ward off the nefarious designs of
divisive forces and the enemies.
52.       The Committee is satisfied to note the steps being taken to revamp the entire intelligence apparatus.  It hopes that
the new set-up will function smoothly.  However, with too many groups and agencies entrusted with different aspects of
intelligence, the real problem arises in regard to proper coordination amongst all of them.  The Committee hopes that all

88th Report
file:///D/Committee%20on%20Home%20Affairs/88threport.htm[30-06-2016 17:51:18]
the finer details vis-à-vis role, functions and structure of each and every functional group and agency have been worked
out so that there are least chances of overlap and conflict of functions.  In this context, the Committee would like to lay
emphasis on one more and equally important aspect of accountability.  With so many functional units in place, there are
situations when the proverbial game of passing the buck starts as each unit points the finger towards the other to save its
skin.    The Committee, therefore, is of the view that a proper system of accountability should be evolved whereby it is
easier to fix the responsibility and follow it up with necessary action.
ISI-BACKED RELIGIOUS FUNDAMENTALIST INSTITUTIONS
53.       Pakistan’s Inter-Services Intelligence (ISI) has been marshalling all its resources to destabilize India.  In order to
set its sinister designs against India into motion, it has spun a web of its operatives across the length and breadth of the
country.  These operatives with the help of their local connections work ceaselessly not just to foment trouble but also to
aid and abet troublemakers.  As part of this strategy, ISI is providing financial assistance towards setting up of religious
fundamentalist institutions along Indo-Nepal border.  These institutions not only provide safe sanctuaries to ISI’s
handpicked men but also indoctrinate young minds to wage a holy war against India.  In other words, these institutions
have become breeding ground for anti-India elements.  The Ministry of Home Affairs, in response to a query as to
whether  it was a fact that some ISI-backed fundamentalist institutions/organizations had sprung up along Indo-Nepal 
border, stated that reports had been received from various security agencies from time to time about the functioning of
ISI-backed religious institutions/ organizations along Indo-Nepal border.  Some of these institutions were reportedly
being misused by ISI activists for subversive activities against India.
54.       The Committee is concerned to note that some religious fundamentalist institutions are working at the beck and
call of the ISI to destabilize India.  The audacity with which ISI is using both Indian people and soil, that too for
destabilizing and disintegrating India, is really condemnable.  The Committee, therefore, urges upon the Government to
leave no stone unturned to root out such institutions and the activists being harboured by them   before any irreparable
damage is caused to the unity and integrity of India.
HOUSING FOR CENTRAL PARA MILITARY FORCES
55.       Housing needs of the personnel of Central Para Military Forces have been a matter of concern for this Committee. 
For last couple of years, it has been urging upon the Government to accord topmost priority to the housing requirements
of the forces.  However, housing requirements of these forces continue to be a victim of either shortage of funds or
inability of the Central Public Works Department (CPWD) to utilize the allocated funds thereby leaving the housing
projects incomplete.  So far as the problem of paucity of funds is concerned, it was addressed  to some extent in the
budget estimates for the year 2001-2002 when Rs. 255.20 crore (both plan and non-plan) were earmarked for the
purpose.  However, the allocations were reduced at RE stage in respect of CRPF, BSF, CISF and Assam Rifles as the
funds earmarked could not be utilized.  In response to a query as to what were the reasons for under-utilisation of funds
by these CPMFs, the Ministry of Home Affairs inter alia stated that it was mainly due to the inability of CPWD to utilize
the allocated funds as  it was not in a position to complete the target works.  As a result, funds remain under-utilised and
the satisfaction level of residential accommodation remains static.   Moreover, a large number of projects for construction
of residential accommodation in respect of almost all the CPMFs are running behind schedule.  Somehow, these CPMFs
have not been able to achieve the target of 14% satisfaction level.  As informed by the Home Secretary in his presentation
before the Committee, the satisfaction level has now been revised to 25% in pursuance to the recommendations of the
Group of Ministers (GoM).  To achieve this revised target, Rs.4000 crore will be required to construct around one lakh
residential units in the next five years as part of the augmentation and modernization of CPMFs.  The Ministry of Home
Affairs will be approaching the Planning Commission as well as the Ministry of Finance for allocation of funds under
both plan and non-plan heads.
56.       Having all said and done, the question arises as to what is the rationale behind allocating funds for a scheme when
they cannot be fully utilized for the intended purposes?  It has now been proved beyond any shadow of doubt that CPWD
has been a total failure in executing the construction projects of CPMFs.  Therefore, some other agencies are required to
be engaged for construction of these forces.  The Home Secretary, in his presentation before the Committee, had informed
that as per GFR-136, all construction works of the Central Government were required to be executed by CPWD.  The
Ministry of Home Affairs had taken up a case for general relaxation of GFR-136 for the construction projects of CPMFs
with the Ministry of Urban Development.  The latter had agreed to provide a relaxation of the said rule in cases of
housing projects of CPMFs located in remote areas of the country like the North East where CPWD did not have adequate
staff to undertake such projects.

INDO-TIBETAN BORDER POLICE
58.       Indo-Tibetan Border Police (ITBP) is doing excellent job in difficult terrain under inhospitable conditions to
safeguard India’s frontiers.  Its personnel are displaying utmost courage and valour in securing the borders.  Apart from
its primary role of guarding Indo-Tibetan Border, ITBP is playing a crucial role in maintenance of internal security,
disaster management, VIP security, etc.  The services rendered by it to the people have earned it the distinction of being
‘friend of the hill people’.  However, it is over-stretched to the extent that its reserve battalions are also deployed which is
adversely affecting its operational preparedness, relief, recuperation and training.  It is, therefore, imperative that five
reserve battalions of ITBP should be released.  Accordingly, the Committee recommends that the Ministry of Home
Affairs should make earnest efforts to see that reserve battalions of ITPB are released in order to sustain its high degree of
operational efficiency.
59.       In order to add technological edge to its operational efficiency, ITBP requires the induction of hi-tech equipment,
gadgetry and weaponry such as Battle Field Surveillance Radars, Pistal Glock 17, MP5 or INSAS5.56 CM, High Power
Telescope, Higher Inputs of Satellite Imagery in Border Management, Snow Scooters, Power Boards (for patrolling
Pangong Tso), Mobile Surveillance Vehicle Systems, Thermal Imager, Laser Range Finders, Global Positioning System
(GPS), Dry Zeroing Device and IED Jammer.  For improving its communiction network, it requires the induction of V-
SAT terminals (under POLNET), radio Jammers, BEST (Burst-Error System for Teleprinter), SMART (Spurt Message
Alpha Numeric Radio Terminal) and Mini Automax (Automatic Message Switching System), communication
interceptors and radio direction finders.  The Committee has always held the view that the needs of CPMFs for
sophisticated weaponry and gadgetry should be given utmost priority.  In the case of ITBP, it had earlier recommended
that it should be provided with state-of the-art surveillance equipment for maintaining a vigil on Banihal tunnel located on
Jammu-Srinagar highway.  The Committee, therefore, feels that the needs of ITBP for hi-tech weaponry, gadetary, and
equipment as mentioned above should be fulfilled by the Government on priority basis.
VACANCIES IN BPR&D
60.       Bureau of Police Research and Development (BPR&D) is the premiere institution in the field of police research. 
Since its inception, it has carried out 132 research projects on various aspects of police administration.  It is compared to
Research Development and Statistical Directorate of the United Kingdom and National Institute of Justice of the United
States of America.  Its performance and track record in its areas of specialization is impressive.  But it is really
unfortunate to note that 106 of its scientific posts are lying vacant.  Out of 106, 39 posts are of Group ‘A’ level.  The
Committee is of the view that all scientific posts especially Group ‘A’ level posts of BPR&D should be filled up at the
earliest so as to ensure that its functions are not hampered due to depletion of its manpower.
DELHI POLICE
61.       The Bureau of Police Research & Development (BPR&D) had been entrusted with the task of studying the
manpower requirements and modernization needs of Delhi Police.  The Bureau submitted its report on 19 October 2000. 
The report was sent to the Lt. Governor of Delhi for examination at his end.  In the meantime, while examining the report
in the Ministry it was noticed that a number of critical issues had not been addressed adequately and some additional
information was called for from Delhi Police.  Accordingly, a Committee was set up in the Ministry of Home Affairs to
examine the report in association with the BPR&D and Delhi Police.  A further action on BPR&D’s report was expected
only after the receipt of additional inputs from the Ministry’s in-house Committee and from the Lt. Governor, Delhi.
62.       In response to a query as to whether the advice of the Lt. Governor of Delhi on the report of BPR&D had been
received, the Ministry of Home Affairs replied that the Lt. Governor of the National Capital Territory of Delhi, to whom a
copy of the BPR&D Study Report was forwarded for advice, expressed the view that it was necessary to lay emphasis on
greater professionalism and reform management as mere increase in the size of the Force was not a progressive step.  He

65.       While going into the details of traffic management in Delhi during the examination of Demands for Grants (2001-
2002), the Committee, in its 73 rd Report thereon, had recommended for setting up of a Unified Traffic Management
Authority in Delhi.  The Ministry of Home Affairs had referred the recommendations to the Government of NCT of Delhi
for obtaining their considered views to enable it to take the decision in the matter in its proper perspective.  In reply to a
query as to what follow up action had been taken by the Government of NCT of Delhi in regard to the setting up of the
recommended authority, the Ministry of Home Affairs informed that the Government of NCT of Delhi had with regard to
the recommendations for setting up of a Unified Transport Management Authority, informed that it was not possible for
them under the existing frame-work to bring about an integrated legislation for setting up of a Unified Transport
Management Authority as Delhi Legislative Assembly was not empowered to legislate with respect to matters connected
with “Police” and “Land”.  The Ministry further informed that it had, however, accepted the recommendation made by
BPR&D in its study report for setting up of a Traffic Advisory Committee under the Chairmanship of the Commissioner
of Police with the representatives drawn from the Government of NCT of Delhi, local bodies and Delhi Development
Authority to continuously monitor such aspects of road engineering and road furniture as help smooth flow of traffic.
66.       The proposal to set up a Traffic Advisory Committee under the Chairmanship of Commissioner of Police, Delhi
falls short of an independent Traffic Management Authority.  The idea behind setting up of such an authority was to
ensure that traffic management was viewed with an integrated approach.  However, that idea seems to have not gone
down well with the policy makers of the Ministry of Home Affairs.  By linking it with a somewhat identical
recommendation as contained in the report of BPR&D, the Ministry has sought to put the setting up of the authority also
on the backburner.  Therefore, the Committee strongly reiterates its earlier recommendation as contained in its 73
rd
Report that the Government should set up a Unified Traffic Management Authority vested with the powers of overall
planning and management of traffic in Delhi.

of Delhi Police because of their commitments elsewhere.  To overcome this hurdle in raising the satisfaction level, the
Committee had recommended for setting up of a Housing Corporation for Delhi Police.  As the proposal did not find
favour with the Ministry of Home Affairs, the Committee endorsed the proposal of the Ministry to carve out a separate
dedicated wing out of the PWD to cater to the housing needs of Delhi Police.  However, this proposal too could not

satisfaction level of Delhi Police in the next couple of years to a fairly satisfactory level.
SITUATION IN PUNJAB
68.       The Home Secretary, in his presentation, apprised the Committee that the recent inputs indicated that Pak based
terrorists of Khalistan Commando Force (Panjwar), Babbar Khalsa International, Khalistan Zindabad Force and
International Sikh Youth Federation (Rode) were desperate to undertake some sensational terrorist action in India because
of constant pressure from Pak ISI.  Attempts to resurrect terrorism in Punjab also became evident from the continued
recovery of large quantities of arms, ammunition, explosives and sophisticated timer devices in the State.  He informed
that the security forces arrested 31 terrorists while one surrendered before the police in 2001.  Similarly, 35 terrorists were
arrested and 3 surrendered in 2000.  He further stated that despite reverses and neutralization of some of their top rung
activists over the years, the Khalistani terrorists still retained the capacity and substantial fire power to carry out acts of
violence.
69.       In view of these developments, the Committee feels that there is a subtle change which is taking place at the
ground level.  Therefore, there is need for being vigilant to ensure that terrorism does not rear its head once again in the
State of Punjab.  The Committee, therefore, urges upon the Government to take all necessary preventive measures to
thwart attempts of Pak-ISI to revive terrorism in Punjab.
70.       In this context, the Committee also draws the attention of the Government to the concern expressed by Members
regarding the plight of 400 odd policemen of Punjab facing trial in various courts for their role in anti-terrorism
operations during the heyday of terrorism in Punjab.  The trial of these policemen may have a demoralizing effect on the
policemen in general and those fighting terrorism in particular.  It becomes even more demoralizing when persons, who
spearheaded the terrorist movement from their foreign bases, are not only being allowed entry into the country but also to
move freely.  Are these recently returned separatist leaders not a threat to the security and unity of the country? 
Ironically, those who indulged in terrorism are moving freely and in sharp contrast those who fought terrorism are going
through hell of an experience.  In view of this, the Committee urges upon the Government to consider the cases of these
400 odd policemen and simultaneously cautions it to keep a strict vigil on the activities and movement of the recently
returned separatist leaders so that they do not become once again, a potent threat to the security and unity of the country.

process of dialogue, at this stage, appears to be a step in the right direction because each passing day will add a

MONSTER OF COMMUNALISM
*          Communalism has been the bane of modern India.  It acted as a midwife in the partition of India and even after
that it continues to plague the social fabric of the country.  It has acquired different forms and dimensions over the
years.  However, in the recent years it is rearing its ugly head in an organized manner much in the same way as it
did in the years preceding the partition.  There are several forces which work tirelessly to ensure that it does not
lose it momentum.  These forces are the real anti-national elements who are bent upon tearing the social fabric of
the nation apart.  In their zeal to gain foothold in their respective communities, these forces are stoking communal
fire.  Their actions are spreading venom in the society which threatens to be divided along communal lines.  These
attempts to create a communal divide in the society are one of the biggest challenges before the Indian polity. 
Therefore, it needs to be nipped in the bud before it grows too large to be controlled.  It is the considered view of
this Committee that the Government must make honest and sincere efforts to maintain the pluralistic character of
the society and bring all right thinking elements of all communities together to try and find ways to check the
growing divide between them.                                      (Para 45)
