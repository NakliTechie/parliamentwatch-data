R E P O R T
            I, the Chairman of the Department-related Parliamentary Standing Committee on Home Affairs, having been
authorised by it, present this Sixty-third Report of the Committee.  The Report pertains to the Salaries, Allowances,
Leave and Pensions of  Officers and Servants of the Delhi High Court Bill, 1994 *
.  The Bill, as per its Statement of
Objects and Reasons, seeks to rectify the  serious anomalies that had crept into the pay scales of the employees  of  the
Delhi High Court on account of various judgements and orders passed by the Courts in various Writ Petitions filed by
the  employees.
2.         Pursuant to the provisions of the Rules relating to the Department-related Parliamentary Standing Committee on
Home Affairs, the Chairman, Rajya Sabha, referred§
 the Bill, as introduced in the Rajya Sabha on 19 August, 1994 and
pending therein, to the Committee for examination and report.
3.0       The Committee (1994-95) considered the Bill in its meeting held on 29 November, 1994 and decided to invite
views/suggestions of the Employees’ Welfare Association of the Delhi High Court on the provisions contained in the
Bill. The Delhi High Court Employees Welfare Association had submitted vide communication dated 28 November,
1994 their views/suggestions on the various provisions of the Bill for consideration of the Committee and requested for
providing an opportunity to give oral evidence on the provisions of the Bill before it.  It was decided to hear the
representatives of the Association at an appropriate stage.  
3.1       While the Bill was still under consideration of the Committee, the Tenth Lok Sabha got dissolved and so did the
previous Committee.
3.2       Meanwhile, the matter relating to service conditions of the employees of the Court was referred to the Fifth
Central Pay Commission.

3.3       The Committee (1996-97), which was constituted on 1August, 1996,   further  considered  the Bill in its meeting

3.5       In 1997, the Fifth Central Pay Commission in its report (para 103.32), recommended as follows :-
(a)         The entire question of conditions of service, including pay scales,     
allowances, etc. of the officers and employees and employees of the     Supreme Court of India and    the
High Court of Delhi has to be decided in the manner laid
down in the Constitution of India, as interpreted in the various judgements of the highest court    in the
land.
(b)
            The Fifth Central Pay Commission finds itself unable to make any recommendations whatsoever for the
officers and employees of the Supreme Court of India and High Court of Delhi.
(c)        However, we have made our detailed recommendations on pay scales, allowances, pensions, etc. with
regard to different categories of Central Government employees.  We would be happy if these
recommendations are of any assistance to the Hon’ble Chief Justice of India and the Hon’ble Chief
Justice of the Delhi High Court, as background materials for basing their own decisions.
Before we close this discussion, we would like to conclude with one last respectful submission. 
In case the Hon’ble Courts decide to grant pay scales, allowances, etc. to their own employees that are at
variance with the pay scales, allowances, etc. of Central Government employees on the ground that their
qualifications, duties and responsibilities, are different, they may, if they so desire, modify the
designations of such posts to make these different from the designations used in the Central Government
so as to avoid any confusion in the future.

4.         The Statement of Objects and Reasons of the Bill reads as under :-
Serious anomalies have crept into the pay scales of  the employees of the High Court of Delhi on account
of  various judgements and orders passed by the Court in Writ Petitions filed by its employees.  These
distortions and anomalies in the pay structures of the staff  of Delhi High Court are likely to cause serious
agitation amongst similarly placed staff of the Central Government and the Government of the National
Capital Territory of Delhi. 
This might ultimately lead to large scale distortions in the pay structure of the employees of the
Central Government and the Government of National Capital Territory of Delhi  with attendant serious
consequential financial implications.  The Government examined the implications of the emerging
distortions in the pay structure of Court employees and considered it appropriate to enact suitable
legislation to rectify the situation as that was the only remedy available to the Government.
5.0       The High Court of Delhi has jurisdiction over the Union Territory of Delhi, now known as the National Capital
Territory of Delhi.  The NCT of Delhi has its own Legislative Assembly  and a Council of Ministers under the
Government of National Capital Territory of Delhi Act, 1991. 
5.1       Article 229(2) of the Constitution provides that –
Subject to the provisions of any law made by the Legislature of the State, the conditions of services of officers and
servants of a High Court shall be such as may be prescribed byrules made by the Chief Justice of the Court or by some
other judge or officer of the    Court authorised by the Chief Justice to make rules for the purpose:
 
Provided that the rules made under this clause shall, so far as they relate to salaries, allowances, leave or pensions,
require the approval of the Governor of the State.
5.2       The Delhi High Court  Act, 1966 constituted the High Court of Delhi.  Section 4(1)(d) (ii) of the Act of 1966
which relates to Article 229 provides as follows:
The references to the State Public Service Commission, the Legislature of the State and the Consolidated
Fund of the State shall be construed, respectively, as references to the Union Public Service Commission,
Parliament and the Consolidated Fund of India.
5.3       The Government of NCT Act, 1991 did not make any modification in the Delhi High Court Act of 1966 and,
therefore, Parliament continues to be the State Legislature for the Delhi High Court under the meaning of Article 229(2)
of the Constitution.

5.4       Article 239AA of the Constitution which was inserted by the Constitution (Sixty-ninth Amendment) Act, 1991
relates to special provisions with respect to Delhi.  Although under sub-clause 3(a) of Article 239AA, the Legislative
Assembly of Delhi has power to make laws with respect to officers and servants of the Delhi High Court, but under sub-
clause 3(b) of  Article 239AA,  Parliament has been empowered to make laws with respect to any matter for a Union
Territory or any part thereof.
5.5
              In pursuance of the constitutional provisions the Chief Justice of Delhi High Court framed rules, namely, the
Delhi High Court Officers and Servants (Salaries, Allowances, Leave and Pensions) Rules, 1970.  Under these rules, the
officers and servants of the Delhi High Court were placed at par with corresponding employees of the Government of
NCT, Delhi and the pay scales were accordingly fixed on the standard Central Government pattern which is being
followed in the Government of NCT, Delhi.
6.0       In the Background Note, the Department of Justice has stated that before the setting up of  Delhi High Court in
1966, a Bench of the Punjab High Court was functioning at Delhi.  The salaries, allowances etc. of the employees
working in this Bench were being governed by the rules framed by the Chief Justice of the Punjab High Court.  Even
after the establishment of a separate High Court for Delhi in 1966, the rules relating to salaries etc. continued to apply
till the formulation of Delhi High Court Officers and Servants (Salary, Allowances, Leave and Pensions) Rules, 1970,
with effect from 1 April,1970.  The employees who originally came on deputation from the Punjab High Court were
given the choice of option for the Central scales of pay and being absorbed or reverting to the Punjab High Court within
a period of six months.
6.1       The Department of Justice informed the Committee that the equation of the employees of the Delhi High Court
vis-à-vis the employees of the Delhi Administration was disturbed on account of challenges to the basis of working out
the parities.  In 1980, the Private Secretaries to Judges filed a Writ Petition in the Delhi High Court (C.W.P. No.329/80-
P.N.  Chopra  Vs. Union of India) praying for a revision of pay scales from Rs.550-900/- to Rs.775-1200/- on the
grounds that the Private Secretary  to the Chief Secretary of Delhi Administration was getting the scale of Rs.775-1200/-
and as a Judge of the Delhi High Court was getting pay higher than the Chief Secretary, his PS should also get a salary
atleast equal to the Private Secretary  of the Chief Secretary, Delhi Administration.  This argument rested basically on
the principle of equal pay for equal work and by comparing the responsibility of Private Secretary  to Judge   with the
responsibilities of PS to Chief Secretary, Delhi Administration.  This Writ  was allowed by the Delhi High Court. 
Special Leave Petition against this judgement filed by the Government in the Supreme Court was dismissed .
 
6.2       The Department of Justice also informed the Committee that, thereafter, the Readers of Delhi High Court filed a
Writ stating that they should be given the same scales as Private Secretary to Judges on the basis of  ‘equal status posts’ 
and this Writ was also allowed by the Delhi High Court.  Later on, the Superintendents, Court Masters and Interpreters
in the Delhi High Court also got higher pay than their counterparts in the Delhi Administration as their Writ Petitions
were allowed by the Delhi High Court on the ground of ‘equal status post’.  The scale of  pay of  Private Secretary  to
Judges was further revised to Rs.3000-4500/- to bring them on par with Private Secretary  to Secretary to the
Government of India. 
6.3       The Committee was further informed that another form of challenge to the basis of equation arose in 1994 when

Dissenting Note
 
            At a time when every Central Government employee has been given an abnormal raise in his or her emoluments,
the reduction of pay in certain cases is unfair and uncalled for.  In fact, it is punitive.

63rd Report
file:///D/Committee%20on%20Home%20Affairs/63rdreport.htm[30-06-2016 17:34:36]
            An autonomous institution may have a different Pay structure base on nature of duties and responsibilities of its
employees.  It is for this reason perhaps that the pay structure of the employees of Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha is
different from that of Government employees.
            The object of the Bill is meant to remove anomalies.  But no such anomalies exist.  Court judgements did the
job.                         
            There is no departure from the common rule between the scales of two classes of persons if they are not similar
as to their status, nature of work, duties, etc.  Employees of the Government and Courts have no similarity. 
Designations are also not similar.  Therefore, any equation based on mere designations is not fair.
            No evaluation of functions or work of Court employees against government employees has been undertaken.  In
Federation of All India Customs and Central Excise Stenographers (Recognised) and Other vs. Union of India and
Others vs. Union of India and Others (W.P.[Civil] No. 5019/82), a Bench of the Supreme Court questioning the value
judgement without rational basis, held as under :
“Equal pay for equal work is a concomitant of Article 14, but equal pay for unequal work will be a negation of
that right.  Equal pay must depend upon the nature of the work done; it cannot be judged by the mere volume of
work; there may be qualitative difference as regards reliability and responsibility.  Functions may be the same
but the responsibilities make a difference.  The same amount of physical work may entail different quality of
work, some more sensitive, some requiring more tact, some less  -  it varies from nature and culture of
employment.
                                    xx                                 xx                                
There is an element of value judgement by those who are charged with the administration in fixing the scales of
pay and other conditions of service.  Differentiation in implementing the award or the recommendations of Pay
Commission without rational basis may amount to discrimination.  But so long as such value judgement is made
bonafide, reasonably on an intelligible criterion which has a rational nexus with the object of differentiation such
differentiation will not amount to discrimination.”
            The Bill strikes at the very root of the powers enjoyed by the High Courts and Supreme Court under Arts. 226
and 32 of the Constitution.
            Instead of removing anomalies, the bill creates them.  It disturbs the functional hierarchy of the Registry as laid
down in the Delhi High Court (Appointment and Conditions of Service) Rules, 1972.  It makes promotional posts lower
and feeder posts higher.  In an effort to
replace salaries, allowances, leave and pension Rules, the bill, as a matter of fact, touches upon conditions of service, in
respect of which the Chief Justice has already made Rules.
            The protection of pays, as envisaged in the Bill, is fallacious.  Its every application will reduce them in real terms
of allowances, such as DA, HRA etc. which are not payable on Personal Pay.  Similarly, for pension purposes, Personal
Pay would not count as pay, resulting in huge reduction in pay/pension.
            The Vth Pay Commission, an expert body appointed by the Government, went into the conditions of service of
Court employees and came to the conclusion that Court employees deserved higher scales of pay but observed that to
avoid wrong parallels being drawn, nomenclatures of various posts required to be changed/redefined in tune with the
nature of work, etc.  Since this has been done, the very basis of this Bill is knocked out.
            It is submitted that under Article 229(2) of the Constitution, the legislature of the State has power to make law in
respect of the conditions of service of the officers and servants of the High Court and under the proviso to Art. 229(2),
the Chief Justice has power to make rules relating to salaries, allowances, leave or pension in respect of the officers and
servants of the High Court but these would require the approval of the Governor.  Thus, Art. 229(2) is in two parts, first
providing for power of the legislature, besides the Chief Justice, to make laws in respect of conditions of service and
second, providing for framing of rules regarding salary etc. by the Chief Justice with the approval of the Governor.  It
would be seen that the legislature could only legislate in respect of conditions of service and not in respect of salary,
allowances, leave and pension of the High Court employees.
            Thus, the present bill which is in respect of salary, allowances, leave and pension should not be enacted because

of the Supreme Court’s judgement on M. Gurumoorthy vs. Accountant General and Ors (AIR 1971 Vol. 2 SCC 137). 
“Clause (1) read with clause (2) of Article 229 conferred exclusive power not only in the matter of appointments, but
also with regard to prescribing the conditions of service of officers and servants of a High Court by Rules on the Chief
Justice of the Court.  This is subject to any legislation by the state legislature but only in respect of conditions of
service.  In the matter of appointment even the Legislature cannot abridge or modify the powers conferred on the Chief
Justice under clause (1).  The approval of the Governor, as noticed in the matter of rules, is confined only to such rules
as relate to salaries, allowances, leave or pension.”
            The Standing Committee mentions about reference to the Chief Justice.  This is the right course.  I suggest that
we should approach him for advice before sending the bill to Parliament

done in the last several years, the Chief Justices of Supreme Court and Delhi High Court had been framing rules
regarding service conditions, pay scales, etc. and getting approval from the President. The Chairman told that
Committee would have to take up clause-by-clause consideration of the Bills and would have to make recommendations

STATEMENT OF OBJECTS AND REASONS
 
 
      Serious  anomalies have crept  into the pay-scales of the employees of the High Court  of Delhi on account of
various judgments and orders passed by the Court in Writ Petitions filed by its employees.  These distortions  and
anomalies in the pay-structure  of the staff of Delhi High  Court are likely to cause serious agitation amongst similarly
placed  Central Government  and the Government of the National Capital Territory  of Delhi.
 
      2.   This might  ultimately lead to large-scale distortions in the pay-structure of the employees of the Central
Government  and the Government of the National Capital  Territory of Delhi with attendant serious consequential
financial  implications.  The Government  examined the implications of the emerging distortions in the pay-structures of

remedy available to the Government.
 
      3.   The Bill seeks to achieve the above object.

      Sub:           The Salaries, Allowances, Leave and Pensions of the Officers and Servants of the Supreme Court and
Delhi High Court  Bills, 1994 – further  pursuing thereof by the Department-related Parliamentary
Standing Committee on Home Affairs. – proposed amendments thereof.
 
 
      Under the Supreme Court Officers and Servants (Conditions of Service and Conduct) Rules, 1961, the  officers and
servants of the Supreme Court have been  placed at par with  corresponding  employees of the Central  Government  and
their  pay scales  had accordingly  been  fixed  on the standard  Central Government  pattern.  Similarly, in the Delhi
High Court  officers and Servants  (Salaries, Allowances, Leave and Pension) Rules, 1970,  the employees  of the Delhi
High Court were getting the pay scales  at par with the corresponding staff of Delhi  Administration which is following
the Standard Central  Government pattern.  However, the Court’s  employees  got higher scales of pay by taking
matters  to the Courts  which allowed their petitions.  This resulted in serious anomalies in the pay scales of the
employees of the Delhi High Court as well as those of the Supreme Court of India.
 
2.   With a view to rectify the anomalies which had crept into the pay scales of the Courts employees, the aforesaid 
Bills of 1994 were introduced in the Rajya Sabha on 19.8.1994.  It was proposed through the Bills that the Officers and
Servants of the Delhi High Court and the Supreme Court be given pay scales on the analogy of the IVth Pay
Commission’s recommendations and the Schedule to the Bills were accordingly prepared.
 
3.   The recommendations of the Vth Pay Commission have become available and the scales of pay  of the Central
Government  Employees  have already   been revised  accordingly.  The pay scales  indicated in the Schedules to the
1994  Bills will be replaced  by the revised  pay scales approved by the Government in respect of corresponding  Central
Government employees on the basis of the recommendations of the  Fifth Central Pay Commission.  Accordingly, the
Schedules to the 1994  Bills  indicating  the revised pay scales based on the  recommendations of the Vth Pay
Commission are prepared and annexed to this NOTe at Annexe-I.
