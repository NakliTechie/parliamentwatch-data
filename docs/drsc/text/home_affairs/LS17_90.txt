INTRODUCTION
 
I, the Chairman of the Department-related Parliamentary Standing Committee on Home Affairs having been
authorized by the Committee to present the Report on its behalf, do hereby present this Ninetieth Report on the
Representation of the  People (Amendment) Bill, 2001·
.
 
2.         In pursuance of the rules relating to the Department-related Parliamentary Standing Committees, the Chairman,
Rajya Sabha in consultation with the Speaker, Lok Sabha referred¨¨ the Representation of the People  (Amendment) Bill,
2001, as introduced in the Rajya Sabha on 5 December, 2001 and pending therein, to the Committee on 10 December
2001 for examination and report.
 
3.0.      The Committee considered the Bill in 4 sittings held on 11 December 2001,4 February, 17 June and 5 July 2002.
 
3.1       The Committee in its sitting held on 11 December 2001 decided to issue a Press communiqué inviting
suggestions/comments on the Bill from individuals/institutions/organisations interested in the subject matter of the Bill
by 7 February 2002.  The Press communiqué appeared in the leading newspapers on 7 January 2002.  
 
3.2       The Committee heard oral evidence of the Secretary, Legislative Department (Ministry of Law, Justice and
Company Affairs) on the Bill in its sitting held on 4 February 2002.
 
3.3       In its sitting held on 17 June, 2002, the Committee held further deliberations on the Bill.
 
3.4       In its sitting held on 5 July 2002, the Committee considered the draft Report on the Bill and adopted the same.
 
3.5       In the sitting held on 5 July 2002, the Committee also decided that the evidence tendered before the Committee
may be laid on the Table of both the Houses of Parliament.
 
4.0       In the course of its deliberations, the Committee has made use of the background note on the Bill received form
the Legislative Department (Ministry of Law, Justice and Company Affairs), replies of the Legislative Department to the
queries raised by the Members in the sittings of the Committee and comments of  Legislative Department on memoranda
received from individuals/organisations. The list of individuals/organisations and gist of their memoranda together with
comments of Legislative Department thereon is placed at Annexure.
 
5.         For facility of reference and convenience, observations and recommendations of the Committee have been
printed in bold letters in the body of the Report.

R E P O R T
            The Representation of the People (Amendment) Bill, 2001 (See Annexure-I) proposes to do away with the
requirement of residential qualification for contesting election to the Council of States (Rajya Sabha) and to introduce
open ballot system in lieu of secret ballot for the election to the Council of States through amendments to relevant
Sections (3, 59, 94 and 128) of the Representation of the People Act, 1951  (hereinafter referred to as RP Act, 1951).
 
2.0       Section 3 of the RP Act, 1951 provides residential qualification1
  for membership of the Council of States.  As
per the provision of the said section, an elector in any Parliamentary Constituency of a State/Union Territory is qualified
to be chosen as representative of that State/UT in the Council of States.
 
2.1       The residential qualification was prescribed by the RP Act, 1951 to restrict the contest to those  candidates who
are enrolled as the electors of the State/UT to be representatives of that State/UT, as Members of  the Council of States
are considered to be representatives  of States/UTs from where they are  elected,  by the Constitution2
 .
 
2.2       Residential qualification  for the Members of Rajya Sabha is unique in the Indian Parliamentary democracy
because of federal character of that House.  In order to be a Member of Lok Sabha, a candidate must  be an elector  of
any Parliamentary Constituency   (except for the Parliamentary constituencies in the States of Jammu & Kashmir and
Sikkim, where residency qualification is required till now) whereas a Member of the Council of States has to be an 
elector of any Parliamentary Constituency in a State/UT  which he represents. 
 
2.3.0    In the United States of America, the requirement of residential qualification is identical for both the Houses of
US Congress (House of Representatives & Senate) whereas it is peculiar to the Upper House only in India.
 
2.3.1    Section 3 of Article 1 of the Constitution of the United States of America which deals with  the composition of
the Senate inter alia  mentions  that a person has to be an inhabitant of the State from where  he is chosen to the Senate,
the Indian counterpart of Rajya Sabha.   Hence there is no requirement in the US Law for a person to be on electoral roll
of a State of which he claims to be an ordinary resident like RP Act, 1951 in India.  In simple term at a particular time, a
person can be an elector of a State and contest election from another State for Senate in the USA which is not the case  in
India.  In view of the position in US Law, Robert F Kennedy (1965), Danial P. Moynihan (1977) and  Hillary  Clinton
(2001)  moved to New York shortly before running for election to  the Senate to fulfil the technical requirement of
residency in that State.
 
2.4.0    The Statement of Objects and Reasons of the Bill inter alia   spells out  that there are numerous instances, where
the persons who are normally not residing in a particular State have got themselves registered as voters  simply to contest
the  election to the Council  of States.
 
2.4.1    To the query of  the Committee how many Members have been elected from the States to which they do not
normally belong  in the biennial  elections  of the Rajya Sabha held since 1954, the Legislative Department submitted  to
the Committee  a list of 20 Members out of 232 Members elected in six  biennial elections held during the period from
1.1.88 to 28.12.93, as collected from the Election Commission of India.  (See Annexure II) which appears to be
illustrative  not exhaustive   as complete information as sought by the Committee is not available with the Election
Commission of India.
 
2.5.0    From the trend  in biennial elections for the Rajya Sabha, it is observed that Section 3 of the RP Act, 1951 has
not served any practical purpose because  the candidates  in the election to the Council of States are selected on the basis
of their party affiliations  and are elected by the Members of Legislative Assemblies more or less based on the strength
of the respective political parties therein.   If the political party wants a person to be elected from a State, it can get him
registered as a voter in that State,  even though the concerned person may not be normally residing in that State.

2.5.1    The Legislative Department has mentioned that  in the present day,  mobility of persons has increased
considerably.  Further, a person may belong to more than one State professionally and may have his residence or place of
business in more than one State.
 
2.6.0    The issue of residential qualification was discussed in the All Party Meeting held on 29 April 2000 at the behest
of Chief Election Commissioner  (See Annexure-III for extracts from the minutes of the All Party Meeting convened by
the Election  Commission of India on Council of States: Qualification  for Election)  wherein divergent views were
expressed.    The Chief Election Commissioner in the same meeting opined that a precise definition of ‘ordinarily
resident’ was very difficult and wanted the political parties to bring about the best possible solution, in the light of the
experience of the past fifty years.  There have been a lot of litigation on the subject which could not solve the issue.
 
2.6.1    The Secretary, Legislative Department had informed the Committee that the Election Commissioner of India in a
communication to his Department had  supported the idea to dispense with the requirement of residence of a particular
State for being the Member of Council of States, which has been proposed in clause 2 of the Bill.
 
2.7.0    The Committee notes that the residential restriction put by the R P Act, 1951 has not been tested in  the Court.
The Committee felt that the said provision is not in consonance with Article 19 of the Constitution which guarantees
freedom of movement and right to reside and settle, to the individual.
 
3.0       The next part of the Bill (i.e. clauses 3, 4 and 5) is intended to provide for a system of open ballot for election to
the Council of States by amending three Sections (59, 94 and 128) of R.P.Act,1951.
 
3.1       The manner of election to be conducted by the system of secret ballot for the Council of States has not been
mandated by the Constitution as in the case of President3
 and Vice-President4.  The Constituent Assembly discussed 
adult suffrage but did not deliberate  on  secrecy of ballot for the elections to Rajya Sabha and Lok Sabha.  The
Parliament in pursuance of the power conferred5 by the Constitution, enacted RP Act, 1951 for introducing secret
ballot.  However, the Constitution under clause (4) of Article 80 provides for the method of election to the Council of
States i.e. system of proportional representation by means of single transferable vote.
 
3.2.0    The maintenance of secrecy of voting is mandated for the election to the Council of States by various provisions
of the R P Act, 1951, namely, Sections 59, 94 and 128.  Section 59 spell out that votes to be given by ballot and details
of manner of voting have been enumerated in the Conduct of Election Rules, 19616
.  Part VI of said rules deals with
voting at elections by Assembly Members and in Council constituencies.  Thus, suitable amendments would be
necessitated in Part VI of the Conduct of Election Rules, 1961, soon after the enactment of this legislation.
 
3.2.1    As per the existing law, the biennial election as well as by-election to the Council of States has to be done
through secret ballot paper.  Votes can be received by postal ballot7
 but not by proxy8 in addition to voting in person at
the polling place.  Moreover, the law also permits9 recording of votes of illiterate, blind or infirm electors by his
companion.
 
3.2.2    The ballot paper for the election to the Council of States is different from that of the election to the House of
People in view of different election methods.  The ballot paper used at the election to Rajya Sabha contains serial
number of the candidate, name of the candidate and blank space opposite to the name of each candidate for making the
preferences.  The elector has as many preferences as the number of candidates in the election fray.  The elector has to
cast his/her preferences in the international form of Indian Numerals or in Roman form or in the form used in any Indian
language in ballot paper to make it valued, as preferences are not allowed to be indicated in words10
.  Further it is
compulsory for an elector to give his first preference vote but there is no compulsion to give his subsequent preference
votes,  without the first preference votes the ballot paper would be treated as invalid.
 
3.3.0    Maintenance of secrecy of vote lies on the part of both the elector as well as officials on election duty.  The law
prescribes certain procedures for both the elector as well as the officials on duty for handling ballot papers in order to

maintain secrecy of vote.
 
3.3.1    The ballot paper contains a counterfoil having serial number of ballot paper.  The ballot paper can be given by
the polling officer only after receiving signature/thumb impression of the elector on the counterfoil of the ballot paper.
Recording of serial number of the ballot paper on the electoral roll of the elector i.e. the list of Members of Legislative
Assembly,  is not allowed by any official; nor any person at the polling centre can note down the serial number of ballot
paper issued to a particular voter.  The serial numbers of the ballot papers issued are also concealed in the manner laid
down by the Election Commission so as to prevent the identification of the elector by using it at the time of counting.
 
3.3.2    The law permits maintenance of secrecy within the polling station by laying down certain voting procedure
which has to be followed by the elector at the time of casting vote.  The officials on duty ensure the secrecy of voting by
the elector.  If the procedure is violated by the electors the polling officer is empowered to take back the ballot paper
from the elector and record on its back the words “Cancelled: voting procedure violated” and put his signature, which are
treated as invalid ballot paper.
 
3.3.3    The official on duty at a polling station/counting of vote,  is liable to penal action11
 for failing to maintain
secrecy of votes whereas the elector is not punishable for failing to maintain the secrecy of vote as the secrecy of vote is
a prerogative for him unlike the official on duty for whom it is a duty.
 
3.4.0    The secrecy of voting is mandated by the Parliament to ensure free and fair elections to the Council of States. 
The Legislative Department maintains that over the years the secrecy of voting has failed to ensure fair election to the
Council of States as money power is used to propel cross voting/political horse trading amongst Members of Legislative
Assemblies as a result of which the purity of election is in question.
 
3.4.1    The Legislative Department in their written submission has submitted that the Tenth Schedule to the Constitution
is not applicable to the election to the Council of States; thus party whip is not issued.  The elector who resort to cross
voting under the garb of conscience voting, flouts party-discipline in the name of secrecy of voting.  This has weakened
party discipline over its errant legislator.
 
3.4.2    The Department has contended that the system of open ballot will, therefore,  ensure purity of election as political
parties would be in position to take appropriate disciplinary action against the legislator who does cross voting under the
influence of money.
 
3.5       The Ethics Committee of Rajya Sabha having noted the emerging trend of cross voting in the election for Rajya
Sabha recommended (First Report, 1998) to the Government to examine the introduction of system of open ballot in lieu
of secret ballot in the election for Rajya Sabha.
 
3.6       The Legislative Department has stated that while preferring a system i.e. secret vs. open in Rajya Sabha election,
the basic purpose is to ensure purity of elections.  This aspect has been clearly recognized by the Supreme Court of India
in Raghbir Singh Gill Vs. Gurcharan Singh Tohra (AIR 1980 SC 1362 pp 1369-70).  The Supreme Court in the same
judgement has observed as under:-
 
“…If the very secrecy of ballot instead of ensuring free and fair elections strikes at the root of the principle
of free and fair elections, this basic postulate of democracy would be utilized for undoing free and fair
elections which provide life blood to parliamentary democracy.  If secrecy of ballot instead of ensuring free
and fair elections is used, as is done in this case, to defeat the very public purpose for which it is enacted, to
suppress a wrong coming to light and to protect a fraud on the election process or even to defend a crime,
viz. forgery of ballot papers..”
 
3.7       The opinion of Attorney General has been sought on the constitutionality of aforesaid proposal to introduce open
ballot system in the election to Council of States, who has in turn opined that the scheme is constitutionally permissible
as the system of voting by secret ballot is neither an indispensable concomitant nor a sine qua non of free and fair
elections.

3.8       To a question of the Committee, whether the provision of open ballot would be extended to election to the State
Legislative Councils, the Legislative Department has submitted that it is not feasible to extend the same to State
Legislative Council as certain seats are required to be filled up by graduates, teachers, etc. who are not  necessarily
governed by party discipline.
 
4.0       Taking note of the proposals for dispensing with the residential  qualification for the membership of Rajya Sabha
and introduction of system of open ballot, the Committee held detailed deliberations on these two issues which have
been in operation for the past half century.
 
4.1.0    There is however lack of consensus in the Committee on those issues. 
 
4.1.1    A section of Members, who agree with the amendment to Section 3 of R P. Act, 1951 for dispensing with
residential requirement, expressed a view that the said qualification acts as hindrance in contesting election to Rajya
Sabha as a person may belong to more than one State occupationally.   It led to submission of false affidavits at the time
of filing nomination by those candidates who normally do not reside in  that State.   It was suggested that the Member of
Parliament should not enter Rajya Sabha with false conscience.    It was felt that the constitutionally guaranteed right to
move and reside freely have been somehow infringed by Section 3 of RP Act, 1951; thus is not in consonance with the
spirit of the Constitution. Even though a person does not normally reside in the State from where he is elected, but he is
considered to be representative of that State by virtue of  he being  elected by elected representatives of that State. It
enables the party in power at the Centre   to induct into the Council of Ministers, a person of a particular State, in a
situation in which that party has no M.P. in Parliament from that State, by getting him elected to  Rajya Sabha from any
other State.  It is therefore suggested that there is no requirement of residence qualification as it is not the case with the
election to the House of the People.
 
4.1.2    The other section of Members who oppose the amendment held the following views for continuing the residency
qualification for the election to the Council of States: -
 
The amendment is unnecessary because it would affect the federal character of Rajya Sabha.  The nomenclature
is the reflection of federal character of that House and the candidate getting elected by the Members of Legislative
Assemblies ipso facto  does not entitle him to be the actual representative of that State.   The amendment is against the
spirit of the right of sons of soil and affects the representative character of Rajya Sabha.  It was also suggested that since
around ten percent of Members elected  during the period from 1.1.1988 to 28.12.1993  were from other States,  there is
no need for amending  Section 3 of RP Act, 1951.  One of those Members referred to the emotional detachment   of
Member , who normally does not reside in that State, in spending from the MPLADS Fund for the State he  represent in
the Rajya Sabha
 
4.2.0 With regard to the open ballot system a section of members supporting the amendment felt that this will help in
lessening horse trading and rampant corruption in Rajya Sabha elections.  Some Members felt that a strict Code of
Conduct was required to check corruption.  Some other Members felt that for electors like MLAs there was no rationale
for secrecy, which was basically meant for ordinary voter in the polling booth for the general elections. The important
thing is ensuring purity of election.
 
4.2.1    The section of Members opposing the open ballot system gave varied reasons opposing the amendment.   They
argued that it would not help in bringing down corruption.  Some members felt that the discipline within the party will
help in curbing horse-trading while other members emphasized upon the need for bringing about conscientious change to
eradicate corruption.   Another view was expressed that even if, money power was curbed to entice MLAs, by
introducing the open ballot system, there was a need to put a check on the political parties from receiving funds in
consideration for getting a Member elected to Rajya Sabha. A view was expressed that since secret voting is applicable
in Presidential & Vice-Presidential elections under Articles 55 and 66 of the Constitution, respectively, it should be
continued in the Rajya Sabha elections too.
 
4.3.0    Notwithstanding the divergent views on the provisions of the Bill, the Members were unanimous on the issue of
curbing money power in the Rajya Sabha elections at the earliest.

4.3.1    In view of the divergent perceptions in the Committee on the subject matter of the Bill, it is of the considered
view that the Government should explore the possibility of evolving a consensus on the issues before piloting the Bill in
Parliament.

it.  He apprised Members of the main aims of the Bill which were to do away with the requirement of residence of a
particular State or Union Territory for contesting elections to the Council of States from that State or Union Territory
and also to introduce open-ballot system for elections to the Council of States.  Accordingly, the Bill proposed suitable
amendments in certain sections of the Representation of People Act, 1951relating to qualifications for membership of the
Council of States, the manner of voting of elections, secrecy of voting and maintenance of secrecy of voting by officers
and clerks or other persons performing the elections duty.
 
2.1       The Chairman further stated that the issue relating to deletion of residential qualification was discussed in a

3.0       The Secretary(Legislative) expressed his gratitude to the Committee for being invited to make a presentation on
the Bill.  He said that the Bill was very short, having only five clauses and the first one being of a routine nature.
 
3.1       He apprised the Committee that clause 2 of the Bill sought to dispense with the requirement of being an elector in
a parliamentary constituency of a particular State or a UT from which a person wants to contest the elections to the
Council of States.  He further said that this provision had not been found to be of much practical value, keeping in view
the fact that many of the leaders of various political parties had been contesting elections to the Council of States from
States to which they did not belong.  The Election Commission of India had also supported dispensing with that
provision in Section 3.
 
3.2       The Secretary further said that the next limb of the Bill, which had three clauses, viz. clauses 3,4 and 5 was
intended to provide for a system of open ballot for elections to the Council of States.
 
3.3       The Secretary said that in prefering a system i.e. secret vs. open ballot in Rajya Sabha election, the basic purpose
to be considered was the purity of elections.  According to him, this aspect had been clearly recognised by the Supreme
Court in S. Raghbir Gill vs. S. Gurcharan Singh Tohra, AIR 1980 SC 1362 at 1369-70 wherein it was ruled that the
principle of secrecy of ballot would have to yield to the larger principle of free and fair elections.
 
3.4       Tracing the background the Secretary said that the Ethics Committee constituted by the Chairman of the Rajya
Sabha in  March 1997, under the Chairmanship of  Shri  S.B. Chavan, examined representatives of various political
parties and presented  its First Report  to Rajya Sabha on 8.12.1998.  It recommended that the question of holding
elections to Rajya Sabha by open ballot be examined.  It was in deference to the wishes of the said Committee and after
examining the matter from legal and constitutional angles that the Government had come forward with the proposal to
introduce the system of open ballot for Rajya Sabha elections.

STATEMENT OF OBJECTS AND REASONS
 
Section 3 of the Representation of the People Act, 1951 prescribes residential qualification for contesting
elections to the Council of States.  There have been numerous instances where the persons who are normally not residing
in a particular State have got themselves registered as voters in that State,  simply to contest an election to the Council of
States.  The Chief Election Commissioner, while discussing this aspect in the all party meeting held on 29
th April, 2001
was of the view that a precise definition of ‘ordinarily resident’ was very difficult and emphasized that it was for the
political parties, acting through Parliament, to carry out what is their judgment, might be the best possible solution, in the
light of the experience of the past fifty years.
 
2. The Ethics Committee of Parliament in paragraph 19 of its first report presented to Parliament on 8
th
December, 1998 recommended that the issue relating to open-ballot system for elections to the Rajya Sabha be
examined.   The issue has again given rise to concerns in the wake of allegations of money power made in the media in
respect to biennial elections to the Council of States held in March-April, 2000.
 
3. In the light of the above, the aforesaid issues were examined in depth by the Government and it has been
decided to do away with the requirement of residence of a particular State or Union territory for contesting election to
the Council of States from that State or Union territory and also to introduce open-ballot system for elections to the
Council of States.  Accordingly, suitable amendments are proposed to be made in certain sections of the Representation
of the People Act, 1951 relating to qualification for the requirement of residence of a particular State or Union territory
for contesting election to the Council of States from that State or Union territory and also to introduce open-ballot
system for elections to the Council of States.  Accordingly, suitable amendments are proposed to be made   in certain
sections of the Representation of the People Act, 1951 relating to qualification for membership of the Council of States,
the manner of voting of elections, secrecy of voting and maintenance of secrecy of voting by officers, clerks, agents or
other persons performing the election duty.
