INTRODUCTORY 
 Environment, over the years, has emerged as a key component a nd all pervading theme 
on the broader contours of economic growth w ithin sustainable development framework.  
Environmental concerns in the process of developmental philosophy have, however, 
sharpened further in the recent years with  the consequences of the environmental 
degradation manifesting in numerous ways su ch as global warming, scanty and scattered 
rainfall, changing weather pattern, depleting glaciers, receding water flow in major rivers, 
poor and pathetic state of e nvironmental resources – land, ai r & water and their adverse 
impact on human health, etc. also known as climate cha nge on the one hand and with 
blatant, brazen and reckless exploitation of natural reso urces resulting in massive 
deforestation and degeneration of green covers on the other.  As recently as on 18th April, 
2013, Supreme Court, in its landmark judgment,  decreed that environment & ecology are 
national assets, they are subject to interg enerational equity a nd the principle of 
sustainable development has been  rendered as part of Articl e 21 of the Constitution of 
India.  Yet, the national commitment to a clean and healthy environment as enshrined in 
Articles 48(a) and 51(a)(g) of the Indian Constitution and the aspiration that human 
beings should be able to enj oy a decent quality of  life remains a far cry.  The reasons are 
not far too difficult to find out.  The fact is that the reas ons are both obvious and opaque.  
An evolving and expanding society, such as  ours, in desperate pursuit of economic 
prosperity for poverty alleviation, livelihood secu rity and restoration of  greater justice to 
those deprived is bound to c onfront environmental challe nges.  Secondly, exponential 
population growth, inappropriate  technology, consumption choi ces, paradigm shift in 
amicable and harmonious relationship of co-existence between people and ecosystem, 
proliferation of polluting indus tries, unplanned urbanisation, etc. have prominently and 

routinely been attributed to be the chie f drivers of environmental degradation and 
ecological imbalances.  But there are certain ot her latent yet potent contributory factors 
which need attention and focus too.  These ar e institutional failures resulting in lack of 
enforcement of rights of access to and use of  environmental resources, failed and flawed 
regulatory regime, governance constrains in environmental management, policy lacunae, 
disincentivising  environmen tal conservation, to mention a few.  The widely 
acknowledged fact that not much could be done to manage the former category of 
prominent factors catapults us to concentrate more on the latter ones as to how best we 
can overcome them.  Sooner or later, this ha s to be done in recogni tion of the axiomatic 
fact that environmental resources play pivot al role not only in pr oviding livelihoods but 
also in securing life supportin g ecological services to all forms of life – human, animal 
and plant.  But, the sooner done is better because environmental damages beyond a 
certain degree, become irreparable and irreversible.   
2. It, therefore, becomes imperative for al l of us to gear and galvanize all the 
resources at our disposal to minimize if not  altogether eliminate the possibilities of 
collateral damage done to the environment, ecology, forests and wildlife. 
3.  Environment is universal, ubiquitous and all pervasiv e comprising of entities – 
natural or manmade, external to oneself and their interrelationships, which provide value 
now or perhaps in the future to mankind.  Mana ging such a vast and wi der rather infinite 
spectrum of challenges, is undoubtedly a daun ting task entrusted to  the Ministry of 
Environment & Forests as a nodal agency in the administrative setup of the Govt. of 
India.  The ministry, since its inception on 4
th January, 1985 has been, through thick and 
thins of overriding and pres sing priorities, sometimes c onflicting and contradictory 
engaged in trying to strike a fine bala nce by planning, promoting, coordinating and 
overseeing environmental, ecological forestry and wildlife programmes and policies.  The 
main activities of the Ministry involve prot ection of the environment; conservation and 
survey of existing forests, wildlife, aqua tic bodies, water resources and eco-systems 
including biodiversity and ot her resources particularly in eco-sensitive zones for 
identification of new species of flora and fauna; afforestation and regeneration of 
degraded areas; prevention and control of pollution (air, water, noise, industrial); 
management of hazardous substances; mitigation and adaptation to climate change 
through adoption of cleaner technologies; pr omotion of sustainable development and 
better environmental governance; ensuring welfare of animals; capacity building; training 
and research in classical and molecular taxonomy; etc.  Apart from these national 
activities, the Ministry is also engaged in promoting in ternational cooperation through 
regional, bilateral and multilateral initiatives in the arena of environment and ecology.  
Issues relating to regional bodies like Economic and Social  Council for Asia & Pacific 
(ESCAP) and South Asian Association for Regional Cooperation (SAARC) and 
multilateral bodies such as the Commission on Sustainable Development (CSD); Global 
Environment Facility (GEF) have been entruste d to the Ministry.  Besides, the Ministry 
also serves as the nodal agency for the United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP), 
South Asia Cooperation Environment Programme  (SACEP) and Intern ational Centre for 
Integrated Mountain Development (ICIMD) , as well as, for follow up on the United 
Nations Conference on Environment and Development (UNCED).

7. Monitorable Targets of XI Five Year Plan 
7.1  As per the Mid-term Appraisal Do cument of the Planning Commission, the 
following 4 monitorable socioeconomic target s in the Environment and Forests Sector 
had been emphasised in the XI Five Year Plan period: 
 To increase forest and tree cover by 5 percentage points; 
 To attain WHO standards of air quality in all major cities by 2011-12; 
 To treat all urban waste water by 2011-12 in order to clean river waters; and 
 To increase energy efficiency by 20 per cent by 2016-17. 
 7.2  When the Committee enquired from the Ministry about the achievements in 
respect of the above targets,  the Ministry did not come out with specific and pointed 
reply.  Going through the replies, it is very  difficult to make out precisely the physical 
achievements.  In reply to the targets of in creasing forest and tree cover by 5 percentage 
points, the Ministry replied that in order to achieve the target of 5 per cent, an additional 
16.5 million hectares forest and tree cover ha d to be obtained by 2012.  But the tree 
planting during the Plan period was around 1.52 million hectare per year which if 
multiplied by 5, gives a figure of 7.6 million hectares during the Plan period, thus 
showing an overall shortage of plantation in  8.9 million hectares.  The Ministry further 
stated that given the constraints of land a nd financial resources, th e achievement of the 
target was ambitious.  With regard to the target of treating all urban waste water by 2011-
12 in order to clean river waters, the Ministry  stated that the primary responsibility of 
treating sewage was with the Municipalities.  The Ministry further added that maintaining 
cleanliness of rivers required minimum river water and it was the Ministry of Water 
Resources, which was the nodal Ministry to ensu re adequate water flow in rivers.  The 
Ministry thus had limited role to play in achie vement of this target.  On the target of 
attaining World Health Organisation standards of Air Quality in all major cities by 2011-
12, the Ministry had a very peculiar reply to offer.  The Ministry felt that the notified 
National Ambient Air Quality Standards in  India, based mostly on United States 
Environmental Protection Agency health Criteria (USEPA) documents and not WHO 
Guidelines would provide a more realistic and appropriate goal fo r achieving better air 
quality in India. Responding to  the monitorable target of en hancing energy efficiency by 
20 per cent points by 2016-17, the Mi nistry tossed the ball in th e court of the Ministry of 
Power stating that action on enhancement of energy efficiency and lowering energy 
intensity was the responsibility  of Ministry of Power.  The Committee feels that if the 
Ministry has to handle the socioeconomic mo nitorable targets given to it in such a 
casual and cavalier manner, the very purpo se of devising and deciding a target 
becomes meaningless.  The Ministry should  have, while or before fixing the target, 
thought as to whether the targets pertained to it or not.  But once the targets were 
set, the Ministry must have  made sincere and serious effo rts to meet them in close 
coordination and cooperation with the ot her concerned Ministries.  The Committee 
takes a very serious view on the way and the manner in which the targets given to 
the Ministry had been handled.  The Commi ttee feels that if the Ministry was not 
serious in trying to seriously pursue the ta rgets set, they must not have accepted 
these monitorable targets at all.  The Committee hopes that monitorable 
socioeconomic targets set for the XII Five Year Plan would not meet the same fate 
as the targets of the XI Five Year Pl an and therefore, recommends, that the 
Ministry should seriously and vigorously pursue the targets and goals set for the XII 
Five Year Plan so as to ensure that concre te achievements in re spect of each target 
are made.   

 
8.  Budgetary allocation of the Ministry of Environment & Forests in the XI and 
XII Five Year Plan 
8.1 The Ministry was provided an outlay of Rs. 10,000.00 Crores in the XI Five Year 
Plan (2007-12), out of which Environmen t & Ecology accounted for Rs. 3786.01 Crores 
and Forestry & Wildlife Rs. 6213.99 Crores.  Sector-wise distri bution was Rs. 1246.01 
Crores for Environment; Rs. 2540.00 Crores fo r National River Conservation Directorate 
(NRCD); Rs. 2943.99 Crores for Forests & Wildlife; Rs. 3150.00 Crores for National 
Afforestation & Eco-Development Board (NAEB) and Rs. 120.00 Crores for Animal 
Welfare.  However, by the end of the Plan period, the actual outlay, based on year-wise 
allocation remained at Rs. 9231.00 Crores with  sectoral share of Rs. 1913.34 Crores; Rs. 
2760.75 Crores; Rs. 2611.44 Crores; Rs. 1826.47 Crores and Rs. 119.00 Crores for 
Environment; NRCD; Forests & Wildlife; NA EB & Animal Welfare respectively.  
Environment & Ecology thus finally account ed for Rs. 4674.09 Crores and Forestry & 
Wildlife Rs. 4556.91 Crores.  While Environment & Ecology got Rs. 888.08 Crores 
more, Forestry & Wildlife got Rs. 1657.08 Cror es less than the init ial earmarking of 
Budgetary allocation.  Ther e was, however, a reduction of Rs. 769.54 Crores in the 
original outlay for the XI Five Year Plan which got restricted to Rs. 9231.00 Crores as 
against Rs. 10000.00 Crores.  On a comparative analysis of the original/ initial vis-à-
vis actual Sector-wise outlay, it is found that while National Afforestation and Eco-
Development Board which consists of the key programme of National Afforestation 
was the worst sufferer, Forests & Wildlife was the second worst sufferer in terms of 
reduction in allocation.  As against an initial amount of Rs. 3150.00 Crores and Rs. 
2943.99 Crores, earmarked under these Sectors, the final outlay came down to Rs. 
1826.47 Crores and Rs. 2943.99 Crores, showing a reduction of Rs. 1323.53 and Rs. 
332.55 Crores respectively.   
8.2  The Ministry was able to utili ze the amount of Rs. 8470.46 Crores, Rs. 3899.67 
Crores under Environment & Ecology and Rs. 4570.79 Crores under Forestry & Wildlife, 
thus, leaving an amount of Rs. 1529.54 Crores unutilized in comparison to the original 
allocation of Rs. 10000.00 Crores and Rs. 769.54 Crores in comparison to the final 
allocated outlay of Rs. 9231.00 Cores.  The Mini stry has informed that the utilisation 
ratio for the first 4 years of the XI Plan remained in the range of around 99 per cent but in 
the last year of the Plan pe riod, i.e., the year 2011-12, the utilisation ratio came down to

13.   Monetary Requirements of the Ministry 
 
13.1  The Secretary, Ministry of Environmen t & Forests while making his presentation 
before the Committee gave an overview of the fund requirements in respect of major 
schemes falling into three broad areas viz. Na tional River Conservation; Forestry and the 
Wildlife Management.  Starting with National River Conservation, it was stated that the 
approach of the Ganga and the Yamuna Ac tion Plan had been to treat waste water 
generated from Class-I and Class- II cities so that critically  polluted stretches falling the 
down streams of major cities on the river bank could be take n care of.  An amount of the 
order of Rs. 9000.00 Crores had been spent from the year 1985 onwards for creating 
treatment capacity of around 5000 Million Litre Per Day (MLD).  There was still a gap of 
around 26000 MLD between the sewage generated and the capacity of treatment created.  
In order to bridge the gap of treatment capacity of the order of 26000 MLD around 5 
times of what had been create d thus far at a cost of ar ound Rs. 9000 crores, the Ministry 
would be requiring money to the tune of approximately Rs. 50,000.00 Crores.  In the area 
of forestry, today we have tree and forest coverage of around 24 per cent, i.e., 78 million 
hectare of the geographical area of the country as against the target of 33 per cent.  To 
achieve the target of 33 per cent coverage  we needed to reach more than 100 million 
hectares from the present 78 million hectare.  Under Green India Mission, the objective is 
to increase forest/ tree cover of 5 million hectares of forest/ non-forest lands and improve 
the quality of forest cover on another 5 million hectares of forest land and the total cost of 
the Mission was Rs. 46,000.00 Crores spread over  ten years coinciding with the XII and 
XIII Plan periods.  Still the gap of 22 million hectares would be partly met and the rest 
almost 12 million hectares would remain to be covered at an estimated cost of around the 
same amount as earmarked for Green India Mission.  Similarly, for Wildlife 
Management/ Tiger Conservation, the appr oved outlay is somewhere around Rs. 300.00 
Crores while the magnitude of funds require d was much more.  There were 660 National 
Parks and sanctuaries, some of them exte nding to about 500 Sq. Kms. in size but the 
average allocation for them comes to about Rs. 0.5 Cror e per National Park/ Sanctuaries 
which is why the ever increasing man – animal  conflict had been on the rise.  For Tiger 
Conservation, there was a programme to relocate 40,000 families from core Tiger 
habitats under which a funding support of about 10 lakhs per family was to be provided 
for their rehabilitation.  The huge fund require d for the purpose can be easily imagined.  
Thus in broad terms, whether it be for ri ver cleaning, to achieve the desired tree and 
forestry coverage to 33 per cent of the to tal geographical area of the country or be it 
wildlife management, the fund requirement wa s pretty huge and the budgetary allocation 

made on an annual basis across these various schemes was pretty small in comparison to 
requirement. 
13.2  While the Committee takes note of the huge  gap between availability of fund vis-
à-vis its requirement, it feels that the entire required funding cannot be done by the Govt. 
of India, owing to budgetary constraints, although, it is of the c onsidered view that 
volume of funding from the Govt. of India must be reasonably raised.  In such a situation, 
the Committee feels that the Ministry should  explore the possibili ties of generating 
resources from sources other th an the Govt. of India.  These sources could be managing 
loans from the Asian Development Bank (ADB),  the World Bank or the Japanese aid.  
Yet another source could be strict enforcement of Polluters Pay Principle, so as to raise 
additional resources; making adequate provisio n for civil administrative adjudication to 
fast track levy of penalty, etc. by making enviro nment laws more stringent and effective.  
Integrating environmental elements/ factors into cost benefit analysis while making 
public, as well as, private investment decisions  for more prudent allocation of resources.  
The Ministry may also think in terms of pe rsuading Ministry of Finance to levy some 
green tax/ cess, on the lines of  Education Cess / service tax, et c.  The Ministry may also 
synergize its activities with projects/ programmes of those sectors of the economy which 
contribute to the volume of pollution the most , i.e., the Transport Se ctor, Energy Sector, 
Oil Sector, Mining Sector, etc. and require diversion of more forest land for non-forestry 
purposes.  One such effort has been made  in Visakhapatnam, where the local public 
sector Units have voluntarily undertaken the task of planting 40 lakh saplings in a span of 
five years and the progress of this experiment has been quite  satisfying.  Such initiative 
can very well be replicated in other such cities/ towns which are bearing the brunt of 
pollution because of Public Sect or/ Private Sector units loca ted there.  Over and above, 
the compensatory afforestation fund havi ng huge amount of money, i.e., Rs. 1718.20 
Crores (Approx.) as pr incipal amount as on 31
st March, 2012 and Rs. 384.59 Crores in 
the form of interest accrue d on the principal am ount put into the fund could also be 
utilised for the purpose of afforestation and regeneration of degraded forestland.  
Expenditure being incurred towards Corporate Social Responsibility by the Public Sector 
Undertaking, Banks, etc. may also be ch annelised towards forestry activities by 
persuading them to earmark certain amount of funds available for Corporate Social 
Responsibility (CSR) for this purpose.

18.4  The moot point is that when the reasons for continuous and consistent increase in 
pollution are well known why is it that we have turned into helpless spectators?  The real 
problem perhaps lies in lack of firm c onviction and resolve to  address them.  The 
Ministry itself has admitted that industrial pollution due to its toxi c, non-biodegradable 
nature contributed mainly by tanneries, disti lleries, paper mills and sugar mills are the 
major contributors.  The stretch from Kanna uj to Varanasi has been acknowledged as 
critically polluted.  In the entire Ganga  Basin, approximately 12410 MLD sewage is

In the main stream of Ganga River about 290 0 million liters of sewage is discharged 
everyday from Class-I cities and Class-II towns located along the banks out of which

18.5  When the Committee enquired from the Ministry about the number of leather 
processing units/ tanneries operational in Kanpur  and since when these were operational; 
what was the total volume of effluent gene rated by these units; and how many of them 
had effluent treatment plant facility togeth er with the details of  volume of untreated

Tannery effluent plus 27 MLD Sewage).   After treatment done through Anaerobic 
Biological Treatment Process (UASB Technol ogy), the combined treated waste water 
was discharged into a dedica ted waste water irrigation ch annel running parallel to the 
Ganga River.  The Ministry further stated th at in normal situation effluent of tanneries 
should not join Ganga River, but at times due to bypass from some defaulting units or 
due to over flow from the co llection system, some effluent  joined Ganga River through 
the open drains in the area. 
18.6  An analysis of the above reply of th e Ministry, prima-facie suggests that the 
Ministry has tried to camouflage this serious issue in such a way as not to reveal the 
actual picture of the reality, but despite best of its efforts the Ministry left some loopholes 
in its reply whereby it becomes clear that the effluents arising out of tanneries in Kanpur 
is getting directly or indirectly discharg ed into the river Ganga.  The Ministry has 
admitted that about 13 MLD effluent is collected  from the tanneries clusters and sent to 
36 MLD capacity treatment pl ant with 9 MLD tannery effl uent plus 27 MLD sewage.  
Obviously, 4 MLD effluent does not get treate d and find its way into the River Ganga.  
The other loophole is that for whatever reasons, be it bypass from some defaulting units 
or due to over flow from the collection sy stem, some effluents do join River Ganga 
through the open drains in the area.  This rais es the question as to why all this is being 
allowed to continue?  The Committee is constr ained to observe that this may not be the 
real picture and the truth may be somewhat, if not altogether different. 
18.7  The Committee also enquired from the Mi nistry about the natu re and effect of 
effluents/pollutants generated in the tanneries and efforts made by it to stop the units 

from discharging deadly pollu tants into River Ganga.  The Ministry was candid to admit 
that the tannery effluent wa s characteristically known to have a high concentration of 
organic matter mostly in suspended form an d also high concentration of trivalent 
Chromium.  The effluent also contained high con centration of TDS.  On the issue of 
efforts made by the Ministry to  stop these units from discha rging deadly pollutants into 
Ganga, the Ministry stated that CPCB has i ssued directions to UP Pollution Control 
Board to check discharge of polluted effluents into Ganga from tanneries.  Besides CPCB 
had also undertaken an appr oach for pollution surveillance in Ganga basin under which 
inspections of polluting industries in Ganga basin were to be carried out every year.  The 
Committee feels that such a lukewarm nonchalant and pass ive action/ response on an 
issue which has acquired serious  dimensions with grave th reat to the life and the 
livelihood of sizeable number of populace living along the coast of Ganga, which has 
potential to hurt the cultural, traditional, relig ious and spiritual sentiments of crores of 
people and which has attracted people organi se mass movement and protests is not only 
unfortunate but also a cause of serious worry  for all of us because if the Ministry 
continues to go ahead with this approach, the condition of River Ganga will deteriorate 
even for worse.   
18.8  Apart from the River Ganga and Yamuna, the conditions of other major rivers of 
the country are no better.  As per the inform ation furnished by the Ministry, there are 35 
rivers spread over 12 States which has Biological Oxygen Demand (BOD) level above 35 
ml, 15 rivers which has BOD in the range of 20 to 30 mg per liter spread over 7 States 
and so on.  There are around 150 rivers spread across the country which are polluted 
though varying only in quantity.   
18.9  In order to know the exact volume of se wage generated in the country in general

II towns of the country, the treatment cap acity was available only for 1180.48 MLD.  
What is noteworthy in this regard, is that  out of the total treatment capacity of 1180.48

18.10  The Committee feels that there are so me simple and effective solutions which 
could go a long way in tackling the problem of  rampant pollution in the River Ganga, but 
some how or the other, they have not found fa vour with the Ministry.  One such solution 
is to ensure minimum ecological flow in both the Rivers the Ganga and the Yamuna.  
Government had in fact got an assessment of cumulative impact of Hydropower Projects 
done by Alternative Hydro Energy  Centre (AHEC) IIT Roorki  and Wildlife Institute of 
India, Dehradun which inter-alia recommen ded that prescribed environmental flow 
should be released from every project ke eping in view hydrological requirements 
organism.  The Ministry informed the Comm ittee that an Inter-Ministerial Group (IMG) 
headed by Member, Planning Commission was constituted by Minist ry of Environment 
& Forests on 15
th June, 2012 to go into the issues re lated to River Ganga and to suggest 
environmental flow requirement for variou s stretches of Bhagirathi, Alakhnanda and 
other tributaries of the Rive r Ganga.  As per a News re port, the IMG has recommended 
that the plethora of dams built and planned in the Ganga Basin be reengineered to 
maintain 30-50 per cent of water flow in th e lean period of December – March.  The 

Group has also recommended that water flow at  25 per cent between April to October, 
the period when the river has a high water levels be ensured.   
18.11  When the Ministry was asked that one of the key features of the holistic river 
basin approach adopted to ensure effective abatement of pollution and conservation of the 
River Ganga, under the NGRBA was to dete rmine minimum ecological flow and to 
regulate water extraction and whether all the ri parian states had agreed to regulate water 
extraction, the Ministry gave a casual reply by saying that not much work had been done 
on environmental flows in Indian  rivers, particularly, the rive rs in the Ganga basin.  It 
also added that environmental flow assessmen t was both a social and a scientific process 
requiring expert knowledge of various fiel ds including, but not limited to hydrology, 
hydraulics, geomorphology, ecology and biodiver sity, socio-culture livelihood and water 
quality and pollution.   
 
18.12  Yet another solution which was suggested by the Committee in its 224 th Report 
was to treat river conservation as a soci al engineering problem and not entirely 
engineering centric, as the latter alone coul d not be successful.  The Ministry was,

18.13  In view of the above, the Committ ee recommends that the engineering 
centric approach adopted by the Ministry  should be supplemented with social

19.  Public Hearing
 
19.1  While public hearing has been made mandatory in the scheme of things of 
according EC/FC Clearance for some of the proj ects particularly those which affect the 
local inhabitants, the Committee has been give n to understand that public hearing is not 
conducted in an objective and impartial manne r.  Often public hearing becomes a state 
managed and an eye-wash show conducted in  the presence of the project proponents 
where their representatives use it as a plat form to persuade, convince and coax the 
affected public to give their nod for the proj ect to the detriment of  their interest and 

welfare.  The Committee is of the view that such instances give ri se to discontentment 
among the public and sometimes also result in protest putting th e entire project in 
jeopardy.  The Committee, therefore, recommends that Govt. must take necessary 
measures to curb such malpractices in pub lic hearing so that p ublic hearing serves 
the purpose for which these are intended.  The Committee further recommends that 
genuine concerns of the people must fi nd place in the envi ronment management 
plan and the project proponents take necessary preventive and curative measures to 
address the genuine concerns of the public.

22.  All India Coordinated Project on Taxonomic Capacity Building 
(AICOPTAX) 
22.1  Taxonomy occupies a pivotal position in  biology as it deals with scientific 
classification of all living and extinct organism s .   I t  i s  a l s o  a n  i n t e g r a l  p a r t  o f  t h e  
conservation and sustainable utilisation of bi ological diversity.  It  is a well-known fact 
that India has been very rich in flora and fauna.  As such, it becomes imperative that 
India’s large variety of ecologi cal habitats with large variations in species of plants, 
animals and microorganisms are identified, classi fied and nurtured for present as well as 
future generations.  So far about 90,000 an imal species and 47,000 plant species have 
been identified and described.  A large number of animals and plants still remain to be 
explored and identified.  Howe ver, the Ministry of Envir onment & Forests in its Annual 
Report, 2012-13 mentioned that there is overa ll decline in expertise in the taxonomy of 
several groups of living orga nisms.  The existing taxonomy base is also eroding rapidly 
due to non-availability of professionals and lack of trained manpower to take the place of 
the retiring specia lists.  The Ministry has gone to the extent of saying that a wide gap 
exists between the magnitude of taxonomic e xpertise needed and th e available limited 
taxonomic knowledge base.  It has further emphasised that the issue of taxonomic 
impediments needs to be addressed urgently in order to manage the biological resources 
and to meet the challenges of the 21st Century. 
22.2 While, the Committee expresses its deep concern over the grim scenario presented 
by the Ministry, it feels that it is the Ministry itself which is to be blamed for this.  The 
Ministry had, in order to strengthen taxonomic base, set up an All India Coordinated 
Project on Taxonomic Capacity Building (AICOP TAX) in 1999.  30 thematic areas were 
chosen for investigation under AICOPTAX, out of which only 15 thema tic areas have so 
far been implemented.  Out of any outlay of Rs. 13.50 for XI Five Year Plan under 
taxonomic capacity building, Rs. 11.99 Crores could be utilised.  In the year 2012-13, out 
of the BE allocation of Rs. 2.90 Crores, a meager Rs. 0.30 Crores could be utilised by the 
Ministry as on 13 th March, 2013.  It would not be out of place to mention here that the 
expenditure by 31st January, 2013 was only Rs. 0.18 Crores which went up to Rs. 0.30 by 
13th March, 2013.  Perhaps, this  is the reason why this year allocation under this 
Programme has been kept at only Rs. 1.90 Lakhs.   
 

22.3  The Committee feels that a sound taxonomic base is a prerequisite for 
environmental assessment, ecological resear ch, effective conservation management and 
sustainable use for biological resources and despite a coordinated project on taxonomy, 
setup some 12 years back, the Ministry la ments that the existing taxonomic base is 
rapidly declining due to non-availability of pr ofessionals and lack of trained manpower.  
In the name of taxonomic capacity building pr oject, the Ministry is  extending financial 
support to Universities, research Institutes, et c.  It is not able to utilise the budgetary 
allocation fully because the release of grants  is linked to proper utilisation of earlier 
released grants and progress on activities.  The Committee feels that besides 
supporting research projects and organizin g training workshops, the Ministry 
should explore the feasibilities of setting  up a Centre in a reputed University for 
studies in Taxonomy or establish its own Institute for higher education and research 
in Taxonomy in order to produce expert and trained professionals in Taxonomy.   
For attracting bright and promising students in this area, the Ministry should also 
think in terms of awarding scholarshi ps for higher studies and research in 
Taxonomy.  In addition, the Ministry shou ld take on a priority basis all other 
measures necessary to build a pool of professionals in taxonomy for meeting the 
requirements of trained professionals in this field.
