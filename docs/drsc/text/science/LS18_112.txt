R E P O R T
 
The world over environmental degradation has assumed alarming proportions.  Concerns for preservation of
 environment constitute the defining feature of our age.  Our first Prime Minister Pandit Jawaharlal Nehru while
 remaining a moving force behind the establishment of heavy industrial enterprises and hydroelectric projects had also
 articulated the needs for protection of environment.  In a letter to the Chief Ministers on 15 August, 1957 he observed,
 “I should like to refer to something which I think is rather important and which, perhaps, has not received much
 attention.  We want to exploit as well as conserve our natural resources. Sometimes the desire to exploit them outruns
 discretion and we forget the part of conservation.” He further added, “We have many large scale river valley projects
 which are carefully worked out by our engineers.  I wonder however, how much thought is given before the project is
 launched, to having an ecological survey of the area and to find out what the effect would be on the drainage system or
 to the flora and fauna of that area.  It would be desirable to have such an ecological survey of these areas before the
 project is launched and thus to avoid the imbalance of nature.”  Establishment of the Ministry of Environment and
 Forests in 1985 as the nodal Ministry in the Government for the planning, promotion, coordination and overseeing the
 implementation of various schemes relating to environment and forests remained at the heart of our efforts to
 effectively address the challenges of the deteriorating environmental situation.  While the Ministry is entrusted with the
 responsibilities of conservation and survey of flora, fauna, forests and wildlife, its functions and jurisdiction also
 encompass numerous other activities such as promotion of afforestation, regeneration of degraded areas, prevention and
 control of pollution and overall protection of environment.  By assessing the impact of developmental projects on the
 environment, by extending assistance to organizations engaged in implementing environmental and forestry

ENVIRONMENTAL PROTECTION OF TAJ MAHAL
10.       The Committee was informed that in pursuance of directions of the Apex Court , Ministry of Environment &
 Forests has taken up the    work of Environmental Protection of the Taj Mahal. The work will be implemented through
 State Government of Uttar Pradesh and Ministries concerned.  The work also involves making of a green belt around
 the Taj Mahal. It is proposed that from  Tenth Plan onwards, the scheme would be expanded to cover other important
 Heritage sites.
11.       The Committee was apprised that ten projects under the Taj Protection Mission Programme which aims at
 improving electric supply, water supply, solid waste management, roads of Agra city were approved and are at different
 stages of implementation.  To reduce and contain pollution, construction of a by-pass for diverting the vehicular traffic
 is also being implemented.   Besides these ten projects, seven other priority projects as recommended by the Mission
 Management Board constituted under the Programme, are also being considered. These include a Heritage Corridor in
 the Taj Trapezium Zone, Automatic Air Monitoring Station, Hazardous Waste Secured Land-fill site for Agra &
 Mathura, common treatment facility for treatment of biomedical waste at Agra, creating public awareness in the Taj
 Trapezium, etc.  The EFC Memo of these projects has been circulated and will be finalized soon.  The Mission
 Management Board, Central Pollution Control Board and the Ministry are regularly monitoring the progress of
 implementation of the projects formulated under this scheme.  The annual average level of Sulphur Dioxide has come
 down from 23mg/m3 in 1992 to 4.5 mg/m3 in 2002 and SPM level from 438 in 1992 to 385 in 2002.  The Nitrogen
 Oxide level has increased from 15mg/m3 in 1992 to 21.7 mg/m3 in 2002.   It has been observed that hydrogen sulphide
 which generate from the swamp of river Yamuna is causing more damage to the Taj.
12.       The protection of the Taj Mahal has been a major priority and constant efforts are required to save the
 monument. The Committee feels that concerted effort should be taken for the protection of the Taj which suffered
 substantial damage due to pollution. The Committee recommends that instead of involving multiple agencies for the
 protection of Taj, a constituent body be formed which would be responsible for coordination with all allied agencies,
 supported by a strong legal framework for effective implementation of the policies undertaken.

 standards by preventing pollution load reaching the river by undertaking works like (i) interception & diversion works

14.       Under this scheme, polluted stretches of major rivers have been identified for sewage collection and treatment. 
 At present, 153 towns have been considered under the National River Conservation Plan (NRCP), out of which 74
 towns are located on Ganga, and 21 on Yamuna.  This project was started with 100 per cent funding by the Centre. 
 However, given resource constraints, states have to share 30 per cent of the cost during the Tenth Plan.
15.       The first River Action Plan was taken up in 1985 under the NRCP as the Ganga Action Plan (GAP) to improve
 the water quality of the River Ganga.  An amount of Rs.452 crores has been spent on the GAP Phase-I.  The entire
 funding has been provided by the Central Government.  It was initially expected that programme would be completed
 in 6-7 years.  However, it has been delayed considerably due to land acquisition problems, court litigation and lack of
 experience in implementing a programme of this nature and magnitude. The work under NRCP, including that of GAP
 are expected to cost Rs.3780.00. Till January, 2002, Rs.1,295 crores has been spent on the scheme.  About 45 per cent
 of the cleaning of the Ganga has been completed.  However, overall progress is poor because of delays in land
 acquisition and slow pace of work by municipal corporations.
16.       GAP Phase-I,  launched in 1985,  was completed on 31.3.2000 at  a total cost of Rs.452 crores, although two
 projects of sewage treatment plants in Bihar have not been completed so far due to problems of litigation on the land
 proposed for these facilities.  Under this plan, a sewage treatment capacity of 865 million litres per day (mld) has been
 created which will become 882 mld against the target of 873 mld after the remaining two sewage treatment plants are
 completed in Bihar.  This will account for about 35% of the pollution load presently reaching the Ganga.
17.       The Committee was informed by the Ministry that despite the problems of operation & maintenance (O&M) of
 assets created under GAP Phase-I the water quality of the river has shown improvement over the pre-GAP period water
 quality.  It is seen that in 1986, the Bio-Chemical Oxygen Demand (BOD) which is an indicator of pollution of river
 water quality was ranging from 5.5 to 15.5 mg/I in the critical stretch of Ganga from Kannauj to Varanasi.  As against
 this, the value of BOD in 2002 in the stretch of Kannauj to Kanpur was ranging from 1.2 to 4.9 mg/I and that of
 Allahabad to Varanasi from 2.5 to 3.8 mg/I.
18.       In the year 2000, fifteen years after the implementation of the Ganga Action Plan, the Norwegian Institute for
 Water Research, after analysing the Ganga water observed that water of Ganga is polluted with concentration of
 Chemical Oxygen Demand twice as high and the concentration of chrome a thousand times higher compared to
 untreated sewer in Norway. On the occasion of Makar Sankranti (14
th January, 2003) and Mauni  Amavasya (1st
 February, 2003) some group of  sadhus protested strongly about the pollution of Ganga at Sangam caused by discharge
 of industrial effluents from factories upstream  of Kanpur. Secretary, Ministry of Environment and Forests discussed
 the matter with the Chairman, UPPCB and directed that punitive action should be taken against the defaulting
 distilleries and industrial units which created the situation and report compliance.
19.       Since Ganga Action Plan Phase-I did not cover the pollution load of Ganga fully, GAP Phase-II, which includes
 river Ganga and its major tributaries viz. Yamuna, Gomti and Damodar was approved in stages between 1993 and
 1996. It covers pollution abatement  works in 95 towns spread over 7 States viz.  Uttar Pradesh, Haryanan, Delhi,
 Uttaranchal, Bihar, West Bengal and Jharkhand.  The present approved cost of GAP Phase-II is Rs.1499 crores against
 which an expenditure of Rs.763.30 crores has been incurred so far.  The quantity of waste water to be treated under
 GAP Phase-II is about 1860 mld out of which a capacity to treat 728 mld has been created under Yamuna Action Plan
 Phase-I on account of completion of works in Haryana, Uttar Pradesh and Delhi funded by Japan Bank for International
 Cooperation (JBIC).  It may be mentioned that six towns in Haryana and eight towns in U.P will have facilities for
 treatment.  Works in the six additional towns in Haryana which are not covered under the  JBIC assistance are in
 different stages of implementation.
20.       The Committee was informed that the operation and maintenance of various pollution abatement works
 constructed under GAP Phase-I and GAP Phase-II is the responsibility of the respective State Governments as
 committed by them.  However, adequate funds are not being provided in time by all the state Governments including
 the UP government for O&M.   This matter is being constantly pursued with them, as a result of which of UP
 Government has provided Rs.10.70 crores recently to UP Jal Nigam for O&M.  They have also promised that in future
 funds would be regularly provided for proper O&M of assets created under the river action plans.

21.       The Committee was also informed that a series of measures have been taken to improve the performance of
 NRCP, which includes release of money directly to the implementing agencies, minimize slippage on account of delays
 in land acquisition, notices to the local bodies  which are responsible for non-operation of assets in Bihar and U.P., and

 Action Plan town is being evaluated.
22.       The Committee observes that for any plan or programme regarding cleaning of rivers of such a magnitude as
 Ganga, to succeed, it is necessary to have a meticulous planning.  Any delay in its execution is bound to result in the
 plan going awry.  More so, because the parameters regarding the size of the population and the extent of urbanization
 etc., which would have been taken into account at the stage of planning, are subject to rapid changes in a country like
 India.  Hence, timely execution is crucial for such programmes or else we would suffer severe consequences of
 contamination of our rivers on the banks of which our civilization began and flourished.
23.       The Committee is constrained to observe that even after nearly two decades of  Ganga Action Plan (GAP)-I, the
 pollution levels at various places in the River has more or less remained the same. It is seen that every programme
 failed to achieve its stipulated objectives and proved ineffective. The Committee feels that a strong administrative will
 is needed to implement such plans.
24.       The Committee recommends that punitive action should be taken against the defaulting distilleries and industrial
 units which are responsible for the problem. The Committee also recommends that a programme to monitor the
 performance should be introduced and it should be reviewed quarterly.  The Committee also feels that the non-
availability of funds in the Tenth Plan would become a major constraint to effectively address the problem of cleaning
 rivers.  The Committee has been apprised that the Ministry of Environment and Forests has written a letter to the Prime
 Minister to augment  the Tenth Plan outlay to undertake the remaining works of river pollution in Ganges.  The

37.       In a recent article written jointly by Shri Nitin Desai of the UN Department of Economic and Social Affairs; 
 Mark Malloch Brown of UN Development Programme; Klaus Toepffer of UN Environment Programme; Koichiro
 Matsuura of UNESCO, Anna Tribaijuka of UN-Habitat; Carlo Bellamy of UNICEF and Hans Van Ginkel of UN
 University the following has been written which has a bearing on the cleaning of our rivers.
“There are signs of hope.  Up until the middle of the last century many of the rivers on continents like North America
 and Europe and especially those running through big industrial areas were so polluted that they were classed as “dead”. 
 Some were so polluted that the water could be used as ink, and noxious gases, bubbling up from their depths, could be
 ignited by a match.  Today after billions of dollars of investment in water treatment and agreements with industry on
 effluents, fish are again spawning and migrating to their upper reaches through these now relatively clean estuaries and
 tributaries.  The Thames in Britain was officially declared a dead river half a century ago, save for a few mud worms. 
 Today some 120 species including migrating salmon can be found.”

INDUSTRIAL AND HAZARDOUS WASTE
66.       Hazardous waste includes sludge contaminated with heavy metals, wastes from paints, dyes and organic
 chemical units and highly acidic and alkaline wastes.  The major hazardous waste generating industries are petroleum
 and petro-chemicals, pharmaceuticals, pesticides, paint and dyes, fertilizers, inorganic chemicals and general
 engineering industries etc.  The presence of toxic chemicals in solid/liquid effluents from industries and other activities
 leads to ground water contamination. 
67.       The Committee was informed that at present, around 7.2 mt of hazardous waste are generated in the country, out
 of which, according to one estimate, 1.4 mt re recyclable, 0.1 mt to be incinerated and 5.2 mt to be disposed on land. 
 The hazardous waste of 5.3 mt would  require about Rs.1,600 crores a year for treatment and disposal at an estimated
 rate of Rs.3,000 tonne.  In addition, land required for disposal will be around 1km   taking a depth of 4 m and density of
 disposal as 1.2 tonnes/m3.
68.       The Committee was also informed that the Water Act, 1974  and Air Act, 1981 were not sufficient to regulate
 the disposal of hazardous waster and has called for the formulation of the Hazardous Waste Management and Handling
 rules, 1989.  Since then, efforts to make an inventory of hazardous waste were initiated. The initiatives taken in this
 direction are:
Estimate of the hazardous waste inventory in various States and identification of disposal sites based on environment
 impact assessment.
CPCB has prepared a ready reckoner to provide information on the source of the hazardous waste, their characteristics
 and the methods for recycling and disposal.
Training programmes have been organized to deal with hazardous waste management.
Import of hazardous waste containing toxic metals like beryllium, selenium, chromium, thallium, pesticides etc. have
 been restricted on the recommendations of an Expert Committee.
Export and import of waste containing cyanide, mercury and arsenic have been prohibited since December 1996.
The import of waste oil and metals such as brass, zinc and lead for processing to recover resources is fully regulated by
 the Ministry of Environment and Forests.
69.       Though the Committee appreciates the efforts being taken by the Ministry for handling of industrial & hazardous

 know their effect vis-à-vis the money being invested in this highly important programme.  It is hoped that the
 Department shall be able to let the Committee know of the nature and periodicity of such monitoring.

ENVIRONMENTAL EDUCATION, TRAINING AND RESEARCH
72.       The Environmental Education and Training Scheme of the Ministry is precisely meant for creating
 environmental awareness.  Under this scheme, various programmes are conducted every year for creating
 environmental awareness both through non-formal activities as well as through the formal education system.  While the
 programmes like national Environment Awareness Campaign (NEAC), National Green Corps (NGC), use of electronic
 Media etc. fall in the non-formal sector, the Ministry’s initiative of taking up a pilot project for strengthening
 environmental concepts in the school curriculum and the linked Global Learning and Observations to Benefit the
 Environment (GLOBE) programme use the formal education system for creating awareness.  The recent initiatives for
 strengthening Environment Education in Business Schools and development of Environmental Appreciation courses
 also fall in this category.
73.       Environmental Research aims at developing strategies, technologies and methodologies by way of better
 facilities and infrastructure to facilitate research and training of manpower. The Environmental Research Programme
 specially deals with problems related to air, water and soil pollution and development of suitable cost effective
 technology for abatement of pollution load in the environment .  Emphasis is given on development of eco-friendly, 
 biological and other interventions for prevention of pollution and development of strategies/technologies, instruments
 etc.  Many projects have been taken for development of biodegradable plastics. Besides, emphasis has been laid on the
 development of clean technologies.  Many studies have been undertaken to reduce the impact of mining on
 environment. Waste recycling and resource recovery from waste along with development of eco-friendly and cleaner
 technology are given due priority.
74.       To strengthen the system of forestry research in India, the Indian Council of Forestry Research and Education
 (ICFRE) is entrusted to undertake, aid , promote and coordinate forestry research and its application; function as a
 clearing house for research results and information; and disseminate technology.  ICFRE imparts forestry education and
 is developing forestry curricula at various levels to accelerate the pace of research and to provide expertise in different
 fields of forestry research.  Universities providing forestry education are being given grants-in-aid for strengthening
 their infrastructure and technical capabilities.  Opportunities are also being provided for furthering the academic
 advancement of foresters/scientists/academicians in the field of forestry.

NATIONAL FOREST COMMISSION
79.       The constitution of National Forest Commission has been notified on 7.2.2003 by the Ministry of Environment
 & Forests and Wildlife Sector with the following terms of reference:
Review and assess the existing policy and legal framework and their impact in a holistic manner from the ecological,
 scientific, economic, social and cultural viewpoint.
Examine the current status of forest administration and the forestry institutions both on all India and State level to meet
 the emerging needs of the civil society.
Make recommendations indicating policy options for achieving sustainable forest and wildlife management and
 development, bio-diversity conservation and ecological security.
Suggest ways and means to make forest administration more effective with a view to help to achieve the above policy
 options.
Establish meaningful partnership and interface between forestry management and local communities including tribals.

 be given funds from the budget of the Ministry for its smooth functioning.

SUMMARY OF THE RECOMMENDATIONS
1.         The Committee recommends that at R.E. stage curtailment of funds for the Ministry may be avoided so as to
 enable  the smooth and unhindered implementation of programmes  and policies of the Ministry.  The Committee also
 recommends that the programmes and policies may be effectively and substantially implemented at the ground   level
 so as to get the desired result.
(Para 7)
2.         The protection of the Taj Mahal has been a major priority and constant efforts are required to save the
 monument. The Committee feels that concerted effort should be taken for the protection of the Taj which suffered
 substantial damage due to pollution. The Committee recommends that instead of involving multiple agencies for the
 protection of Taj, a constituent body be formed which would be responsible for coordination with all allied agencies,
 supported by a strong legal framework for effective implementation of the policies undertaken. 
(Para 12)
3.         The Committee observes that for any plan or programme regarding cleaning of rivers of such a magnitude as
 Ganga, to succeed, it is necessary to have a meticulous planning.  Any delay in its execution is bound to result in the
 plan going awry.  More so, because the parameters regarding the size of the population and the extent of urbanization
 etc., which would have been taken into account at the stage of planning, are subject to rapid changes in a country like
 India.  Hence, timely execution is crucial for such programmes or else we would suffer severe consequences of
 contamination of our rivers on the banks of which our civilization began and flourished.
(Para 22)
4.         The Committee is constrained to observe that even after nearly two decades of  Ganga Action Plan (GAP)-I, the
 pollution levels at various places in the River has more or less remained the same. It is seen that every programme
 failed to achieve its stipulated objectives and proved ineffective. The Committee feels that a strong administrative will

 notes with concernthat  waste is still dumped in the Yamuna river through drains. The Committee notes with anxiety
 that cleaning of sewerage has become erratic and the functioning of the sewage treatment plants have been stalled due

 cleansing of Yamuna has to go beyond cosmetic measures like removal of slums along the banks and greening of

 implemented by 31.3.2003 or are the Governments responsible for non-compliance, therefore, committing  contempt of
 the Supreme Court.

7.         The Committee is also of the view that the Ministry in coordination with the State Government of Uttar Pradesh,
 Haryana and Delhi should plan a strategy for augmentation of water resources in the upper stretches of the Yamuna and
 for conserving water both for domestic and irrigational purposes. 
(Para 35)
8.         The Committee is of the view that the present status of the river cleansing programme is not satisfactory and
 under these circumstances it would not be possible to achieve the target of clearing all the rivers by 2007.  All the
 phase-wise programmes need to be monitored and the Measure of Performance (MOP) should be introduced to review
 the cleaning activities.
(Para 36)
9.         The Committee therefore feels that efforts at the government and community levels must be redoubled to clean
 our rivers.
(Para 38)

 mythologies show as to how norms were prescribed to keep the water of the Ganges clean.  The Committee observed
 that the Bramhanda Purna prohibited thirteen actions to maintain the purity of the Ganges. Some of these are (i)
 defecations (ii) ablutions (iii) discharge of waste water (iv) throwing of used floral offerings (v) rubbing of filth (vi)
 body shampooing (vii) frolicking                (viii) acceptance of donations into the rivers (ix) obscenity (x) discarding or
 cleaning of garments (xi) beating and swimming across.  The Committee recommends that many of these can be
 incorporated by the Ministry in their norms for prohibiting activities to keep the Ganges clean.

 forest fires continues unabated.  The problem cannot be overcome unless a consciousness is built among the people that
