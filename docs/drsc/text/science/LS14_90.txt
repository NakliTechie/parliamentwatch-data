R E P O R T
 
      The Ministry of Environment and Forests is entrusted with the responsibility of planning, promotion and coordination of environment and
 forestry programmes. The activities of the Ministry include conservation and control of pollution, afforestation and regeneration of forests in
 degraded areas, protection and conservation of forests, wildlife and protection of environment.  The tasks are sought to be achieved through
 environmental impact assessment, eco-regeneration, assistance to organisations implementing environmental and forestry programmes, enactment
 of environmental legislation, formulation of environmental policies, promotion of environmental and forestry research, extension, education and
 training to augment the requisite manpower, dissemination of environmental awareness among all sectors of the country’s population.  The
 activities of the Ministry are divided in four major sectors, i.e.
1.       Environment;
2.       National River Conservation Directorate;
3.       Forests and Wildlife; and
4.       National Afforestation and Eco-Development Board.

 defaulting units (initially in 1993 there were 540 units which came to 53 units in 2000).

9.1  The Committee observes that the national emission standards introduced by the government are nothing more than the minimum requirement. 
 In the interest of a clean environment there is perhaps a need for more rigid standards.  At the same time there is a need to consider whether any
 particular type of fuel or gas, say CNG alone will result in a clean and green environment.  Whether it is safe to use it?  A decision has to be taken,

9.2  The  Committee is aware that  the Ministry of Environment & Forests enjoys statutory powers under the Air Act, 1981 and the Environment

 controversy with regard to use of CNG in public transport and help bring a harmonious solution to the problem with a view to control the pollution
 without causing hardships to any section of the society.
NATIONAL RIVER CONSERVATION PLAN INCLUDING GANGA ACTION PLAN
10.  The Secretary informed the Committee that the Ganga Action Plan (GAP)  referred to in the Comptroller and Auditor General’s Report for the
 year ending March,2000 includes works under GAP Phase-I and Phase-II . The GAP Phase-I, which was started in 1985, has been declared
 completed as on 31.03.2000.  Although two schemes of sewage treatment plants in Bihar are yet to be completed.  The total expenditure incurred
 on this plan is Rs. 452 crore. Pollution abatement works in 25 towns in the States of UP, Bihar and West Bengal have been taken up under    Phase-
I. Under this plan, a capacity of 835 million litres per day (mld) has been created against the target of 873 mld. The total estimated sewage in 1985
 was 1340 mld. Thus, pollution load treated in terms of pollution level of 1985 is about 65%, but in terms of the present pollution level, it would
 amount to about 35% only. Certain problems of operation & maintenance of assets created under GAP Phase I have emerged in Bihar and to some
 extent in Uttar Pradesh. The matter was being constantly pursued with the State Governments concerned at the highest level. These were the major
 reasons for a lower visibility of impact of GAP Phase I on the water quality of river Ganga. However, the water quality monitoring results
 indicated that wherever the pollution abatement works have been commissioned and made functional, the river water quality has shown
 improvement. For example, in 1986 the biochemical oxygen demand (BOD) ranged from  5.5 to 15.5 mg/l in the stretch from Kannauj to Varanasi
 whereas the BOD in this stretch ranged from 1.8 to 6.5 mg/l in 1999.
10.1 As for the GAP Phase II, the programme was approved in 3 stages from 1993 to 1996. A total capacity of about 1861 mld of sewage treatment
 along with allied works was to be created under this Plan. The works of Yamuna Action Plan under GAP Phase II in Haryana ( 12 towns), U.P. (8
 towns) and Delhi were in an advanced stage of completion.
10.2   The Committee observes that for any plan or programme regarding cleansing of rivers of such a magnitude as Ganga, to succeed, it is
 necessary to have a meticulous planning.  Any delay in its execution is bound to result in the plan going awry.  More so, because the parameters
 regarding the size of the population and the extent of urbanization etc., which would have been taken into account at the stage of planning, are
 subject to rapid changes in a country like India.  Hence, timely execution is crucial for such programmes or else we would be back to square one.
10.3 At the same time it is necessary to see whether any alternative effective indigenous technologies can be advantageously used instead of
 draining out vast sums from national exchequer for which no audit may be possible.
10.4 The Committee has been given to understand that a team of experts from Varanasi has devised a method to rid the Ganga of a big chunk of its
 pollutant load, using the inherent energy of the flowing waters and the sun and the filtering properties of the riverbed.
10.5   Banaras Hindu University’s (BHU) Ganga Laboratory has developed the “three gradient theory”, using the river’s sand bed, solar energy and
 the kinetic energy of the water that could prove a vital pollution management tool in cities located along the country’s rivers that are struggling to
 contain water pollution.
10.6   The Committee understands that the system basically filters the inorganic, organic and microbiological pollutant load of the river – usually
 left untreated by sewage treatment plants (STPs) – through the sand piled up on the concave bank or downstream and upstreamofthecity.
 10.7  “The polluted water first flows out of a trickling box – which is a large filter comprising sand and stone layers and then the sand bed.  It then
 mingles with the high velocity zone in the river for a natural course of flow again”.  The problem of pollution is acute between December and July,
 which is the dry season, when the river level falls below 65 meters and the dissolved oxygen also decreases.  But this energizes the sand bed as
 solar radiation converts it into a hot plate to treat a higher quantum of inorganic and microbiological waste.
10.8  The Secretary also apprised the Committee the reasons for delayed implementation of GAP Phase II  which are as under:-
(i)
                    The scheme of National River Conservation Plan of which GAP Phase II is a part, was originally envisaged on 50:50 cost sharing basis between Centre and States.
 However, in view of the difficulties of the State Governments in bringing their 50%  share,  the National River Conservation Authority under the Chairmanship of Prime
 Minister at its 9th Meeting held in July, 1997 converted the scheme into a 100% Centrally Funded Scheme. But the Cabinet approval to this decision could be obtained
 only in November, 1998. During the intervening period of 16 months, the States were not providing their share in anticipation that the scheme would become 100%
 Centrally funded. As a result, the progress of the scheme became very slow.
 (ii)       The convention technologies proposed earlier did not address the problem of bacterial pollution fully. In order to address this issue, a decision was taken in July, 1999 to
 adopt oxidation pond based sewage treatment in all the places. These ponds, although requiring large areas of land were capable of reducing fecal coliform suitably. The
 State Governments could not provided such areas of land in larger towns which account for a major chunk of the capital cost. As a result, the progress of work was
 extremely slow for nearly 18 months.
(iii)
                  Until 1999-2000, grants were released to the State Governments. Transfer of such grants to the implementing agencies were invariably delayed in almost all the States
 for a period ranging from 6-9 months affecting the progress of work adversely.
(iv)                 The quality of preparation of Detailed Project Reports was not up to the mark from a number of States which resulted in significant delays.
(v)                  There were delays in land acquisition.
(vi)                 Decision was taken by the Ministry not to release funds for GAP Phase II in Bihar because the operation and maintenance of assets created under GAP Phase I was not
 satisfactory.
10.9   The Ministry was of the view that most of these bottlenecks have now been resolved and the plan was targeted for completion by 2005. The
 impact of the Action Plan on the improvement of water quality of Ganga and its tributaries can be expected only after its completion.

YAMUNA ACTION PLAN
11.  About 1.3 billion litres of untreated sewage is discharged into the river Yamuna in Delhi everyday. This has been revealed by a joint inspection
 report of the Central Pollution Control Board of the Ministry of Environment and Forests and Central Public Health and Environmental
 Engineering Organisation of the Ministry of Urban Development.
11.1 The Yamuna Action Plan was approved by the Government in April 1993 with an approved cost of Rs.509.54 crore. Under this Plan, works
 have been taken up in 21 towns out of which 8 towns are in Uttar Pradesh and 12 towns in Haryana besides Delhi. The Plan includes such works as
 interception & diversion of sewage, sewage treatment plants, low cost toilets, crematoria and river front development. So far, an expenditure of
 Rs.453 crore has been incurred on this Plan. The Delhi component of Yamuna Action Plan was small comprising 2 sewage treatment plants each of
 10 million litres per day capacity and one electric crematorium.
11.2 The Government of National Capital Territory of Delhi was implementing the major part of pollution abatement programme of Yamuna out of
 their own plan funds. 14 additional sewage treatment plants along with allied works are to be constructed under the programme. Out of these, 9
 treatment plants have already been completed and another 5 would be completed by the year 2003. Besides, 15 common effluent treatment plants
 were also to be constructed in order to treat industrial effluents emanating from 21 industrial estates of Delhi. This Project was under
 implementation and would be completed by December 2002.  A number of steps have been taken
by the Government of National Capital Territory of Delhi under the directions of the Supreme Court to close down polluting industries. In pursuance of the directions of a High Powered
 Committee set up under the chairmanship of Member (environment), Planning Commission on minimum flow in river Yamuna, all the riparian states except Delhi have started releasing
 their respective share of water for maintaining 10 cusecs of minimum flow in Yamuna along Delhi. The quality of river water and underground water in the area is expected to improve
 only after all the common effluent treatment plants and sewage treatment plants have been constructed & commissioned and pollution from other industries would effectively be
 monitored and controlled.
11.1     In  Delhi,  out of  16 proposed Sewage Treatment Plants (STPs) along the banks of the  Yamuna, 8 have been completed while 5 were operating. The Committee is
 constrained to observe that despite the Supreme Court orders in  1995  for setting up of STPs, number of treatment plants have not been established. The Committee has been given
 to understand that out of 22 drains discharging effluents into the Yamuna, 20 have not met the water quality standard. Rather the quality of the river water has deteriorated because of
 continuous discharge of domestic and industrial effluents into the river.  It has been further stated that the dissolved oxygen in the river water was nil while the river should contain
 minimum 4 mg/l of dissolved oxygen. The river has 41 mg/l of Biological Oxygen Demand(BoD), which is much higher than the prescribed limit, of minimum 3mg/l.
11.2     The Committee is also of the view that the Ministry of Environment & Forests  in coordination with the State Governments of Uttar Pradesh, Haryana and Delhi should  plan out
 a strategy for augmentation of water resources in the upper stretches of Yamuna and for conserving water both in domestic and irrigation use.
FOREST FIRE CONTROL & MANAGEMENT
12.  A National Master plan for prevention and control of forest fires in India has been prepared.  Funds have been released to 26 States under
 Centrally Sponsored Scheme 'Forest Fire Control & Management" during 2000-2001. This Scheme was different from the earlier scheme as
 follows:-
•   The present scheme was extended to cover all the States of the country. Previously only 11 States were being funded.
•   Traditional methods like fire line creation etc. would be reemphasized for fire prevention & control along with the use of modern fire fighting methods.
•   A Joint Forest Management (JFM) component has been introduced under which co-operation of the local people would be enlisted for prevention & control of forest fires for
 which they would be provided with incentives.
•   Air Operation Wing would be closed.
•     Remote Sensing methods would be used for early detection & monitoring of forest fires.

WILD LIFE  PROTECTION
14.  The Secretary referred to the news reports that have been appearing frequently in the media for past some time about the death of Rhinos,
 tigers, elephants, Peacocks in one or the other part of the country.  The Secretary in the Ministry referred to the reports regarding Death of Tigers in
 Jim Corbett National Park, Uttaranchal and Srisailam Tiger Reserve in Andhra Pradesh. He stated that as per reports received from State
 Government death of two tigers in Jim Corbett National Park appeared to have been caused by infighting. One tiger had been killed in Nallmala
 Forests of Nagarjun Sagar of Srisailam Tiger Reserve by using the snare. Forest and Police Officers had arrested five persons in this regard.
14.1 The Committee was informed that the Tiger census was carried out every fourth year. Last two tiger census were conducted in the year 1993 and 1997. The estimated tiger
 population was 3750 and 3810 in 1993 and 1997 respectively which showed an increase in the tiger population. Next tiger census would be conducted in the current year i.e. 2001.
14.2   The Committee was also apprised that following steps were taken to protect wildlife.
Steps taken at the State level:
(i)
                    Legal protection has been provided to wild animals against hunting and commercialisation.
(ii)                   Protection under the provisions of the Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972.
(iii)                  State level and District level coordination committees have been set up in several states.
(iv)                 Steps to prevent poaching and illegal trade in wildlife.
Steps taken at National level :
(i)                  Government have set up Regional and Sub-regional Offices for wildlife preservation in major export and trade centres of the
 country to prevent smuggling of wild animals and their products.
(ii)                   Central Bureau of Investigation (CBI) has been empowered under the Wildlife  (Protection)  Act,  1972 to  apprehend  and  prosecute
wildlife offenders. Anti poaching efforts were being coordinated with INTERPOL.
(iii)                                          Financial and technical help was being extended to the States under various Centrally Sponsored Schemes, viz. Project Tiger, Project Elephant,
 Development of National Parks and Sanctuaries and Eco-development around Protected Areas for enhancing the capacity and infrastructure of the States for
 providing effective protection to wild animals. Financial assistance was, in particular, being provided for raising 'Strike Forces' and providing arms to the protection
 staff for combating the organised poachers. Assistance was also provided for giving rewards to the informers for eliciting information about poachers and smugglers.
(iv)       A Special Coordination and Enforcement Committee had been set up under the chairmanship of Secretary, Environment & Forests, Government of India, for control of
 poaching and illegal trade in wildlife.
(v)       Export of wild animals and their derivatives was prohibited under EXIM policy.
(vi)       Ministry of Home Affairs has written to all the State Governments to lend a helping hand to the field formations to curb poaching and illegal trade in wildlife. Recently,
 a meeting of State Ministers of Environment & Forests was held on 29- 30th January, 2001 at Coimbatore in which following resolutions on agreed priority areas for
 Wildlife Conservation were adopted-
(a)        Formulation of an appropriate insurance scheme for   compensating damage to agriculture crops failing within 5 Kms. of National Parks and
 Sanctuaries.
(b)       Provisions for payment of compensation in cases of death due to wild animals were emphasised, with the objective of ensuring compliance by the
 State/Union Territory Governments.
(c)       The ban on recruitment of forestry personnel should be lifted and vacancies filled up immediately on the lines of Police Deptt. The field formation would be
 reorganized into viable units and provided with adequate mobility, firearms and effective communication network.
(d)       An effective mechanism for intelligence gathering and database of habitual offenders should be developed for preventing wildlife crimes.
                   (e)       Adequate expertise would be developed for prosecuting offenders and training organized for undertaking investigations.
(f)        Steps would be taken for designating special courts for wildlife offences.
                   (g)       To create effective enforcement infrastructure both within and outside Protected Areas to prevent poaching.
 
Steps taken at International level:
(i)
                    Government of India seeks international co-operation under the provisions of the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Flora and Fauna
 (CITES) for control of illegal trade in wildlife items.
 
(ii)        A Memorandum of Understanding (MOU) with Nepal has been signed by India to establish a joint Task Force to check the trafficking
 across the borders.
(iii)       Indo-Chinese Protocol in tiger conservation Article of the protocol inter-alia stipulates joint measures to crackdown illegal activities of poaching of tigers, smuggling and
 selling the tiger bones and other parts of the tiger as well as its derivatives.
(iv)       A Global Tiger Forum of Tiger Range Countries has been created for addressing international issues related to Tiger Conservation.

FOREST CONSERVATION, DEVELOPMENT & REGENERATION
17.  Forest Policy Division was implementing a Centrally Sponsored Scheme of "Association of Scheduled Tribes and Rural Poor in regeneration
 of Degraded Forests on Usufruct Sharing Basis. The Scheme was being implemented in the States of Andhra Pradesh, Arunachal Pradesh,
 Jharkhand, Gujarat, Jammu & Kashmir, Karnataka, Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh Maharashtra, Manipur, Mizoram, Nagaland, Orissa, Rajasthan,
 Tripura and West Bengal. The Objectives of the Scheme are:
(1)
      To improve forest based biomass resource in degraded forest lands and to manage it on a sustained basis for the domestic needs of the identified communities.
(2)  To involve local Scheduled Tribes and other Rural Poor in protection and rehabilitation of degraded forests for maintenance of ecology
 of the area and for sustenance of local communities.
(3)  To provide gainful employment and sustainable economic base to scheduled tribes and other rural poor in the vicinity of their
 habitation.
 
17.1   The total outlay for the Ninth Plan was Rs.1500 lakhs for rehabilitation of an area of 15667 ha. Budget Allocation for the Scheme during
 2000-01 was Rs.750 Lakhs  (including Rs.3.5 lakhs for North Eastern States), which was revised to Rs.356.75 lakhs at RE stage for 2000-01.
 During the ninth plan till March, 2001 the Ministry had released an amount of Rs.951.852 lakhs out of allocated fund of Rs.1500 lakhs and thus
 approx., an amount of Rs.550 lakhs would be available against the sanctioned work programme during 9th Plan. In order to implement the Scheme
 and achieve the approved target, Budget allocation of 2001- 2002 has been kept at Rs.535 lakhs  which includes Rs.135 lakhs for North Eastern
 States. The work proposed to be carried out during 2001-2002 includes maintenance of Plantations raised during previous years, aided natural
 regeneration, soil and moisture conservation, training to tribals etc. as per the approved scheme.
18.The Committee appreciates that the development of forestry sector has now got the active involvement of people and this effort has to be further strengthened. There is, however,
 much still to be done in this area as the phenomenon of cutting of trees, removal of precious timber and mysterious forest fires continues unabated.  The problem cannot be overcome
 unless a consciousness is built among the people that the forests belong to them and they belong to the forests and the feeling of alienation among the tribals and local population
 goes.  The Committee feels that the local population (including tribals) residing in the forest areas should be actively associated with the management of forests and  allowed to share
 the usufructs, without in any way compromising on the preservation of our national forests. If this happens, many of the ills currently afflicting our forests will disappear.  The Ministry
 should devise steps to inculcate among them, a sense of belonging and purpose about the forests. The people performing outstanding work in their area should be suitably rewarded
 and honoured at the appropriate forum.

            RED OIL PALM PROJECT-ANDAMAN & NICOBAR ISLANDS FOREST & PLANTATION DEVELOPMENT
 CORPORATION LIMITED
24.During its visit to Andaman & Nicobar Islands the Committee was informed that in order to increase the availability of edible oil in the country, the Government of India decided to
 take up a Red Oil Palm Plantation Project (ROP) in Andamans which is agro climatically similar to Malaysia and Indonesia.  The project was accordingly sanctioned for raising Red Oil
 Palm in 2400 ha. and the project was entrusted to Andaman & Nicobar Islands Forest & Plantation Development Corporation Limited. An area of about 1593 ha has been brought under
 Red Oil Palm cultivation till 1985. In the year 1984 an expert Committee appointed by the Andaman & Nicobar Administration identified about 68,000 ha. of area suitable for Red Oil
 Palm
 cultivation. In view of adverse impact  on  the local  ecology perceived due to  monoculture of  Red Oil Palm
plantations the Government of India put a ban on further plantation of Red Oil Palm from 1986.  The project was thus stopped halfway. Various Project constraints cited were as under:-
 
(a)  Ban imposed by the Government of India in 1986 on further extension of the project;
 
(b)  Ban confirmed by the Island Development Authority in 1994;
 
(c)  Existing size of Plantation in 1593 ha. of land was unviable;
 
(d)  Unviable size of the project would require low investments and hence low productivity and
 
(e)  Large import of edible oils due to import duties resulting in decline in prices.
 
25.1 The Island Development Authority commissioned the Central Agricultural Research Institute(CARI), Port Blair to conduct a study on the
 impact of Red Oil Palm Cultivation on the local ecology in the year 1987.  The Report of CARI, was published in 1996.  According to the report
 cultivation of Red Oil Palm has no significant adverse impact on the local ecosystem. The Report further stated that Little Andaman Island was
 most suitable agro-climatically for cultivation of Red Oil Palm.
26.The Committee notes that on the basis of the Report submitted by the Central Agricultural Research Institute(CARI), Port Blair which conducted a study on the impact of Red Oil Palm
 Cultivation on the local ecology, there has not been any significant adverse impact on the ecology of the Islands.  The report has also pointed out that Little Andaman Island was most
 suitable agro-climatically for cultivation of Red Oil Palm.  The Committee therefore recommends that the Ministry of Environment & Forests should go ahead with the plantation/cultivation
 of Red Oil Palm for extension to 2,400 ha. which was originally sanctioned in 1979 and thereafter to 5,000 ha. so that a full fledged refinery unit could be established in Andaman &
 Nicobar Islands and the Project completed.  This would also provide succour to Andaman & Nicobar Islands Forest & Plantation Development Corporation Ltd., which at present is a
 venture, incurring losses.
27.After considering the Demands for Grants of the Ministry of Environment & Forests and assessing the progress of various programmes and projects administered by it, the Committee
 recommends that the Demands for Grants of Rs. 911.00 crores as sought by the Ministry of Environment & Forests for the year 2000-2001, under Demand No.21  may be approved.
RECOMMENDATIONS/OBSERVATIONS AT GLANCE
1.  The Committee observes that there is need for radical transformation in the attitude of officials concerned with inspection of the level of pollution generated by industrial units.  The
 attitude should be objective, need based and be guided by the need to control the pollution in the interest of present generation as well as posterity.
The Ministry should also prepare an Action Plan for already identified 24 critically polluted areas of the country for improvement of Environment.
 (Para 8.2)
2.   The Committee observes that the national emission standards  introduced by the government are nothing more than the minimum requirement. 
 In the interest of a clean environment there is perhaps a need for more rigid standards.  At the same time there is a need to consider whether any
 particular type of fuel or gas, say CNG alone will result in a clean and green environment.  Whether it is safe to use it?  A decision has to be taken,
 keeping in view the infrastructure available and without causing hardships to commuters and throwing the entire economy out of gear.
(Para 9.1)
3.The  Committee is aware that  the Ministry of Environment & Forests enjoys statutory powers under the Air Act, 1981 and the Environment (Protection) Act to enforce more stringent
 regime of standards with a view to control pollution.  The Ministry should have stepped in the current controversy with regard to use of CNG in public transport and help bring a
 harmonious solution to the problem with a view to control the pollution without causing hardships to any section of the society.
(Para 9.2)
 
4.   The Committee observes that for any plan or programme regarding cleansing of rivers of such a magnitude as Ganga, to succeed, it is necessary
 to have a meticulous planning.  Any delay in its execution is bound to result in the plan going awry.  More so, because the parameters regarding the
 size of the population and the extent of urbanization etc., which would have been taken into account at the stage of planning are subject to rapid
 changes in a country like India.  Hence, timely execution is crucial for such programmes or else we would be back to square one.
(Para 10.2)

 pollutant load, using the inherent energy of the flowing waters and the sun and the filtering properties of the riverbed.
(Para 10.4)
 
7.   Banaras Hindu University’s (BHU) Ganga Laboratory has developed the “three gradient theory”, using the river’s sand bed, solar energy and
 the kinetic energy of the water that could prove a vital pollution management tool in cities located along the country’s rivers that are struggling to
 contain water pollution.
(Para 10.5)
 
8.   The Committee understands that the system basically filters the inorganic, organic and microbiological pollutant load of the river – usually left
 untreated by sewage treatment plants (STPs) – through the sand piled up on the concave bank or downstream and upstream of the city.
(Para 10.6)
9.
  “The polluted water first flows out of a trickling box – which is a large filter comprising sand and stone layers and then the sand bed.  It then mingles with the high velocity zone in
 the river for a natural course of flow again”.  The problem of pollution is acute between December and July, which is the dry season, when the river level falls below 65 meters and
 the dissolved oxygen also decreases.  But this energizes the sand bed as solar radiation converts it into a hot plate to treat a higher quantum of inorganic and microbiological waste.
(Para 10.7)
10.  The Committee observes that the Ganga Action Plan, launched in 1985, with the objective of bringing water quality of river Ganga and its
 tributaries to bathing levels , has not been able to achieve its objective till date. GAP Phase-I of the plan is not yet fully complete, even after delay
 of over 10 years. GAP Phase-II is also far behind schedule, and is due to end in December 2001. There are reports of creation of only 13.7% of the
 targeted sewage treatment capacity so far. The assets created under the Scheme suffered serious impairment and often closure particularly in Bihar
 and Uttar Pradesh because of technical flaws in design, problems in land acquisition, contract mismanagement, lack of maintenance and in general
 because of the lackadaisical attitude of the States and their implementing agencies. Technologies adopted by the National River Conservation
 Directorate (NRCD) for construction of Sewage Treatment Plants (STPs) were often questionable inasmuch as they could not adequately address
 the problem of reducing bacterial load in the river to the desired level.  The NRCD has abandoned the crucial activity of monitoring the water
 quality on river Ganga since September, 1999, reportedly for want of funds, and deprived itself of a key instrument of overall performance
 monitoring of the GAP. The Ministry of Environment and Forests needs to seriously review the implementation of the entire GAP and  evolve a
 financing arrangement whereby the States and the implementing agencies develop a more involved stake in creation of assets, their maintenance,
 and their functionality at all times; review and strengthen technical and administrative monitoring to ensure the value for money and assets created
 at great public expense; and facilitate competent technological support for optimum utilization of resources.
(Para 10.10)

(Para 10.11)
