  INTRODUCTION 
 
 
          I,  the  Chairman of the Department -related Parliamentary Standing Committee on Science 
and Technology, Environment, Forests and Climate Change, having been authorized by the 
Committee to present the Report on its behalf, present this Four Hundred Fourth Report on  
“Review of the functioning of Tata Memorial Centre (TMC), Bhabha Atomic Research Centre 
(BARC) and Nuclear Power Corporation of India Limited (NPCIL)”. 
2.   Tata Memorial Centre (TMC) is a grant -in-aid institute under the Department of Atomic 
Energy (DAE). It serves approximately 150,000 new cancer patients annually, making it one of 
India‟s largest and most advanced cancer care institutions. The Committee visited TMC on 21 st 
September, 2025 as part of its study tour to Chandigarh and Mumbai from 19th to 2 2nd September 
2025 to explore the remarkable contributions being made by the institute in the field of cancer 
research and treatment, and to discuss the challenges and opportunities that lie ahead in advancing 
India‟s capabilities in these critical areas. 
3.  Bhabha Atomic Research Centre (BARC) is an R&D institution under the Department of 
Atomic Energy. Nuclear Power Corporation of India Limited (NPCIL) is a Public Sector Enterprise 
under the administrative control of the  Department of Atomic Energy (DAE) , responsible for 
design, construction, commissioning and operation of nuclear power reactors. The Committee 
visited BARC on 21 st September 2025 as part of its study tour to Chandigarh and Mumbai from 
19th to 22nd September 2025 to gain first -hand information about various aspects such as progress 
and roadmap of India‟s three-stage nuclear power programme, advancements in indigenous reactor 
technology, including Small Modular Reactors (SMRs) and the pivotal thorium cycle, the critical 
challenges of capacity  addition, financing, and public acceptance, and the strategies to address 
them and the unparalleled record of safety, security, and operational excellence. 
4.   The Committee expresses its thanks to the officers of the Department of Atomic Energy, 
Bhabha Atomic Research Centre, Nuclear Power Corporation of India Limited and oncologists and 
administration of Tata Memorial Centre for placing before the Committee the required material and 
replying to the clarifications sought by the Members on the subject. 
5.      In the meeting held on 9 th December, 2025, the Committee considered the draft Report and 
adopted the same.

REPORT  
CHAPTER –I 
TATA MEMORIAL CENTRE  
Introduction 
1.1 Tata Memorial Centre (TMC) is a grant -in-aid institute under the Department 
of Atomic Energy (DAE). It was established in 1941 as the Tata  Memorial Hospital 
(TMH) by Sir Dorabji Tata Trust amidst the challenges of Wor ld War II in memory 
of Lady Meherbai Dorabji Tata, who succumbed to blood cancer in 1931. In 1952, 
the Indian Cancer Research Centre (later renamed the Cancer Research Institute, 
CRI) was established to foster cutting -edge research in the field of cancer.  The Trust 
transferred TMH‟s administration to the Ministry of Health in 1957, and in 1962, it 
was designated a grant -in-aid institute under the DAE at the behest of Dr. Homi 
Jehangir Bhabha. On January 6, 1966, TMH and CRI were merged to form the Tata 
Memorial Centre, which today serves approximately 150,000 new cancer patients 
annually, making it one of India‟s largest and most adv anced cancer care institutions. 
International Atomic Energy Agency has  recently designated TMC as a „Ray of 
Hope‟ Anchor Centre. 
 
1.2 TMC presently operates 9 units under its umbrella, each serving specific aspect 
of cancer care. The flagship Tata Memorial Hospital in Mumbai houses 648 beds 
dedicated to patient treatment, serving as the primary referral centre for complex 
cases. The Advanced Centre for Treatment, Research, and Education in Cancer 
(ACTREC) in Kharghar, Navi Mumbai, initially established primarily as a research 
facility, now offers 480 beds, a dedicated Women & Children's Cancer Centre, and 
the specialized Radi ological Research Unit (RRU). The RRU, with 40 beds, is 
positioned to become the world's largest therapeutic nuclear medicine treatment 
facility. ACTREC also houses India's first and only public secto r Proton Beam 
Therapy facility. This advanced form of ra diation therapy is provided at subsidized 
rates, making cutting -edge treatment accessible to Indian patients who would 
otherwise need to seek such treatment abroad at significantly higher costs.  Between 
2014 and 2023, TMC established several regional cance r hospitals, benefiting local 

2 
 
populations across diverse geographical areas including Visakhapatnam, Punjab, 
Varanasi, Guwahati, and Bihar.  
 
Study visit of the Committee  
1.3 The Department -related Parliamentary Standing Committee on Science and 
Technology, Environment, Forests and Climate Change visited Advanced Centre for 
Treatment, Research and Education in Cancer (ACTREC), Tata Memorial Centre 
(TMC), Kharghar, Navi Mumbai on 21stSeptember, 2025 as part of its study tour to 
Chandigarh and Mumbai  from 19th to 22 nd September 2025 . The purpose of 
Committee‟s visit to TMC was to explore the remarkable contributions being made 
by the institute in the field of cancer research and treatment, and to discuss the 
challenges and opportunities that lie ahead in adva ncing India‟s capabilities in these 
critical areas . During the visit, the Committee interacted with the Secretary and 
representatives of Department of Atomic Energy& BARC; Director, TMC and the 
team of oncologists working at TMC on the subject „Enhancing C ancer Care 
Delivery in India: Challenges, Strategies and Way Forward‟. The complete list of 
officials who were present during the meeting is at Annexure I. 
1.4 The Committee had earlier presented its Three Hu ndred and Twenty -fifth 
report on TMC titled  „An expanded role for the Department of Atomic Energy in 
Cancer treatment in India through an enlarged network of the Tata Memorial Centre‟ 
in the year 2019. 
Cancer care Infrastructure in India and Disparities 
1.5 The Committee sought information on the c urrent distribution of cancer care 
infrastructure across India. In response, TMC informed the Committee that cancer 
care infrastructure in the country remains unevenly and inequitably distributed and 
continues to face the following challenges: 
a. Disparities in Research and Clinical Trials: This is primarily due to lack of 
trained professional as well as infrastructure to conduct research. Allocation 
of funds for research in most government run cancer facilities is deficient 
and private hospitals hardly invest in cancer research. 
b. Infrastructure Gaps: Many cancer centres are concentrated in urban hubs, 

3 
 
leaving vast regions underserved. Many districts lack comprehensive cancer 
centres with integrated diagnostics, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy that is 
essential f or multi -disciplinary cancer care. Similarly, Northeast India has 
very high incidence but poor infrastructure to handle that. The Southern and 
Western States are better equipped for cancer care.  
c. Registry and Data Gaps: India's population -based cancer regi stries show 
uneven coverage with some states lacking reliable data to guide policy. This 
hamper targeted interventions and resource allocation. 
d. Establishing a Next -Generation IT Backbone for Comprehensive Cancer 
Care: Critical implementation challenges inc lude ensuring data security and 
patient privacy while maintaining system accessibility; achieving 
interoperability with national health initiatives like Ayushman Bharat Digital 
Mission; designing user -centric interfaces that enhance rather than impede 
clinical workflows; and managing change adoption across diverse healthcare 
professionals. Additionally, the infrastructure must accommodate oncology -
specific requirements including complex treatment protocols, multi-specialty 
coordination, and longitudinal pat ient tracking while supporting data 
analytics for evidence -based decision -making and policy planning across 
regional cancer care networks. 
Cancer burden in India  
1.6 During the interaction, the Committee sought information on the incidence of 
cancer in India and globally. In response, TMC stated that cancer continues to pose a 
major global health challenge, with approximately 20 million new cases reported 
worldwide in 2022, a number expected to exceed 30 million by 2040, according to 
the latest WHO data.  Low- and middle -income countries (LMICs) carry a 
disproportionate share of this burden, accounting for around 70% of cancer -related 
deaths due to limited access to timely diagnosis and treatment. International 
initiatives, including WHO programs targeting  childhood, breast, and cervical 
cancers, aim to reduce premature cancer mortality by one -third by 2030. Recent 
advances, such as personalized cancer vaccines, precision oncology, and AI -driven 
diagnostics, are transforming treatment by enabling more targe ted and effective 
therapies. However, ensuring equitable access to these innovations remains a major 
challenge, particularly in resource-limited settings.  

4 
 
1.7 In India, the cancer burden is rising rapidly, with 1.46 million new cases 
reported in 2022, projected to reach 1.57 million annually by 2025, corresponding to 
a crude incidence rate of 100.4 per 100,000 population. Lung cancer and breast 
cancer are the most common types among men and women, respectively, while 
lymphoid leukemia predominates in pedia tric cases. Northeast India has the highest 
incidence of cancers.  Besides breast and lung cancers, the Northeast suffers from 
high incidence of gastric, oesophageal, gall -bladder and oral and hypopharyngeal 
cancers. India is addressing this growing challenge through significant investments in 
healthcare infrastructure, indigenous treatment innovations, and expanded screening 
programs. Despite these efforts, gaps persist in rural access, workforce capacity, and 
early detection, highlighting the need for a co mprehensive and inclusive strategy to 
ensure equitable cancer care delivery. 
1.8 The Committee notes with concern the increasing number of cancer cases 
in the country, particularly the significantly higher incidence reported in the 
North-Eastern region of India. The Committee believes that it is essential to 
examine whether the ethnic groups of the Northeast are  genetically predisposed 
to specific types of cancers. To address this, the Committee recommends TMC 
to collaborate with the CSIR-Institute of Genomics and Integrative Biology 
(IGIB), which has been conducting extensive work in population and cancer 
genomics. Such collaborative research should aim to identify genomic signatures 
and risk factors unique to the North -Eastern population. The Committe e feels 
that these insights will be critical for developing tailored cancer therapies, 
targeted prevention methods and early detection strategies for the northeastern 
population.  
Notification of cancer as a documentable disease 
1.9 During the interaction,  the Committee observed that only 19% of India‟s 
population is currently covered by the population -based cancer registry program. As 
cancer is not a „Notifiable disease‟, hospitals and medical practitioners are not legally 
required to report cancer cases to public health authorities. Thus, the true incidence of 
cancer in the country remains unknown. As a result, existing estimates are largely 
based on extrapolations from data collected by these registries. 

5 
 
1.10 The Committee then sought information on notifiable diseases, the criteria used 
to classify a disease as notifiable, and the potential benefits of designating cancer as a 
notifiable disease. In response, TMC informed that any disease that is required by law 
to be reported to the public health authorit ies is called a „Notifiable‟ disease. The 
purpose of such notification is to facilitate a prompt investigation, risk assessment for 
human health, and response to cases of infectious disease and contamination. Medical 
practitioners and diagnostic labs are r equired to notify the local health department of 
cases of notifiable diseases. In India, the state government is responsible for 
determining which diseases must be reported to the medical officer in their area and 
notifying them of the diseases. The govern ment and regional authorities maintain a 
list of notifiable diseases in India, which is subject to change as new diseases are 
added or existing ones are removed. In India, there are several diseases under 
notification, such as Cholera, Dengue, AIDS, and Tu berculosis. The Department of 
Health classifies diseases as notifiable based on their potential as threats to the 
community, for e.g. COVID-19.  
1.11 The Committee was also informed that d esignating cancer as a notifiable 
disease is likely to be beneficial  in view of improved data quality and quantity. 
Making cancer a notifiable disease will result in capturing the incidence and pattern 
of cancer throughout India in a more comprehensive manner. If cancer is made a 
notifiable disease, cancer incidence and pa ttern will be available at a very granular 
level, including each district. However, making cancer a notifiable disease may 
involve logistical and implementation challenges that would need to be carefully 
considered. Several states in India have made cancer  a reportable disease by 
administrative order by the Director of the Public Health Department of the state.  
1.12 The Committee notes with serious concern that only 19% of India’s 
population is currently covered by the population -based National Cancer 
Registry Programme. The Committee was given to understand that, at present, 
cancer registration in India is largely voluntary and that  hospitals and 
healthcare providers a re presently under no legal obligation to report cancer 
cases to public health authorities  because cancer is not a notifiable disease . As a 
result, existing cancer statistics are largely derived from extrapolations based on 
data from hospital -based and pop ulation-based registries. The Committee is of 
the considered opinion that effective prevention and control of cancer is possible 

6 
 
only when the Government has accurate and comprehensive data on the 
incidence and prevalence of cancer in the country. The avai lability of real -time 
cancer statistics would enable timely epidemiological analysis and help identify 
priority areas for focused prevention and control efforts.  In view of the above, 
the Committee recommends that the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare 
should declare cancer a mandatory documentable disease.  
Need for a nation-wide population based cancer registry  
1.13 During the interaction, the Committee observed that currently, th ere is no 
nation-wide population based cancer registry in India . TMC apprised the Committee 
that there are two types of cancer registries in India, namely, population based cancer 
registries and hospital based cancer registries. A population -based cancer registry  
(PBCR) collects cancer cases that occur within a defined geographical area in a given 
period of time. Cancer patients should be residents of that area for at least one year, 
which is the criterion for the PBCR. The PBCR staff visit all the hospitals for data 
collection, where cancer patients from defined geograp hical areas go for diagnosis 
and treatment . For example, the Varanasi district PBCR covers a population of 40 
lakhs and includes cancer cases who are Varanasi district residents for at least one 
year. The data is collected from all hospitals in Varanasi an d neighbouring districts, 
as well as from Mumbai. While collecting the data, the address and geography of 
Varanasi are considered from the source hospital. 
1.14 A Hospital-based cancer registry (HBCR), on the other hand, collects 
information on cancer case s diagnosed in a particular hospital. It includes all cases 
that come to the hospital. Resident and geography criteria are not required for case 
inclusion. For example: TMC Varanasi HBCR, include all cases coming to the TMC 
Varanasi centre in a particular year. There is no nationwide population -based cancer 
registry in India. There are 52 population -based cancer registries in different 
locations of India. It covers 18.56% of the country's population. As per the NCRP -
ICMR report, there are 243 hospital-based cancer registries. 
1.15 The Committee notes with serious concern that India still does not have a 
nationwide, population -based cancer registry. At present, the 52 population -
based cancer registries and approximately 243 hospital -based cancer registries 
together cover only about 19% of the country’s population. In the absence of a 

7 
 
national-level database, it is impossible to accurately assess the true burden of 
cancer in the country.  The Committee finds it particularly worrying that a 
country with an advanc ed medical system like India lacks a unified national 
cancer registry, whereas many nations across the world maintain comprehensive 
nationwide cancer databases. For example, NORDCAN maintains cancer 
statistics for the Nordic countries, namely, Norway, Sweden, Finland, Denmark, 
Iceland, Greenland, and the Faroe Islands, providing a consolidated picture of 
incidence, prevalence, mortality, and survival rates.  In view of the above, t he 
Committee recommends the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare to establish 
a nationwide population -based cancer registry , with districts designated as 
nodal units. District-wide and state-wide registries should feed into a centralized 
national registry at the Union level. Maintaining a unified national database will 
provide an accurate and real -time picture of cancer incidence, prevalence, 
mortality, and survival. This, in turn, will significantly strengthen evidence -
based policymaking and program interventions for the prevention and control 
of cancer. 
TMC’s Hub-and-Spoke Model  
1.16 The „Hub-and-Spoke model‟ of cancer care delivery ispioneered and advocated 
by TMC. Under this system, simpler, more common cancer treatments are made 
available in “spoke” cen tres close to patients‟ homes, minimizing disruption to their 
lives, while more complex or rare cancers are handled at specialized “hub” centres.  
The Committee had, in its Three Hundred and Twenty -fifth report recommended the 
nationwide expansion of the Hub-and-Spoke network.  
 
1.17   The Committee inquired about the curren t reach of the TMC‟s network of 
hubs-and-spokes. In response, it was submitted that the first Hub, Homi Bhabha 
Cancer Hospital & Research Centre (HBCHRC) is in Visakhapatnam, and its Spoke 
facility, currently under construction in Jatni, Odisha will extend  specialized care to 
the eastern region.  In Punjab, the Homi Bhabha Cancer Hospital (HBCH) in Sangrur 
serves as the hub with its spoke facility HBCHRC in New Chandigarh. Serving 
Central and Northern India, the Mahamana Pandit Madan Mohan Malaviya Cancer 
Centre (MPMMCC) in Varanasi operates alongside its Spoke, the HBCH in Varanasi. 
The MPMMCC also serves as the Hub for the Homi Bhabha Cancer Hospital and 

8 
 
Research Centre in Muzaffarpur, Bihar. The  Dr. Bhubaneswar Borooah Cancer 
Institute (BBCI) in Guwahati was brought under TMC's umbrella  to extend quality 
cancer care to the northeastern region of India. 
 
1.18 Noting that the current Hub and Spoke network is concentrated mainly in 
seven states, the Committee inquired whether it would be feasible for TMC to expand 
the Hub-and-Spoke model to all states across the country. The Committee also sought 
clarification on the type of support, financial, infrastructural, or administrative, that 
would be required to scale this model nationwide. In response, TMC submitted the 
following:  
i. Tata Memorial Centre (TMC) has already established a network of 12 
cancer hospitals across 7 states (Mumbai, Varanasi, Visakhapatnam, 
Sangrur, New Chandigarh, Guwahati and Bhubaneswar). Nine hospitals are 
currently functional, an d three are under construction. TMC has proposed 
expanding this model nationally, planning roughly 30 –40 specialized hub 
centres (serving approximately 5 –7 crore people each) with ability to 
provide the full range of cancer care, including that involving a high level of 
complexity, with supporting spoke centres handling management of 
common cancers. Within its own network, the Homi Bhabha Cancer 
Hospital Sangrur acts as the spoke for the Homi Bhabha Cancer Hospital 
and Research Centre, New Chandigarh. All T MC hubs can handle complex 
cases.  
ii. TMC chain of hospitals handle  over 1,30,000 new cancer patients annually, 
making it one of the busiest group of hospitals globally. Despite our 
expansion in different regions of India, the footfall in our hospitals in 
Mumbai and Navi Mumbai remains very high. In short, the demand 
continues to outpace the capacity. Many arrive with advanced -stage cancers, 
reducing survival chances and increasing treatment complexity. 
iii. TMC recognizes that “covering the entire country through TMC‟s networks 
of cancer hospitals wouldn‟t be feasible,” which is why it submitted this 
model to parliamentary committees and urged scale‑up in partnership with 
Central and State Departments of Health. TMC‟s existing 7‑state network 
proves the concept, bu t nationwide rollout will require much broader 
participation. TMC proposes that the expansion of cancer hub -and-spoke 

9 
 
model should build on existing public health institutions.  
iv. Current policy under the National NCD (NPCDCS/NP‑NCD) programme  
already envisions a two‐tier hub‑and‑spoke cancer network. Under this 
scheme, State Cancer Institutes (SCIs) serve as regional hubs, and approved 
Tertiary Care Cancer Centres (TCCCs) act as spokes providing specialist 
care to surrounding district hospital s and medical colleges. District NCD 
clinics and day -care centres become sub -spokes for diagnosis and basic 
treatment. Parliamentary recommendations have urged that all existing SCIs, 
RCCs and major centres (e.g. AIIMS and long -standing cancer hospitals) be 
upgraded or designated as hubs/spokes based on their infrastructure. In 
practice, established AIIMS campuses and other premier state health 
institutions could become hub centres by adding oncology wings (radiology, 
oncology, laboratory, etc). Spokes woul d be medical colleges or district 
hospitals with basic oncology units. In short, the entire public cancer care 
network (AIIMS, RCCs, SCIs, medical colleges, district hospitals) should be 
knitted into the hub‑and‑spoke framework. 
Support Needed for Nationwide Expansion 
i. Expanding hubs and spokes to all 28 states requires coordinated financial, 
infrastructural and administrative support from the Central and State 
governments. The Government‟s existing funding programmes can be 
leveraged. Under NPCDCS, the Cent re provides capital investment and 
recurring (staffing and operations) grants for new SCIs/TCCCs and district 
oncology facilities. States submit proposals to draw on this pool of funds. 
Financial support would cover construction or upgradation of buildings , 
procurement of equipment (radiotherapy units, imaging, laboratories, etc.) 
and salaries for trained cancer staff. Infrastructural support involves 
equipping designated hubs and spokes with necessary technology. In 
practical terms, the Centre and states w ould need to allocate budgets (on the 
order of hundreds of crores per hub) to set up each model hub and spoke. 
TMC‟s earlier submissions to Standing Committees gave detailed budget 
estimates for model hubs and spokes, including capital costs and staffing 
needs. The NHM cancer scheme has approved 19 SCIs and 20 TCCCs so 
far.   

10 
 
ii. In its previous submissions to the Parliamentary Standing Committee, TMC 
has already quantified the investment needed for model hubs and spokes. 
Roughly speaking, a model hub (300 beds , fully equipped for advanced 
cancer care) would require on the order of Rs 650 crore in one‑time capital 
investment, and about Rs 110 –120 crores per year in recurring costs. A 
model spoke (100 beds, handling common cancers) would need around Rs 
400 crore capital and Rs 35–40 crore annual running costs. 
iii. TMC is ready to provide technical guidance and capacity‐building for the 
scale-up. It has long provided mentorship to the broader cancer ecosystem in 
India via the National Cancer Grid (350+ centres) and bil ateral state 
collaborations. TMC has already been collaborating with multiple state 
governments, offering advice on staffing patterns, training of oncologists, 
procurement of equipment, etc. These efforts demonstrate TMC‟s role as a 
technical hub, providin g protocols, training, telemedicine support, and 
quality standards. TMC can thus help train staff at new hubs/spokes, ensure 
adherence to evidence -based care pathways, and share best practices.  This 
approach ensures a sustainable, government‑owned hub‑and‑ spoke network 
across India. 
1.19 The Committee commends TMC for developing a successful Hub -and-
Spoke model  for decentralized cancer care. The Committee is given to 
understand that establishing a model hub requires an initial investment of 
approximately ₹650 crore and recurring annual costs of about ₹120 crore, while 
setting up a model spoke requires an initial investment of around ₹400 crore and 
annual recurring costs of about ₹40 crore. The Committee feels that it would be 
difficult for TMC to establish hubs and spokes across the entire country on its 
own. The Committee also understands that TMC’s existing infrastructure is 
already overburde ned, and the demand for cancer care has significantly 
outpaced its current capacity.  
1.20 The Committee in its 325 th Report titled ‘An expanded role for the 
Department of Atomic Energy in Cancer treatment in India through an 
enlarged network of the Tata M emorial Centre’ had recommended that TMC 
should take all the stakeholders on board related to cancer treatment, and 
leverage some of the existing centres and upgrade them to TMC standards 
rather than building de novo centres. The Committee recommends that each of 

11 
 
the 28 States should designate one government hospital with an existing 
oncology unit (such as an SCI, RCC, AIIMS campus or major medical college) 
as the State Hub, supported by 1 -2 medical colleges or district hospital s as 
Spokes. Strengthening these centres should be funded jointly by the Centre and 
States through existing NPCDCS and related schemes. The Committee further 
recommends that TMC act as the ‘national technical partner’ and collaborate 
with the States and pr ovide requisite guidance, mentorship, wo rkforce training, 
and standardized clinical protocols required to deliver specialized cancer care. 
1.21 The Committee had also previously recommended that a High Level 
Steering Committee headed by the Minister of Sta te for Atomic Energy, the 
Chief Secretaries/Administrators of all the States and Union Territories as 
Members and Director, TMC as Member Secretary be constituted so that all the 
modalities related to the adoption and rolling out of the Hub and Spoke Model  
can be chalked out in a time bound manner. The Committee reiterates its earlier 
recommendation. The Committee recommends the Department of Atomic 
Energy and the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare to provide full support, 
including enhanced budgetary al locations, policy backing, and greater financial 
autonomy to TMC to help it achieve this objective. 
Proton beam therapy  
1.22 The Committee visited the P roton Therapy Centre at ACTREC and noted that 
the proton beam machine housed at the Centre, developed indigenously at a cost of 
₹400 crore, is unique and remains the only machine in India equipped with three 
gantries. The Committee was informed that the machine has the capacity to treat 
approximately 900 patients annually who require precision radiation therapy that 
spares healthy tissue s. To date, around 636 patients, including 95 children, have 
received treatment at the Centre, many of them at affordable or no cost. 
1.23   The Committee sought clarification on the science underpinning proton beam 
therapy and how this modality differs from conventional cancer treatment. In 
response, it was explained that the fundamental objective of any radiation therapy 
technique is to deliver the required radiation dose to the tumour while minimising 
exposure to surrounding healthy tissues. Reducing radiation to normal tissues helps 
avoid early and long-term side effects, a consideration of particular importance in 
paediatric patients, whose normal growth and development can be severely affected 

12 
 
by radiation to structures such as muscle, bone, brain, heart, and eyes. Proton beam 
therapy offers significant potential to improve cancer treatment outcomes by reducing 
treatment-related toxicities. Unlike conventional radiation, which uses photons (X-
rays, gamma rays, or electrons), proton therapy employs charged particles such as 
protons or heavier hadrons, including carbon ions. These particles deposit the bulk of 
their energy at the end of their trajectory, a phenomenon known as the Bragg peak, 
resulting in minimal radiation beyond the target tissue. Although protons share 
similar radiobiological properties with X-rays, their unique physical characteristics 
and sophisticated delivery systems enable highly precise targeting of the tumour 
while sparing adjacent normal tissues, thereby facilitating optimal dose delivery with 
markedly fewer side effects. 
1.24 The Committee was also informed that proton beam therapy is of special 
significance in the treatment of tumours that are located close to vital body structures, 
tumours considered resistant to radiation, and in the treatment of children as they are 
highly susceptible to the long term adverse effects of radiation. 
1.25 The Committee is given to understand that the superior precision of 
proton beam therapy and its significantly lower toxicity profile makes it 
particularly suitable fortumours located near vital body structures, tumours 
considered resistant toradiation and for paediatric patients who are highly 
vulnerable to long-term radiation-induced developmental complications. The 
Committee, therefore,  recommends the Ministry of Health & Family Welfare to 
take proactive steps to expand access to proton beam therapy. Considering that 
TMC has already established a Proton Therapy Centre and has treated 636 
patients, including 95 children, the Committee further recommends that the 
Ministry collaborate with TMC to develop a national plan for setting up 
additional proton therapy centres in strategic regions of the country. Given 
TMC’s proven expertise and operational experience in commissioning and 
managing India’s first proton therapy facility, the Committee further 
recommends that the Ministry formally engage TMC as the principal technical 
partnerfor planning, commissioning, workforce capacity building, and 
establishing clinical protocols to ensure that these centres can effectively meet 
the growing needs of paediatric and other eligible cancer patients.

Department of Atomic Energy. Dr Bhabha established the  Tata Institute of

India’s three stage nuclear programme 
2.9 During the presentation, the Committee was briefed about the three stages of 
India‟s three stage nuclear programme as mentioned below:  
India's three-stage nuclear program, as conceptualized by Dr H J Bhabha, is 
a sequential program where the “Stages” come in -series and cannot be 
pursued in parallel.  
 
Stage 1:  Uses Pressurized Heavy Water Reactors (PHWRs). Natural 
uranium (natural uranium is 0.7 percent U -235, which is fissile, and the rest 
is U-238) is the primary fuel. Heavy water (deuterium oxide, D2O) is used 
as moderator and coolant. 
 
Stage 2:  The spent fuel from Stage -1 is reprocessed  in a reprocessing 
facility, where Plutonium -239 is separated. Pu -239 then becomes the main 
fissile element, in what are known as fast breeder reactors (FBR). These are 
known as breeder reactors because the U -238 “blanket” surrounding the fuel 
core will un dergo nuclear transmutation to produce more PU -239, which in 

25 
 
turn will be used to create energy. The stage also envisages the use of 
Thorium (Th -232) as another blanket. Th -232 also undergoes neutron 
capture reactions, creating another uranium isotope, U -233. It is this isotope 
which will be used in the third stage of the programme. Thorium by itself is 
not a fissile material, and cannot be used directly to produce nuclear energy. 
 
Stage 3:  In addition to the U -233 created from the second stage, breeder 
reactors fuelled by U -233 with Th -232 blankets, will be used to generate 
more U-233.  
 
 
 
As the spent nuclear fuel from one stage provide nuclear fuel for the next stage, the 
successful & self-sustainable progression from 1st stage to 2nd stage and from 2nd 
stage to 3rd stage is possible only when there is generation of a sizeable quantity of 
spent fuel in each of the preceding stage. Hence the possibility to directly advance to 
3rd stage are not scientifically & technologically feasible/ viable for harnessing power 
through utilization of Thorium at a scale. The challenges are not mere logistical and 
infrastructural limitations, but that of technological advancement, maturity, scale and 
sustainability, besides technical know-how and capability.   
2.10 The Committee has sought clarification on the timeline by which India is 
expected to enter the third stage of its nuclear programme.  In response, it was 
submitted that many technological milestones have to be crossed and capacity is to be 
built at scale to progress from 1st stage to 2nd stage and subsequently to 3rd stage.  At 
a lab scale, the demonstration of 2 nd stage Fast Reactor Technology is done through 


26 
 
successful running of Fast Breeder Test Reactor (FBTR) for more than 40 years now, 
which besides building crucial experience of Fast Reactor technology is also 
producing electricity of 10 MWe capacity at a demonstration scale. This reactor is run 
through P lutonium-239 extracted from spent fuel of 1 st stage reactors. Further, 
Thorium has been converted in FBTR to U -233 and has been used in KAMINI test 
reactor. While the lab scale demonstration has happened which has been key to build 
technical know-how, to t ake it to the field at commercial level, what is required is 
technological maturity and scale of deployment, right from 1 st stage. At present, there 
is 8.8 GW of capacity built in 1 st stage, which is now planned to be taken to 100 GW 
by 2047, based on exis ting proven technologies and few GWs of Fast Breeder 
Reactor, for which prototype plant (PFBR) is at advanced stage of commissioning. 
The mix of reactor (predominantly 1 st stage reactors) to be deployed by 2047 is 
supposed to generate enough spent nuclear fuel to support/ feed a sustainable 2 nd 
stage, though Plutonium & depleted -Uranium, obtained by reprocessing of spent 
nuclear fuel of 1 st stage.  Also in parallel, during this period, development of 
technology and infrastructure towards Reprocessing technology and Thorium Reactor 
technology for Power generation need to take off. As they are to pass demonstration 
and/or prototype stage, before being considered for commercial deployment, it would 
be premature to specify a timeline for entering the 3 rd stage of program at commercial 
scale, but most likely to happen in the latter half of the century.

Cancer burden in India  
 The Committee notes with concern the increasing number of cancer cases 
in the country, particularly the significantly higher incidence reported  in the 
North-Eastern region of India . The Committee believes that it is essential to 
examine whether the ethnic groups of the Northeast are  genetically predisposed 
to specific types of cancers. To address this, the Committee recommends TMC 
to collaborate with the CSIR-Institute of Genomics and Integrative Biology 
(IGIB), which has been conducting extensive work in population and cancer 
genomics. Such collaborative research should aim to identify genomic signatures 
and risk factors unique to the North -Eastern population. The Committee feels 
that these insights will be critical for developing tailored cancer therapies, 
targeted prevention methods and early detection strategies for the northeastern 
population.                                                                                                     (Para 1.8) 
Notification of cancer as a documentable disease 
 The Committee notes with serious concern that only 19% of India’s 
population is currently covered by the population -based National Cancer 
Registry Programme. The Committee was given to understand that, at present, 
cancer registration in India is largely voluntary and that  hospitals and 
healthcare providers a re presently under no legal obligation to report cancer 
cases to public health authorities  because cancer is not a notifiable disease . As a 
result, existing cancer statistics are largely derived from extrapolations based on 
data from hospital -based and pop ulation-based registries. The Committee is of 
the considered opinion that effective prevention and control of cancer is possible 
only when the Government has accurate and comprehensive data on the 
incidence and prevalence of cancer in the country. The avai lability of real -time 
cancer statistics would enable timely epidemiological analysis and help identify 
priority areas for focused prevention and control efforts.  In view of the above, 

33 
 
the Committee recommends that the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare 
should declare cancer a mandatory documentable disease.                   (Para 1.12) 
Need for a nation-wide population based cancer registry  
 The Committee notes with serious concern that India still does not have a 
nationwide, population -based cancer re gistry. At present, the 52 population -
based cancer registries and approximately 243 hospital -based cancer registries 
together cover only about 19% of the country’s population. In the absence of a 
national-level database, it is impossible to accurately asse ss the true burden of 
cancer in the country.  The Committee finds it particularly worrying that a 
country with an advanced medical system like India lacks a unified national 
cancer registry, whereas many nations across the world maintain comprehensive 
nationwide cancer databases. For example, NORDCAN maintains cancer 
statistics for the Nordic countries, namely, Norway, Sweden, Finland, Denmark, 
Iceland, Greenland, and the Faroe Islands, providing a consolidated picture of 
incidence, prevalence, mortality, an d survival rates.  In view of the above, t he 
Committee recommends the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare to establish 
a nationwide population -based cancer registry , with districts designated as 
nodal units. District-wide and state-wide registries should feed into a centralized 
national registry at the Union level. Maintaining a unified national database will 
provide an accurate and real -time picture of cancer incidence, prevalence, 
mortality, and survival. This, in turn, will significantly strengthen evid ence-
based policymaking and program interventions for the prevention and control 
of cancer.                                                                                                      (Para 1.15) 
TMC’s Hub-and-Spoke Model  
 The Committee commends TMC for developing a successful Hub -and-
Spoke model  for decentralized cancer care. The Committee is given to 
understand that establishing a model hub requires an initial investment of 
approximately ₹650 crore and recurring annual costs of about ₹120 crore,  while 
setting up a model spoke requires an initial investment of around ₹400 crore and 
annual recurring costs of about ₹40 crore. The Committee feels that it would be 
difficult for TMC to establish hubs and spokes across the entire country on its 
own. The Committee also understands that TMC’s existing infrastructure is 

34 
 
already overburdened, and the demand for cancer care has significantly 
outpaced its current capacity.                                                                    (Para 1.19) 
 The Committee in its 325 th Report titled ‘An expanded role for the 
Department of Atomic Energy in Cancer treatment in India through an 
enlarged network of the Tata Memorial Centre’  had recommended that TMC 
should take all the stakeholders on board related to c ancer treatment, and 
leverage some of the existing centres and upgrade them to TMC standards 
rather than building de novo centres. The Committee recommends that each of 
the 28 States should designate one government hospital with an existing 
oncology unit ( such as an SCI, RCC, AIIMS campus or major medical college) 
as the State Hub, supported by 1 -2 medical colleges or district hospitals as 
Spokes. Strengthening these centres should be funded jointly by the Centre and 
States through existing NPCDCS and relat ed schemes. The Committee further 
recommends that TMC act as the ‘national technical partner’ and collaborate 
with the States and provide requisite guidance, mentorship, wo rkforce training, 
and standardized clinical protocols required to deliver specialized cancer care.    
(Para 1.20)    
 The Committee had also previously recommended that a High Level 
Steering Committee headed by the Minister of State for Atomic Energy, the 
Chief Secretaries/Administrators of all the States and Union Territories as 
Members and Director, TMC as Member Secretary be constituted so that all the 
modalities related to the adoption and rolling out of the Hub and Spoke Model 
can be chalked out in a time bound manner. The Committee reiterates its earlier 
recommendation. The Committee  recommends the Department of Atomic 
Energy and the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare to provide full support, 
including enhanced budgetary allocations, policy backing, and greater financial 
autonomy to TMC to help it achieve this objective.                                (Para 1.21) 
Proton beam therapy  
 The Committee is given to understand that the superior precision of 
proton beam therapy and its significantly lower toxicity profile makes it 
particularly suitable fortumours located near vital body structures, tumours 
considered resistant toradiation and for paediatric patients who are highly 
vulnerable to long-term radiation-induced developmental complications. The 

35 
 
Committee, therefore,  recommends the Ministry of Health & Family Welfare to 
take proactive steps to expand access to proton beam therapy. Considering that 
TMC has already established a Proton Therapy Centre and has treated 636 
patients, including 95 children, the Committee further recommends that the 
Ministry collaborate with TMC to develop a national plan for setting up 
additional proton therapy centres in strategic regions of the country. Given 
TMC’s proven expertise and operational experience in commissioning and 
managing India’s first proton therapy facility, the Committee further 
recommends that the Ministry formally engage TMC as the principal technical 
partnerfor planning, commissioning, workforce capacity building, and 
establishing clinical protocols to ensure that these centres can effectively meet 
the growing needs of paediatric and other eligible cancer patients.      (Para 1.25) 
 The Committee, in its 387th Report, had earlier recommended TMC to 
take steps to expand access to Proton Beam Therapy (PBT) and Boron Neutron 
Capture Therapy (BNCT) which offer precise tumour targeting with fewer side 
effects, reducing long-term treatment costs.As regards Boron Neutron Capture 
Therapy, the Committee was informed that TMC is working with scientists in 
BARC's Reactor and Radiobiology groups and is looking into constructing 
reactor-based BNCT equipment for treating patients with recurrent Head and 
Neck and Brain tumours. The Committee was also informed that TMC is 
currently actively involved in discussions on the construction of such equipment, 
patient treatment rooms, and research laboratory facilities for patient treatment 
and the discovery/ generation of new pharmacological boronated compounds to 
make such treatment feasible and affordable in India. The activity is still in the 
stages of discussions and approval of the concept and design. BNCT therapy is 
already a mainstream treatment in countries like China and Japan; India should 
explore its adoption at the earliest. The Committee, therefore, recommends that 
TMC expedite these discussions and ensure that this therapy becomes available 
in India at the earliest.                                                                                (Para 1.26) 
 The Committee observes that cancer treatment cannot follow a one-size-
fits-all approach and must be tailored to a patient’s unique molecular profile. 
The Committee notes with concern that India’s continued reliance on 
conventional radiotherapy and chemotherapy often results in suboptimal 
treatment, higher fatality rates, and unnecessary expenditure. In light of the 

36 
 
rising incidence of cancer in the country, the Committee stresses the urgent need 
to transition towards precision medicine, where therapies are based on 
individualized molecular characteristics, thereby improving clinical outcomes 
and reducing overall costs.The Committee therefore recommends the 
Department of Atomic Energy and the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare to 
prioritise the development and adoption of personalised oncology treatment 
regimens at scale. Future research efforts should focus on precision medicine, 
particularly gene-network-based precision oncology, to better understand 
tumour biology, analyse how patient-specific gene regulatory networks 
contribute to cancer, and ultimately enable the delivery of tailored therapies and 
drugs rather than uniform treatments for all patients.The Committee further 
emphasises that precision medicine must be made available at a population scale 
and urges the concerned Departments to provide necessary policy support and 
funding to facilitate this transition.The Committee also recommends that the 
cancer institutes under the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare should 
collaborate with TMC and help achieve this transition.                         (Para 1.27) 
CSR funding  
 The Committee appreciates the welfare -oriented initiatives being 
undertaken by TMC, particularly in providing free treatment to paediatric 
cancer patients. The Committee notes that in 2024 –25, TMC spent 
approximately ₹74 crore on free treatment for paediatric patients, of which 
around ₹70 crore was sourced from non -governmental sources, including ₹45 
crore from CSR contributions. Considering the rising incidence of cancer in the 
coming years, the Committee observes that existing funding levels would be 
insufficient to meet the growing demand for free treatment. In view of this, the 
Committee recommends the Department of Public Enterprises  to direct Central 
Public Sector Enterprises (CPSEs) to allocate a portion of their CSR funds to 
TMC. This additional support will help ensure sustained provision of free 
treatment to paediatric and economically disadvantaged cancer patients.

KEVAT 
 The Committee appreciates the Tata Memorial Centre (TMC) for 
conceptualizing and successfully rolling out the KEVAT program, India’s first

formal training initiative for patient navigation. The Committee notes that the 
role of patient navigators, though of ten overlooked, is integral to strengthening 
the entire healthcare continuum, particularly in complex domains such as 
cancer care, where patients and families face multiple barriers including lack of 
awareness, language challenges, financial constraints, e motional distress, and 
difficulty in navigating hospital systems. The Committee observes that patient

addressing systemic and patient -level challenges. In view of the remarkable 
outcomes achieved by KEVAT, both within the TMC network and 
internationally, the Committee recommends that the Ministry of Health and 
Family Welfare, in collaboration with the National Medical Commission should 
encourage universities, medical colleges, and healthcare institutions across the 
country to initiate dedicated courses on patient navigation.                  (Para 1.36) 
Designation of TMC as an Institution of National Importance 
 The Committee notes with concern that one of the most pressing 
challenges in strengthening cancer care delivery in India is the acute short age of 
trained oncology professionals. This deficit adversely affects early diagnosis,

oncology training hub, TMC continues to face significant difficulties in 
recruiting specialised personnel  including oncologists, radiologists, pathologists, 
and allied health professionals, es pecially in its newer centres located outside 
Mumbai. The Committee observes that the demand for a skilled oncology 
workforce is rapidly outpacing supply, and incremental increases in intake of 
students alone will not adequately meet national requirements.  The Committee, 
in its 325th Report, had earlier recommended the Government of India to

consider conferring Institute of National Importance (INI) status on the Tata 
Memorial Centre (TMC)/Homi Bhabha National Institute, in view of its pivotal 
national role in cancer care, training, and research. Keeping in view the rising 
burden of cancer cases in the country and the growing demand for skilled 
oncology workforce, the Committee reiterates its above recommendation to 
designate TMC/HBNI as Institute of National Importance.                   (Para 1.40)
