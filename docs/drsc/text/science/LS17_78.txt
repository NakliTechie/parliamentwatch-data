 The mandate of the Ministry of Environment and Forests entails planning, promotion and coordination of environment
 and forestry programmes. The main activities of the Ministry relate to conservation and control of pollution,
 afforestation and regeneration of forests in degraded areas, protection and conservation of forests, wildlife and
 protection of environment. The tasks are sought to be achieved through environmental impact assessment, eco-
regeneration, assistance to organisations implementing environmental and forestry programmes, enactment of
 environmental legislation, formulation of environmental policies, promotion of environmental and forestry research,
 extension, education and training to augment the requisite manpower, dissemination of environmental awareness
 among all sectors of the country’s population. The activities of the Ministry are divided in four major sectors, i.e.
1. Environment,
2. National River Conservation Directorate,
3. Forests and Wildlife and
4. National Afforestation and Eco-Development Board.

IMPACT OF BIOTIC PRESSURE
26. According to the Forest Survey of India (FSI), 40% of forest area comes under the category of degraded forests. The
 main causes of degradation are excessive grazing and excessive fuel wood collection. Illegal felling for timber and
 encroachments contribute to the problem. According to statistics available, the forest cover in India forms only 1.8% of
 the Global Forest Area. As against this the Forests are required to support 17% of human population and 16-17%
 livestock population. The estimated number of livestock that graze within the forests is estimated at 270 millions. At
 least 30% of the fodder requirement for the livestock is met from the forests. The number of people who are dependent
 on forests for their fuel wood supply is around 226 millions.
27. According to the report entitled "Demand and Supply of fuel wood, timber and fodder in India", published by Forest
 Survey of India, against a demand of 946 Million tons dry and 1136 Millions tons of green fodder, the availability of
 fodder is only 440 Million tons and 292 Million tons respectively. This yawning gap between demand and supply of the
 fodder is instrumental in excessive biotic pressure on forest. Likewise, there is a wide gap between the demand and
 supply of fuel wood in our country. Indian forests today are in a position to supply 17 million tons fuel wood on
 sustainable basis but the actual removal is around 86 million tons.
28. The Committee was apprised of the consequences of grazing on forest Eco-system. Forest grazing has several direct
 effects of which the effect on regeneration of the land is particularly injurious to the small regeneration because of the
 browsing of tender shoots and buds. The indirect damage caused by grazing is mainly on the forest soil itself, the effect
 being in varying proportion depending on the size or weight of the animals, each kind behaving in its own way as it
 moves from one place to another. The foot marks of different species affect differently on the forest land according to
 the light or reverse movement in each case.
29. The Committee was also informed that the removal of excessive fuel wood leads to removal of young saplings and
 poles in the forest area. Excessive lopping affects the crown of the trees and reduces their growth. The resultant over
 exposure of the floor of the forest leads to deterioration of the soil and there is over all decline in the fertility of the
 land. Grazing and excessive removal of saplings and poles also lead to invasion of the forest area by weeds like
 Lantana, Parthenium and Azeratum species. The growth of these weeds further affects the regeneration of forest

30. The Committee recommends that in order to augment the household income in rural areas and reduce their

31. The Committee does not fully share the findings of the Survey of India for nature has its own regulatory
 system. Seen in proper perspective, the fuel wood collection by villagers or tribals living near the forests is an
 activity which has been going on ever since the man set its foot on the earth. This activity by tribals or villagers,
 the Committee feels, only helps in the regeneration of the land thus adding indirectly to the growth of the forests.
 Likewise the dry leaves also contribute to the process for they ultimately convert into a kind of manure that is
 conducive to the growth of forests. The Committee does not agree to the view the animal footing and grazing
 affects the tender plants. On the other hand there is a remote possibility of such tender plants being there in a
 dense forest unless they are planted as a part of the planned forestry. In fact, a forest is nothing but a wild
 growth which has sprung up as a part of normal process of the nature. Again, seen in a proper perspective, the
 grazing by animals is indirectly an activity equal to the filling of land which should help the reproductive
 process.
32. The Committee observes that as a result of persistent recommendation of the Committee or otherwise on
 merits the rights of tribals and villagers etc. living near the forests to collect the fuel wood have at least being
 recognized by no less a person than the Prime Minister of the country. The Ministry should therefore fall in line
 and recognize these rights which only help, as stated above, in the growth of forests as part of a natural activity.
33. Animal grazing is also not an activity which would affect the growth of the forests. Apart from what has been
 said above, the animal excreta ultimately converts itself into a kind of manure which would help the growth of
 forests.
34. The Committee further observes that the Ministry should in fact concentrate on organized felling of trees
 undertaken on a large scale by timber smugglers in connivance with the forest authorities. If checked properly,
 this alone would go a long way in saving the forests.
WILDLIFE (PROTECTION) ACT,1972
35. The Committee was informed that the Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972 in its present form is quite effective,
 particularly, in dealing with persons violating the provision of the Act. According to Section 51 of the Act, punishment
 for violating any of the provisions of the Act may extend to 3 years of imprisonment or with a fine which may extend
 up to Rs.25,000/- or both. In case an offence is committed in relation to any animal specified in Schedule I or Part II of
 Schedule II of the Act or whether the offence relates to hunting in or altering the boundaries of a Sanctuary or a
 National Park, such offence is punishable with imprisonment for a term not less than one year and also with a fine not
 less than Rs.5,000/-. The term of imprisonment can extend up to six years In case of a second or subsequent offence,
 the term of imprisonment can extend to 6 years and shall not be less than 2 years and the amount of fine shall not be
 less than Rs.10,000/-. The term of imprisonment for persons indulging in illegal trade or species included in Schedule I
 or Part n of Schedule II can extend up to 7 years and also with a fine which shall not be less than Rs.5,000/-. In addition
 to the above deterrent measures, there is a provision to forfeit to the State Government any trap, tool vehicle, vessel or
 weapon used in committing the offence.
36. The Committee is of the view that the above mentioned provisions should constantly be kept in view taking
 into account the experiences gained in operation of these provisions. While the Wildlife needs to be protected
 from the onslaughts of the man, a balance has also to be maintained in the penal provisions of the law. The penal
 provisions if they are made more stringent will only encourage corruption. There is a need to discourage the
 tendency of amending the Act on the basis of individual incidents. On the other hand the experience gained over
 a period of time should form the basis of a review of various provisions.

37. The Kaziranga National Park has time and again been the scene of the recurring Floods resulting in high mortality of
 Indian one horned Rhinoceros and other animals in the Park. The Secretary informed the Committee that the total area
 of the Kaziranga National Park is 472.45 sq.kms and it harbours the world's largest population of Indian One Horned
 Rhinoceros. Kaziranga National Park is situated in the flood plains of Brahmaputra River on the southern bank and the
 entire area has been formed by silt deposition carried by different river systems flowing through it. The beds of the
 entire river systems particularly of the Brahmaputra river have been raised by 1950 earthquake. As a result, floods have
 become a recurring feature in Kaziranga
National Park. The area has more than 200 water bodies interspersed with grassy maidans and woody highlands. It is
 this unique topography of the area that has provided the park very rich and varied wildlife. It has also been notified as
 world heritage site by UNESCO. Inundation of the park during the monsoon is an annual feature. However in certain
 years ( on every 9th or 10th year) floods are severe and they cause considerable damage and inflict large scale mortality

38. The Committee was apprised that the floods in Kaziranga National Park has both positive and negative aspects as
 enumerated below:
Positive impact of floods:
1. Facilitates migration of fish to and from the National Park for breeding of fishes.
2. Clearance of water hyacinth and other debris (to some extent).
3. Creation of different site conditions and soil formations.
4. Prevention of getting water bodies polluted by the re-cycling of water.
5. Keeping right mix of trees and grasses to support the bio diversity found in this park.
Negative impact of floods:
1. Mortality of wildlife by drowning/poaching knocked down by vehicles during migration.
2. Siltation of wet lands.
3. Erosion along northern boundary.

BIODIVERSITY CONSERVATION
40. While considering the Demands for Grants of the Ministry of Environment and Forests last year, the Committee
 observed that there was a need for comprehensive legislation on Biodiversity. Consequently, a draft legislation on
 biodiversity has been prepared. The Secretary informed the Committee that the Cabinet in its meeting held on 7.3.2000
 has approved the proposal for enacting legislation on biodiversity .

41. The purpose of this legislation is to protect India's rich biodiversity and associated knowledge against their use by
 foreign individuals and organisations without sharing the benefits arising out of such use. The Act provides for setung
 up of a National Biodiversity Authority (NBA) at Chennai, State Biodiversity Boards (SBBs) and Biodiversity
 Management Committees (BMCs) in local bodies. NBA and SBBs are required to consult BMCs in decisions relating
 to use of biological resources/related knowledge within their jurisdiction.
42. The Committee notes with satisfaction that the Ministry has initiated a right step towards enacting the
 Biodiversity legislation and hopes that the same would be enacted at an early date.

 44. The Committee is happy to note that the Government has taken the steps, as follows, to control air pollution but
 recommends that the Ministry should take urgent steps towards creating mass awareness on environment about
 vehicular pollution through newsletter, pamphlets, newspaper, magazines, television, radio, workshops, seminars and
 exhibitions to make the control of vehicular pollution a big success. The Ministry must utilise the services of NGOs for
 this purposes instead of routing through the channels utilised earlier. However, the performance of the NGOs should be
 critically reviewed on regular intervals.
• Vehicular emission norms have been progressively tightened, starting from the year 1991. The emission standards
 were made stringent in 1996 and further tightened in the year 2000 known as India 2000 emission standards akin to
 EURO-I.
• Bharat stage-II emission standards akin to EURO-II for private non-commercial vehicles requiring supply of 0.05%
 sulphur content in fuel was notified to be effective from 1.4.2000 in the National Capital Region.
• Specifications for petrol and diesel quality commensurate with emission related parameters were notified in 1996 and
 refineries were directed to comply with the specifications.
• Unleaded petrol for new vehicles was introduced in four metro cities from April, 1995 and only unleaded petrol is now
 being supplied in the entire country from 1.2.2000.
• Low sulphur diesel (0.5% sulphur content) was introduced during 1996 which was reduced to 0.25% in 1997 in
 metrocities. Now only 0.25% sulphur diesel is available in the entire country w.e.f. 1.1.2000.
• Ultra low sulphur fuel (0.05%) would be supplied in Delhi from 1.4.2000 for EURO-II vehicles and phased
 programme for supply of the same quality fuel to other metros is being worked out by the Ministry of Petroleum and
 Natural Gas.
• Specifications of2T oil have been notified which is effective from 1.4,1999 in the entire country.
• Ban on sale and supply of loose 2T oil and supply of only pre-mixed 2T oil at retail outlets in NCT of Delhi has been
 made mandatory effective from 31.12.98.
• The benzene content in petrol has been reduced from 5% to 3% from 1.1.2000. Further, it has been decided to supply
 petrol with benzene content of maximum 1% in NCT w.e.f. 1.10.2000.
• The Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas is also procuring low sulphur crude oil so as to have proper blending in the
 refineries before undertaking various operations to produce required quality of fuel.

8. The Committee does not fully share the findings of the Survey of India for nature has its own regulatory
 system. Seen in proper perspective, the fuel wood collection by villagers or tribals living near the forests is an
 activity which has been going on ever since the man set its foot on the earth. This activity by tribals or villagers,
 the Committee feels, only helps in the regeneration of the land thus adding indirectly to the growth of the forests.
 Likewise the dry leaves also contribute to the process for they ultimately convert into a kind of manure that is
 conducive to the growth of forests. The Committee does not agree to the view the animal footing and grazing
 affects the tender plants. On the other hand there is a remote possibility of such tender plants being there in a
 dense forest unless they are planted as a part of the planned forestry. In fact, a forest is nothing but a wild
 growth which has sprung up as a part of normal process of the nature. Again, seen in a proper perspective, the
 grazing by animals is indirectly an activity equal to the filling of land which should help the reproductive
 process.
(Para 31)

 merits the rights of tribals and villagers etc. living near the forests to collect the fuel wood have at least being

 and recognize these rights which only help, as stated above, in the growth of forests as part of a natural activity.
(Para 32)
10. Animal grazing is also not an activity which would affect the growth of the forests. Apart from what has been
 said above, the animal excreta ultimately converts itself into a kind of manure which would help the growth of
 forests.
