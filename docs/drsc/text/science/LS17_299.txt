INTRODUCTORY
The Department of Atomic Energy (DAE) came into being on August 3, 1954, and has been
engaged in the development and deployment of nuclear power technology, application of radiation
technologies in the field of agriculture, health care, industry and basic research. The Atomic Energy
Commission (AEC) is the apex body for formulation of policies and direction of the programmes.
The Commission implements its policies and programmes through the Department of Atomic Energy
(DAE). The Programmes of DAE have been divided into four sectors viz. Research and Development,
Industries, Minerals and Power Sector. The current mandate of the Department, on which its
programmes are based, extends to:-
z Increasing share of nuclear power through deployment of indigenous and other proven
technologies, along with development of fast breeder reactors and thorium reactors with
associated fuel cycle facilities;
z Building and operation of research reactors for production of radioisotopes and carrying
out radiation technology applications in the field of medicine, agriculture and industry;
z Developing advanced technologies such as accelerators, lasers, supercomputers, advanced
materials and instrumentation, and encouraging transfer of technology to industry.
z Support to basic research in nuclear energy and related frontier areas of science;
interaction with universities and academic institutions; Support to research and development
projects having a bearing in DAE’s programmes, and international cooperation in related
advanced areas of research; and
z Contribution to national security.
2. VISION OF THE DAE
The vision of the DAE is to empower India through technology, creation of more wealth and
improving standard of living of its citizens. To realise its vision, the Department has been engaged
in the design, construction and operation of nuclear power/research reactors and the associated
nuclear fuel cycle technologies across the country.
NITI Aayog has also identified following 10 targets to be achieved by the Department.
(i) First stage of Indian Nuclear Power Programme
Creation of 2.5 to 3 GWe (average capacity) per year for next 15 to 20 years.
(ii) Uranium and Rare Metals-Exploration, mining and milling
Target : Tenfold rise in exploration and production of uranium and rare earths to achieve
self-sufficiency.
(iii) Second stage of Indian Nuclear Power Programme
Target : Tenfold rise in power from Fast Breeder Reactors (FBR) and creation of
matching fuel cycle facilities in the back end.

2
(iv) Health Care
Affordable cancer care for 5 lakh new patients per year and decreasing the cancer
mortality rate by 15%
(v) Food Security
Creation of networks and Facilities to provide food security through nuclear agriculture
and food preservation for 10% of the Indian population.
(vi) Water and Waste Management
Management of Municipal waste in 50 cities and providing water purification facilities
for 20000 villages.
(vii) Mega Science Schemes
Completion and utilisation of various accelerator programmes, India-based Neutrino
Observatory (INO), Laser Interferometer Gravitational Observatory (LIGO), European
Centre for Nuclear Research (CERN), International Thermonuclear Experimental Reactor
(ITER) etc.
(viii) Basic Research and Science Education
Pursuing curiosity driven basic research programmes for understanding the fundamental
nature of processes, creation of knowledge base and science education.
(ix) Directed Research
R&D activities directed towards achieving specific objectives of the Department in the
nuclear and allied fields.
(x) Social Outreach and Awareness
Creation of network for need based social outreach activities in and around the Department
facilities and step up awareness programmes for target audience.
3. MAJOR PROGRAMMES
3.1 Over the years, the Department has been able to establish a network of institutions engaged
in R&D as well as industrial activities and acquired expertise in all aspects of the nuclear fuel
cycle.
3.2 The share of nuclear power in the total electricity generation from April, 2016 is about 3.3%.
The Nuclear Power Corporation of India Limited (NPCIL) in the country has kept pace with the
total electricity generation with the share remaining around 3 to 3.5%. A substantial increase in the
share is planned by augmentation of the installed nuclear power capacity.
3.3 At present, NPCIL is spearheading India’s Nuclear Power Programme with 22 Nuclear Power
Reactors in operation, with total installed capacity of 5780 MW at seven locations. Locations
(including RAPS-1 of 100 MWe owned by the Government). The reactor units are: 4 at Tarapur
(Maharashtra), 6 at Rawatbhata (Rajasthan), 2 at Kalpakkam (Tamil Nadu), 2 at Narora (Uttar
Pradesh).

3
3.4 The DAE is also developing advanced technologies such as accelerators, lasers, super computers,
advanced materials and instrumentation and encouraging transfer of technology to the industry,
which could contribute to the national prosperity. A wide range of technologies have been developed
to come to the present stage of harnessing nuclear energy and efforts are continuing to develop
such technologies further with future requirements in mind. Technologies to be developed for
future applications include those necessary for building accelerator driven sub-critical reactor system
(ADS), high temperature reactor systems and fusion technologies. Several applications of radiation
and isotope technologies for improving the quality of life have been developed and efforts on this
front are to be continued.
3.5 The Department is also engaged in the development of radiation technologies and their applications
for better crop varieties, techniques for crop protection, radiation-based post harvest technologies,
techniques for radio diagnosis and radio therapy of diseases particularly cancer, technologies for
safe potable water, better environment and industrial growth.
3.6 The DAE contributes to the enrichment of knowledge domain by pursuing basic research in
nuclear energy and related frontier areas of science; interaction with universities and academic
institutions; support to research and development project having a bearing in DAE’s programmes,
and international co-operation in related advanced areas of research.
3.7 The Committee notes with satisfaction the achievements of the Department of Atomic
Energy in its 62 years of operation. In addition to its considerable achievements in the
nuclear power generation, the Department has also produced useful spin-off applications for
the benefit of the society and industry. Keeping in view the Department’s success, the
Committee is confident that the Department  will  be able to fulfil ten point vision  outlined
by NITI Aayog  for the Department.
4. ORGANISATIONS/INSTITUTIONS/CENTRES/PSUs
4.1 The DAE, over the years, has established a vast network of institutions and evolved into a
broad-based organisation comprising research centres, industrial units, Public Sector Undertakings
and fully aided autonomous institutions along with service organisations. These organisations are
engaged in research, technology development and commercial operations in the areas of nuclear
power and associated technologies, radiation technologies and their applications, basic research and
academic programmes to support, sustain and carry forward its avowed objectives and activities.
So far, it has established 107 installations spread across several States of the country. The organisations/
institutions under the ambit of DAE, category-wise, are as follows:
z Research Centres: Bhabha Atomic Research Centre (BARC)– Mumbai; Indira Gandhi
Centre for Atomic Research (IGCAR)– Kalpakkam; Raja Ramanna Centre for Advanced
Technology (RRCA T)– Indore; Variable Energy Cyclotron Centre (VECC)– Kolkata; Atomic
Minerals Directorate for Exploration and Research (AMD) – Hyderabad; and Global
Centre for Nuclear Energy Partnership (GCNEP), Gurgaon.
z Industrial Organisations: Heavy Water Board (HWB) – Mumbai; Nuclear Fuel Complex
(NFC) – Hyderabad; and Board of Radiation and Isotope Technology (BRIT) –
Mumbai.

z Service Organisations: Directorate of Purchase & Stores (DPS) – Mumbai, Directorate
of Construction, Services and Estate Management (DCSEM) – Mumbai; and General
Services Organisation (GSO) – Kalpakkam.
z Public Sector Undertakings: Nuclear Power Corporation of India Ltd. (NPCIL)– Mumbai;
Bharatiya Nabhikiya Vidyut Nigam Ltd. (BHA VINI); Kalpakkam, Tamil Nadu; Uranium
Corporation of India Ltd. (UCIL)– Jaduguda; Indian Rare Earths Ltd. (IREL)– Mumbai;

z Aided Institutions: Tata Institute of Fundamental Research (TIFR) – Mumbai; Tata

Institute of Physics (IOP) – Bhubaneswar; National Institute of Science Education and

z DAE through Board of Research in Nuclear Sciences (BRNS) and National Board for

research in nuclear and allied fields and mathematics, respectively.
z Academic programmes run by BARC, IGCAR, RRCAT, VECC, SINP, IPR, IOP, NISER,
IMSc, HRI and TMC come under the fold of Homi Bhabha National Institute, a deemed
University. TIFR is also a deemed university. Centre for Excellence in basic Sciences

10. ATOMIC ENERGY REGULATORY BOARD (AERB)
10.1 The Atomic Energy Regulatory Board was constituted on 15
th November, 1983 by exercising
the powers conferred by section 27 of the Atomic Energy Act, 1962 (33 of 1962) to carry out
certain regulatory and safety functions under the Act. The regulatory authority of AERB is derived
from the rules and notifications promulgated under the Atomic Energy Act, 1962 and the Environmental
protection Act, 1986. The mission of the Board is to ensure that the use of ionizing radiation and
Nuclear Energy in India does not cause undue risk to health of people and the environment.
Monitoring of Radio Active Substances and Radiation Generating Units Outside Plants:-
10.2 The Committee desired to know the manner in which Atomic Energy Regulatory Board was
monitoring the use of radioactive substances and radiation generating Plants outside nuclear installations
to avert accidents like Mayapuri, Delhi. In response thereto, it was informed that the existing
regulations, viz, Atomic Energy (Radiation Protection) Rules, 2004 and Atomic Energy (Safe Disposal
of Radioactive Wastes) Rules, 1987, specifies the rules to be followed for safe handling, use and
disposal of radioactive material as well as their regulation. Some of the requirements of these Rules
were violated in the case, which resulted in the disused radioactive source of a high hazard
category finding its way in the public domain causing the untoward incident.
10.3 During the meeting of the Committee held on 13
th October, 2016 review of functioning of the
Department, a query was raised regarding regulation of diagnostic X-ray facilities by a Member of
the Committee. In response thereto, the following information was furnished by the Department:-
AERB exercises graded regulation based on the radiological hazard potential associated with
the type of facility as per Atomic Energy (Radiation Protection) Rules, 2004 [AE(RP)R, 2004]. In
this graded approach, the Computed Tomography (CT) and Interventional Radiology (Cath Lab)

22
applications of X-rays in Medical Diagnostic Radiology having relatively higher hazard potential are
ranked above the general diagnostic X-ray equipment.
10.4 W ith regard to the specific question on current status of X-ray machines in India which are
registered with AERB and plans for covering all the unregistered ones, the followed information
was provided.
(a) The regulatory control over medical X-ray equipment is governed by AERB safety code:
AERB/RF-MED/SC-3 (Rev. 2) issued under the provisions of AE(RP)R, 2004.
(b) AERB has implemented an online system e-LORA (e-Licensing of Radiation Applications)
for convenient filing of applications and issuance of regulatory consents for various
types of radiation facilities and equipment. With this system, there is a marked improvement
since 2013 in registration of all radiation applications including X-ray equipment. As of
now 46,173 number of x-ray equipment have been declared by the utilities in the e-
LORA system of AERB.
10.5 The registration of the declared equipment is based on availability of safety personnel, radiation
protection accessories, satisfactory Quality Assurance (QA) test report of X-ray equipment and
fulfilment of all other regulatory requirements as per the AERB Safety Code. The QA tests are
conducted by independent agencies which are accredited by AERB. On compliance with these
requirements, the X-ray equipment are registered through e-LORA promptly. The Department has
informed that 31,926 X-ray equipment have been registered with AERB.
10.6 In addition to the above, there exists a scope for unauthorized import of umpteen number of
X-ray tubes / X-ray equipment in the country as per the existing EXIM policies of the Directorate
General of Foreign Trade (DGFT), Ministry of Commerce & Industry. AERB has informed that the
current policy does not require a “procurement permission” of AERB for such imports. These
imports also included refurbished old machines from international markets.
10.7 To curb such activities, AERB had communication with DGFT, for Amendment in the DGFT
Import Policy for radiation generating components, i.e. incorporation of requirement of AERB
“Procurement Permission” as a condition precedent for free trade import of items containing X-
ray Tube(s)/X-ray Tube Insert(s)/X-ray Equipment itself so that only authorized suppliers/users can
import above items in the country. In response of AERB proposal, DGFT has sent the draft
Notification which has been reviewed and transmitted to DGFT through DAE, for incorporation in
the EXIM Policy. Incorporation of this will go a long way in restricting the unauthorized installation
of x-ray machines through import and supply of X-ray tubes/X-ray equipment.
10.8 The Committee was also informed that process of X-ray units registration has started around
2008. In 2013, around 5000 X-ray machines were brought under the regulatory purview of AERB
and by October, 2016 this went upto 46, 173 out of which 31,926 machines registered through
e-LORA.
10.9 The Committee was also informed that the ‘Atomic Energy (Safe Disposal of Radioactive
Wastes) Rules-1987 clearly spell out the requirements, roles and responsibilities of various parties.
In addition to the above-mentioned Rules, AERB has developed and issued Safety Codes, Standards
and other regulatory documents, specifying the regulatory and safety requirements to be followed

13. BHABHA ATOMIC RESEARCH CENTRE (BARC)
13.1 BARC is the core Research & Development Organisation of the Department of Atomic Energy.
It is now in 57 th years of its establishment. BARC is a premier multidisciplinary nuclear research
Centre of India with excellent infrastructure for advanced research & development and expertise
covering the entire spectrum of nuclear Science and engineering and related areas. Apart from its
headquarters at Trombay spread over 3000 acres, various programmes and facilities of BARC are
in operation at Tarapur, Mysore, Kalpakkam, Gauribidanur, Vadodara, New Delhi, Mount Abu,
Gulmarg and Hyderabad. Several Environmental Survey Laboratories and Units are also in operation
in different parts of the country. A new campus is being developed at Visakhapatnam.
13.2 The BARC supports a comprehensive programme in basic research and human resource
development in the areas of nuclear sciences and technologies. The Centre is engaged in interaction
with academic institutions and international cooperation and related advanced areas of research are
being strengthened.
13.3 Its organisational structure comprises 20 Groups, 94 Divisions, 4,200 Scientist and Engineers
and 15,200 total staff strength. The multidisciplinary and interdisciplinary nature of R&D at BARC
calls for a blend of Science, Engineering and Technology related to all aspects of nuclear Science.
13.4 It’s mandate includes almost the entire spectrum of fuel-cycle activities. Its wide ranging
activities include development of new technologies fabrication of nuclear fuels, reprocessing of
spent fuel, waste management, robotics, super computers, lasers, accelerators, human genomes,
nano-technology and super conductivity, etc.
13.5 BARC also pursues R&D for application of radiation technologies (non-power application) in
the areas of industrial applications, food preservation, radiation mutation to develop varieties of

15. THE GURUSHIKHAR OBSERV ATORY FOR ASTROPHYSICAL SCIENCES (GOALS)
15.1 During its study visit to Mount Abu on 1st July, 2016, the Committee interacted with the
representatives of Department of Atomic Energy, and Scientists of GOALS Observatory under the
control of Bhabha Atomic Research Centre on its functioning. The Committee visited facilities of
The Gurushikhar Observatory for Astrophysical Sciences (GOALS) dedicated to GAMMA Ray

15.2 The Committee was informed by the Scientist In-charge that the Observatory was operated
by the Astrophysical Sciences Division, Bhabha Atomic Research Centre. The Astrophysical Sciences
Division operated three outstation facilities. At Mount Abu, Laboratories are centered on high energy
gamma ray astronomy, theoretical astrophysics and instrumentation development, while a low energy
Neutron monitor is operated at the High Altitude Research Laboratory, Gulmarg. Locally relevant
radiation application studies and instrumentation development activities are being pursued in the
Srinagar Laboratory.
15.3 The GOALS is situated at Oriya village, Mt. Abu, Rajasthan at an altitude of 1300 m above
sea level. The work for the observatory started in early nineties and it became fully operational
from 1997. At GOALS, Asia’s first imaging atmospheric Cherenkov telescope TACTIC was installed
and became operational in March 1997. The main purpose of this telescope is to study the universe
in the very high energy gamma-ray regime. This study revealed the intricate working of nature at
energies much more than the highest achieved energy by human endeavour (for example the Large
Hadron Collider). This helps to advance human knowledge on the creation of universe. The other
most important aspect of this indigenously built telescope is that the Department developed the
know-how of developing ultra-fast electronics in the country which has commercial potential in
many areas other than astronomy and astrophysics.
15.4 Immediately after the installation of the TACTIC telescope in April 1997, GOALS detected a
flaring event from an active galaxy known as Mkn 501 and the result was supported by other
telescopes around the world. Since then, they have studied different types of astrophysical sources
such as supernova remnants, active galaxies with TACTIC telescope and it resulted research papers
in reputed international journals.
15.5 The telescope was further upgraded during 2011 and it was still being used for regular
observations of the astrophysical sources. Recently GOALS reported 400 hrs. of observations of
Crab Nebula which is considered as the standard candle in very high energy astronomy. GOALS
observatory also has a 14 inch optical telescope to monitor the sky simultaneously with TACTIC
telescope. It was also informed that GOALS observatory had facility to monitor the local atmosphere
to record the dust content in the air, rain fall, temperature variation etc.
15.6 The Chairperson while complimenting the scientists at the GOALS Observatory for its
achievements, despite being located in an adverse climatic location, desired to know the recent
accomplishments made by GOALS and its roadmap for future. The Committee also desired to

24. BHARATIYA NABHIKIYA VIDYUT NIGAM LTD. (BHA VINI)
24.1 As a part of 2
nd stage of Indian power program, 500 MWe Prototype Fast Breeder Reactor
(PFBR) is being built for the demonstration of commercial scale power production. These reactors
produce more fuel than they consume and are essential for expansion and sustenance of Indian
nuclear power programme. PFBR will be fore-runner to a series of fast reactors being planned.
24.2 The Bharatiya Nabhikiya Vidyut Nigam Limited (BHA VINI), a Government of India Enterprise,
is a Public Limited Company set up with the objective of constructing and commissioning the first
500 MWe Fast Breeder Reactor (FBR) at Kalpakkam and establish subsequent Fast feeder Reactors
in the country.
24.3 The objective of BHA VINI is to plan, execute, and operate an integrated programme of Fast
Breeder Technology based Nuclear Power Stations for generating electricity on a commercial basis,

44
commencing with PFBR, symbolising commencement of the second stage of the Nuclear Power
Programme.
24.4 Performance of BHA VINI
Informing the Committee about physical progress the Department has informed that all construction
activities and total equipment erection and installation have been completed in Prototype Breeder
Reactor (PFBR). A number of mandatory pre-requisites tests viz. Integrated Leak Rate Test of
Reactor Containment building, commissioning of handling flasks for demonstrating the maintainability
of Primary Sodium Pumps, Pressure hold test of Reactor Assembly and Main Vessel, completion
of Pre-Service Inspection of 10% tubes of all the Steam Generators and segmental preheating of
sodium lines have been completed.
24.5 Presently, work is in progress for commencement of integrated pre-heating, towards which
the number of jobs pertaining to reductions of leaks in Reactor Containment Building and Reactor
assembly, trial testing of fuel handling systems and reactor protection system devices, augmentation
of supports for sodium piping, erection & installation of necessary piping for commencement of
nitrogen purging etc. are in progress.
24.6 The Department has informed that commissioning works were in full steam and all efforts
were being made to ensure that there were no further slippages owing to any errors in job-
scheduling so as to make the PFBR fully functional by October 2017.
24.7 The Committee finds that BHA VINI was allocated ` 20.00 crore for BE 2016-17 but by
January, 2017, it could not utilise the entire sum allocated to BHA VINI Project. However, this year
Budgetary support has been increased from ` 20.00 crore at BE 2016-17 to ` 50.00 crore at BE
2017-18. In response to the Committee’s query about substantial reduction in IEBR amount projected
for BE 2017-18 for BHA VINI, it was informed that the original plan was for 2x500 MWe Project.
However, it has been decided to go for 2x600 MWe. It was further decided to start this project
after completion of the first Project (PFBR). Since PFBR was getting delayed, consequently the
second project could not be started as planned.
24.8 In response to the Committee’s query about PFBR it was informed that PFBR is under
advanced stage of commissioning and the project was expected to be completed by October, 2017.
Although all construction activities, total equipment erection & installation have been completed in
PFBR, time scheduled for the First Approach to Criticality (FAC) followed by subsequent power
operation got shifted. This is primarily owing to augmentation of certain additional assessments and
checks on the installed equipment prior to commencement of their commissioning, which have
essentially emanated owing to both increased regulatory requirements and as a matter of abundant
caution.
24.9 In addition, as commissioning is under progress, occasional mismatches and design anomalies
in the system/ equipment between the prior assessments based on theoretical models and actual
observation are being noticed and appropriate rectification actions are undertaken. These anomalies
are essentially owing to First-Of-A-Kind (FOAK) design and engineering having no international
reporting/benchmarking.
24.10 During the meeting held on 13
th February, 2017, the Committee desired to know reasons
behind delay in commissioning of BHA VINI. The Secretary replied as under:

45
“Yes, we wanted to finish it last year. But as it is a new technology, from both the sides,
from the regulator’ s side and from the project side, we are going in a more conservative way.
We are trying to do tests and retests and then prove everything before we go ahead. So, what
I am expecting is that by October, 2017, we should be able to do it smoothly, and, Sir, I will
tell you today’ s status. Today, we have completed construction in all respects. All systems are
in operation and we are in the final phase of commissioning of this one. And this is a system
where instead of water cooling, we use sodium cooling and that works at about 600 degree
Celsius. That is a much higher temperature compared to what we see in other reactors or even
thermal power stations. This higher temperature causes some problem and there are some
concerns about that. Because of that, we are going in a very systematic way. Again, the
Atomic Energy Regulatory Board is going through all the steps. So, that also is making this
thing slower. The policy adopted is that we will be slow and steady but sure. That is the way
it is going on. And, you are right, Sir. Whatever uranium we get from the mines, only 0.7
per cent of it can be used because that is what is uranium-235. Whereas in this case, entire
uranium quantity can be used, which is hundred times or more, 140 times the total. That
means, if you get one kilogram of uranium from your mine, you can use 140 times compared
to what is available in other thermal reactors. Yes, it is a very important step. I am sure,
we will make it. We don’t have any technical problem. It is only a matter of time.”
The Secretary, Department of Atomic Energy further added
“Sir, uncertainty is not that much because we have a smaller reactor which is of 10 mw
capacity and it has been in operation for the last 25 years. The technology is same. As a
matter of fact, fuel is a little simpler here. But that is a much bigger reactor.  That is the
main difference. I cannot say that because of this reason it got delayed. It is a mix of
reasons. We are cautious at every step. That is the main reason for delay. If some problem
comes in rushing through it, then it will be questioned all over the world. It is not that just
India is responsible for this. I am sure something positive will happen. Please bear with us.
We are doing our best to make it work.”
Another senior scientist of Department of Atomic Energy added as under:
“Sir, I would just like to add to what the Secretary said just now. This technology was
designed at the Indira Gandhi Centre for Atomic Research at Kalpakkam. It is a new
technology. As was rightly pointed out, it runs with sodium. Such programmes are either
stopped or delayed all over the world like France and Japan because of sodium leakage. We
are working with regulators to make sure that we get it right at the first step. Therefore, some
amount of caution has been built into it. We are a bit more cautious than other countries so
that we don’t fail once we get the reactor.”
