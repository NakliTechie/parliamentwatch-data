CHAPTER – 1 
INTRODUCTION 
1.1 The National Capital Region  (NCR) of Delhi stands as a pivotal centre of 
national importance, driving significant economic, political, and cultural activity. 
However, the region's rapid and often unplanned expansion has placed immense 
strain on its natural resources, culminating in a critical environmental challenge: 
the severe degradation of its water quality. The assurance of a clean and reliable 
water supply is a cornerstone of urban viability, and the current situation demands 
a systematic and urgent response. The crisis manifests not as a singular issue but as 
a multi-faceted problem encompassing the profound contamination of the Yamuna 
River, the alarming pollution of groundwater aquifers, and the systematic 
deterioration of lakes and other water bodies. 
1.2 The Yamuna River, a major hydrological system, serves as the most visible 
indicator of the crisis, with the segment flowing through Delhi accounting for a 
disproportionately high percentage of the river's total pollution load. This 
transformation of a major river into a heavi ly polluted channel is symptomatic of 
broader systemic failures. The challenge extends beyond surface water, as 
groundwater sources are being increasingly compromised by the infiltration of 
toxic contaminants. This comprehensive degradation presents a clea r and present 
danger, with direct consequences for the well -being of the region's inhabitants and 
its economic prospects.  
1.3 The drivers of water pollution in the NCR are interconnected and stem from 
intense anthropogenic pressure. A primary contributor is the inadequate 
management of wastewater, where a significant gap exists between the volume of 
sewage generated and the capacity for its treatment, leading to the direct discharge 
of untreated domestic effluent into natural drains and rivers. This is fur ther 
compounded by industrial pollution, wherein clusters across the region discharge 
inadequately treated effluents containing heavy metals and hazardous chemicals, 
often in violation of statutory standards. Concurrently, agricultural runoff from the 
surrounding areas introduces fertilizers and pesticides, leading to nutrient loading 
and chemical contamination in water bodies. A further critical source of pollution 
is the leaching of toxic leachate from unsecured landfill sites into the groundwater, 

 
2 
 
polluting aquifers with a cocktail of hazardous substances. This confluence of 
pollution sources operates within a complex and often fragmented governance 
landscape, involving numerous central, state, and local agencies, which can lead to 
challenges in coordinat ion, accountability, and the consistent enforcement of 
regulations. 
1.4 The ramifications of this widespread pollution are profound and far -
reaching. Public health bears a significant burden, with the continued prevalence of 
waterborne diseases imposing su bstantial costs on the healthcare system and 
impacting societal productivity. Ecologically, the damage is severe, with aquatic 
ecosystems experiencing degradation and biodiversity loss, as evidenced by the 
anaerobic conditions prevailing in stretches of th e Yamuna. The economic costs 
are multifaceted, encompassing not only healthcare expenditures but also the costs 
of water purification, impacts on agriculture and fisheries, and potential 
reputational damage affecting investment and tourism.  
1.5 Considering the ramifications of this widespread water pollution on our 
environment, ecology and public health, the Committee took up the subject "Water 
Pollution in Delhi-NCR and steps taken by various agencies for its  mitigation" for 
detailed examination.  The Committee, accordingly, held meeting on the subject on 
4thDecember, 2024 and 28thApril, 2025 and heard the views of the representatives 
of Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change and Central Pollution 
Control Board.  The Committee again met on 1stJuly, 2025 to hear the views of the 
representatives of Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change and 
Ministry of Jal Shakti on the subject. On 15thJuly, 2025, the Committee first met to 
hear the views of the representatives of (i) Centre for Science and Environment and 
(ii) Confederation of Indian Industry  and thereafter, heard the representatives of

the subject. The Committee visited Okhla Sewage Treatment Plant on 1 3thAugust, 
2025 and heard the views of the Chief Secretary, Government of NCT of Delhi on 
the subject. In all its deliberations, the Committee considered diverse viewpoints 
on the subject and acknowledged the measures that have been implemented, as 
well as those in progress, by the concerned Ministries, Departments, and agencies.  
The Committee also referred the 27 th Report of the Department -related Standing

A. Prevention Control and Abatement of Water Pollution in Country 
 
1. Legal / Administrative Framework 
 
Central government enacted the Water (Prevention and Control of 
Pollution) Act, 1974 with the objective to promote cleanliness of streams, 
wells and the maintaining or restoring of wholesomeness of water. The 
Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) and State Pollution Control 
Boards (SPCBs) / Pollution Control Committees (PCCs) are constituted 
under Section 3 and 4 of the said Act, 1974. CPCB performs function such 
as to advise the Central Government, Plan program for prevention, control 
& abatement of pollution, collect and disseminate environment data, 
Advisory function, Investigation & Research, Lay down standards, etc.  
 
SPCBs / PCCs under said act are performing its functions under Section 17 
and have respective power for grant of consent for discharge of trade 

 
4 
 
effluent, entry & inspection of industry, and to take appropriate action 
against the industry in case of non-compliances of norms etc.  
 
The Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 is  enacted to take measures to 
protect and improve the quality of environment including water. Discharge 
/ Emission Standards of environmental pollutants are being notified under 
the Environment (Protection) Rules, 1986. Rules have also been notified 
under Environment (Protection) Act, 1986, for the management of various 
wastes such as: 
 
1. Solid Water Management Rules, 2016 
2. Bio-medical Waste Management Rules, 2016 
3. Hazardous & other Wastes (Management & Transboundary Movement) 
Rules, 2016 
4. E-Waste (Management) Rules, 2022 
5. Plastic Waste Management Rules, 2022 
6. Battery Waste Management Rules, 2022 
7. Construction and Demolition Waste Management Rules, 2016

2.15 The Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change further apprised 
the Committee that the various so urces of groundwater pollution can be 
categorized as either anthropogenic or geogenic. Anthropogenic, or human-
induced, sources include contaminants such as nitrate from agricultural runoff, 
fertilizers, pesticides, industrial discharges, and leachates from landfills. In 
contrast, geogenic pollution arises from natural geological formations and 
commonly includes elements like Arsenic (As), Iron (Fe), and Fluoride (F). 
2.16 To track the quality of this vital resource, monitoring is conducted by two 
key agencies: the Central Ground Water Board (CGWB) at over 15,000 locations 
and the Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) at 1,233 locations across 32 
States and Union Territories. The groundwater quality data from the CPCB's 
monitored locations for the year 2023 was assessed against the permissible limits 
of the BIS Drinking Wa ter Standards. The analysis revealed specific exceedances, 
with no sites exceeding the limit for Nitrate. However, Total Dissolved Solids 
(TDS) were above the permissible limit at 106 sites across 12 States/UTs, Fluoride 
was high at 41 sites in 7 States/UTs, and Arsenic was found to exceed the standard 
at a single site in one State/UT. 

 
13 
 
2.17 In a parallel effort to control pollution at its source, the CPCB has 
implemented a robust, technology -driven monitoring system for industries. To 
strengthen oversight a nd promote self -regulation, the board has directed all 17 
categories of high pollution-potential industries, grossly polluting industries within 
the Ganga basin, and common waste treatment facilities to install Online 
Continuous Effluent/Emission Monitorin g Systems (OCEMS). This system 
ensures constant vigil on pollution levels, with real -time data on trade effluent and 
emissions transmitted online to the CPCB and the relevant State Pollution Control 
Boards 24/7. Centralized software processes this data, an d if any pollutant 
parameter exceeds the prescribed norms or other irregularities are detected, an 
automatic SMS alert is generated. This immediate notification allows industries to 
take prompt corrective action and enables regulatory bodies to initiate ap propriate 
measures, thereby ensuring more effective compliance. 
2.18 The Committee observes that there is a severe degradation in water 
quality of River Yamuna between the Palla and Wazirabad locations, and 
then again after the meeting the Shahdara Drain. This indicates that major 
pollution loads are entering within these stretches. To precisely identify the 
specific drains and discharge points responsible, a denser network of 
monitoring stations should be established between Palla and Wazirabad, and 
immediately upstream and downstream of every major drain outfall. 
Furthermore, the monthly monitoring frequency is insufficient to capture the 
dynamic nature of pollution, especially from non -point sources and during 
non-compliance events. A shift to weekly or e ven continuous automated 
monitoring at these critical points is essential to provide a more accurate, real-
time picture of pollutant loads and enable swift corrective action. 
2.19 The Committee also observes that  while BOD, DO, pH, and Fecal 
Coliform are c ritical, they do not provide a complete picture of the river's 
health or the sources of contamination. The monitoring program should be 
enhanced to include parameters such as  Chemical Oxygen Demand (COD)  to 

 
14 
 
distinguish between biologically degradable and i ndustrial waste,  heavy 
metals (like lead, mercury, and chromium) to track industrial 
pollution, ammoniacal nitrogen  which is a key indicator of raw sewage, 
and phosphate and nitrate to assess nutrient loading that causes 
eutrophication. For groundwater, the consistent exceedance of Total Hardness 
and Total Dissolved Solids in Delhi warrants a mandatory and regular 
analysis of these parameters at all locations, moving beyond the current focus 
on nitrate and fluoride. This expanded parameter list will allow a uthorities to 
fingerprint pollution sources more accurately and tailor remediation 
strategies accordingly. 
2.20 The Committee also observes that c urrently data is collected under 
different program mes (NWMP, DPCC monitoring) and assessed against 
different standards (Bathing Water Criteria, BIS Drinking Standards). There 
should be a concerted effort to harmonize the presentation of data, ensuring 
that all river water quality is consistently assessed ag ainst a single uniform 
standard. All this data — from the CPCB, DPCC, and other S tate Pollution 
Control B oards—should be integrated into a single, open -data portal with 
interactive maps and time-series analysis tools. This would allow for real -time 
tracking of compliance, easy identification of pollution trends, and better 
public accountability.  
2.21 The Committee also recommends that the practice of reporting only 
"min-max" ranges for annual data should be supplemented with the 
calculation and reporting of annual average values for each parameter at each 
location, as a single high BOD max value can be an outlier, while a high 
average confirms persistent pollution.

CHAPTER – 3 
 
SEWAGE MANAGEMENT IN DELHI NCR 
3.1 The River Yamuna, a sacred lifeline of northern India, flows approximately 
1,370 km from its glacial origin at Yamunotri in the Himalayas to its confluence 
with the Ganga at Prayagraj. Tragically, despite this long course, a mere 2% of its 
length—the 22-kilometer stretch passing through the Nat ional Capital Territory of 
Delhi—contributes to over 70% of the river's total pollution load. This segment, 
from Palla in the north to the Okhla Barrage in the south, has been officially 
categorized as a "highly polluted stretch" by the Central Pollution C ontrol Board 
(CPCB).

3.23 The Committee also heard the views of the Centre for Science and 
Environment ( CSE) on this issue during its meeting held on 15 thJuly, 2025. In 
course of the presentation given by CSE, the following points emerged:- 
3.24 Despite massive financial investment and nearly complete sewage treatment 
infrastructure in Delhi, the Yamuna River re mains ecologically dead because the 
cleanup strategy is fundamentally flawed. The core failure stems from a series of 
critical gaps: the official data underestimates the actual volume of wastewater 
generated; a large unsewered population's waste, collected  by tankers, is dumped 

 
30 
 
directly into drains, bypassing treatment plants entirely; and the technical design is 
self-defeating, as treated effluent is released into the same drains carrying 
untreated sewage, causing re -contamination. Furthermore, only 10 -14% of treated 
water is reused, wasting a potential resource, while stringent treatment standards 
are applied uniformly instead of being tailored to the water's end -use. A paradigm 
shift is essential, moving from simply building infrastructure to ensuring 100 % 
sewage interception, mandating 100% reuse of treated water, rationalizing 
treatment standards, and formally regulating waste collection to create a circular 
water economy that can truly revive the river. 
3.25 The Committee enquired with CSE the reasons b ehind the under-utilization 
of Delhi's Sewage Treatment Plants. CSE informed the Committee that a ccording 
to the data submitted by Delhi Pollution Control Committee (DPCC) to the 
Ministry of Jal Shakti, there does not appear to be a significant gap in the capacity 
utilization of the treatment plants in the city of Delhi. As per the data, the total 
treatment plant capacity shows a gap of roughly 18%, which is much lower than in 
other cities where sewage interception does not take place and sewage plants 
remain underutilized. The key issue in Delhi is to ensure that the water quality 
standards used for treatment are designed based on the reuse objectives of the 
treated water. This approach would also ensure that funds are not spent on 
upgrading plants to meet water quality standards intended for direct discharge into 
the Yamuna River, which currently has no assimilative capacity. Another major 
problem in Delhi is the gross under -estimation of the actual quantity of 
wastewater/sewage that is not intercepted offi cially and therefore flows directly 
into drains and ultimately into the river, adding to the pollution load.  
3.26 The Committee also enquired with CSE that whether the decentralized 
wastewater treatment plants can replace Delhi‘s mega -STPs. CSE informed the 
Committee tha t the issue of treatment should not be viewed simply as a choice 
between decentralized plants versus centralized plants. The fact is that we need to 
ensure 100% treatment of sewage, and it is also clear that if we can do this as close 
as p ossible to the point of sewage discharge, it will reduce both pumping 
requirements and costs. At the same time, it is important to locate treatment plants 
with the reuse potential of the treated water in mind.  If this is done, there can be a 
mix of both la rge-centralized plants and smaller fecal sludge treatment plants 

 
31 
 
(FSTP) or co-treatment plants that can intercept waste from highly congested areas 
as locally as possible, so that both interception and reutilization is possible. It is 
important to focus on  intercepting and treating 100% of the sewage and designing 
plants for reuse or end -use discharge, rather than framing the problem as whether 
to build large or small plants. Both approaches can work depending on the location 
and design. 
3.27 During the cou rse of  its interactions,  the Committee also learnt that 
according to an assessment done by Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) in 
2023, only 3% of installed sewage treatment plants (STPs) met the requisite 
effluent standards. A more recent progress repo rt from the Delhi Pollution Control 
Committee (DPCC) from February 2024 indicates some improvement, stating that 
14 STPs are now compliant, yet this still leaves 23 STPs non -compliant. The root

3.28 The Committee understands that this stringent standard is presumably based 
on the Yamuna‘s lack of assimilative capacity, with the underlying assumption that 
all treated wastewater would be discharged directly into the river. However, this 
approach presents several conceptual and practical flaws: 
1. Disincentive for Reuse:  The assumption of direct river discharge is 
contrary to the objective of incentivizing wastewater reuse. If reuse is a 
priority, then discharging highly treated water into drains is a misallocation of 
resources. 
2. One-Size-Fits-All Standards:  The current standards do not a ccount for 
the final point of discharge. For instance, if treated effluent is intended for 
agriculture, standards for certain parameters, like nitrogen, could justifiably be 
different, as it can act as a soil nutrient. Differentiated standards based on end-
use could significantly reduce treatment costs and actively promote reuse. 
3. Negated Environmental Benefit:  In practice, most treated effluent is not 
discharged directly into the Yamuna but into heavily polluted drains. The 
substantial investment in upgradi ng STPs to achieve the 10 mg/l standard is  
effectively negated when this high-quality effluent mixes with untreated waste 
in these drains, long before it reaches the river. 
3.29 Consequently, the policy focus has shifted entirely to the costly 
refurbishment of existing STPs to meet the new norms. This demands significant 
additional investment, the efficacy of which remains uncertain given the drain 
discharge issue. Furthermore, this singular focus on refurbishment diverts attention 
and resources from the mo re fundamental task of intercepting a greater volume of 
sewage from both sewered and non-sewered areas. 
3.30 The Committee notes that the current action plan, which targets compliance 
for all STPs by March 2025, fails to address these systemic contradictio ns. It 
prescribes a uniform, end-of-pipe solution without considering the actual discharge 
pathways or creating a framework that genuinely incentivizes reuse, thereby 
risking a substantial public investment for a potentially diminished return in terms 
of river water quality.

CHAPTER – 5 
 
ENVIRONMENTAL FLOW OF YAMUNA RIVER 
 
5.1 Rivers are the arteries of our plan et. They are not just channels of water but 
complex, dynamic ecosystems that support an immense variety of life and provide 
indispensable services to humanity —from drinking water and food production to 
cultural sustenance and climate regulation. The natura l pattern of a river's flow, 
from dry -season lows to monsoon highs, is the master variable that dictates its 
health, shaping its physical habitat and determining which species can thrive. 
5.2 Globally, this natural rhythm is being shattered. To fuel our gr owing 
populations, economies, and agriculture, we have heavily engineered rivers with 
dams, barrages, and diversion canals. These structures fragment rivers and hoard 
water, often leaving the downstream stretches —the river's very heart—starved and 
ecologically crippled. 
5.3 In response, the science of  Environmental Flows (e -flows) has emerged. It 
moves beyond the old idea of a "minimum flow" to a more holistic concep t. It‘s 
about ensuring that a river receives the right  quantity of water, at the right  time, 
and of sufficient  quality to remain healthy and continue providing its benefits to 
the people.  
5.4 There are many definitions of environmental flows. The International Union 
for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) (2003) defines ―E -flows as the water regime 
provided within a river, wetland or coastal zone to maintain ecosystems and their 
benefits where  there are competing water uses and where flows are regulated‖.  
Proclaimed at the 10 thInternational River Symposium and International 
Environmental Flows Conference, held in Brisbane in September 2007 , the 
Brisbane Declaration defined Environmental flows as the quantity, timing, and 
quality of water flows required to sustain freshwater and estuarine ecosystems and 
the human livelihoods and well-being that depend on these ecosystems. 
5.5 India framed its National Water Policy in 2012 which also recognized the 
importance of determining a river's ecological needs through scientific study. 

 
45 
 
5.6 The Committee referred the 27th Report of the Department -related Standing 
Committee on Water Resources (2023-24) which was presented to/laid on Table of 
both Houses of the Parliament on 6 thFebruary, 2024 , to understand the issues 
related to environmental flow of River Yamuna. The Report highlightsthat:- 
A Memorandum of Understanding (MoU) was signed by the basin States, namely 
Uttar Pradesh, Haryana, Rajasthan, Himachal Pradesh, and the National Capital Territory 
of Delhi regarding the allocation of the surface flow of the River Yamuna u pto Okhla on 
12.5.1994. The MoU promised a minimum flow of 10 cubic meters per second( cumecs) 
to the river.  However, this well -intentioned commitment was conditional on the 
construction of upstream storage projects, which were never built, leaving the prom ise 
unfulfilled for decades. While the National Green Tribunal (NGT) intervened in 2015 to 
mandate this release from Hathinikund Barrage, its order merely enforced a two -decade-
old standard that was already scientifically outdated. 
Despite the NGT order, t he situation downstream of Wazirabad in Delhi is 
catastrophic for most of the year. The Ministry of Jal Shakti in its  reply to a query raised 
by the Department-related Standing Committee on Water Resources highlightedfollowing 
critical failures: 
The 10 cumecs released from Hathinikund is a token amount in a dry, hot region. A 
significant portion is lost to  evaporation and seepage  into the riverbed during its long 
journey to Delhi. By the time it reaches Wazirabad, it is a fraction of what was released.  
At Wazirabad, whatever little water remains is almost entirely  diverted for Delhi's 
drinking supply. The Ministry admitted that the demand here is so high that the river is 
completely skimmed. The result is that for  nine months of the year, the river downst ream 
of Wazirabad is effectively dead—a dry or nearly dry channel. 
A river has a natural capacity to clean itself, but it needs water to do so. Even if the Delhi 
Jal Board were to treat every drop of the city's sewage to a very high standard (bringing 
Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD) down to 10 mg/l), it would be insufficient. Without 
a substantial flow of fresh water to dilute the treated effluent, the river cannot achieve the 
healthy water quality standards (BOD < 3 mg/l, Dissolved Oxygen (DO) > 5 mg/l) 
required to support aquatic life. The treated sewage, however clean, becomes a pollutant 
in the absence of a flowing river. 
The National Institute of Hydrology (NIH) conducted a scientific study and 
concluded that the 10 cumec s standard is ecologically inade quate. They recommended 
increasing the e-flow to 23 cumecs during the lean season. This higher volume is needed 
to: 
 Ensure a continuous, meaningful flow reaches Delhi. 

 
46 
 
 Provide enough water for effective dilution of treated sewage. 
 Maintain the river's ecological functions. 
However, this is where the process has stalled. The  Yamuna basin S tates 
(primarily Haryana, Uttar Pradesh, and Delhi) did not  agree on this higher 
allocation. Water is a State subject, and each S tate faces its own immense pressures for 
agriculture and urban water supply. Releasing more water for the "environment" is seen 
as taking water away from farmers and cities. Consequently, the scientifically 
recommended standard has been politically rejected, leaving the river with an inadequate, 
token flow. 
5.7 Thus, t he fundamental problem that emerges is that the Demand vastly 
exceeds supply.  The water available in the Yamuna at Hathinikund is entirely 
allocated for human use —primarily irrigation in Haryana and Uttar Pradesh and 
drinking water for  Delhi. The "environment" is the lowest priority in this 
competition. The e-flow release is the first to be reduced or rendered meaningless 
when shortages occur. 
5.8 In essence, the Yamuna in Delhi is not a river by design but a  casualty of 
water resource management. It has been reduced to a seasonal canal for 
transporting water to users and a drain for carrying away treated wastewater. Its 
revival is entirely contingent on a political consensus to share a scarce resource 
more equitably, not just among peop le, but also with the river itself. This requires 
moving from outdated, political compromises to scientifically -grounded, 
sustainable water allocation. 
5.9 The Committee would like to reiterate the recommendation made by the 
Department-related Standing Com mittee on Water Resources in its 27 th 
Report that the Department of Water Resources, River Development and 
Ganga Rejuvenation  should make concerted efforts to evolve consensus 
among the Yamuna basin States to maintain E -flow of 23 cumecs in the lean 
season as recommended by the NIH.  
 
5.10 During its meeting with the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate 
Change held on 1st July, 2025, the Ministry informed the Committee that minimum 

 
47 
 
Environmental Flow for the dilution of the polluted water in river Yamuna in Delhi 
is required to meet the desired water quality levels in river Yamuna for bathing 
purpose i.e. BOD < 3 mg/l and DO >5 mg/l. T o assess the minimum required 
environmental flow of river Yam una for stretch between Hathinik und to Okhla, a

5.11 The Committee observes that this is a critical situ ation characterized by 
inadequate environmental flows, inter -State disagreements, and severe 
ecological degradation. The Committee also observes that this situation 
demands a multi -pronged strategy where the primary objective must be to 
transition from ad -hoc, politically contentious water releases to a 
scientifically-grounded, collaboratively managed, and legally robust regime 
for environmental flows. The Committee, therefore, recommends reconvening 
the Yamuna River Basin Board with a renewed mandate to ach ieve a 
consensus on the scientifically recommended environmental flow of 23 cumecs 
for the lean season, as established by the National Institute of Hydrology. This 
process should be facilitated by a neutral technical expert group and must 
incorporate a transparent water accounting system to build trust among basin 
states by clearly documenting water withdrawals, uses, and losses. The 
dialogue must shift from a perception of loss to a shared vision of securing the 
river’s health as a fundamental prerequisite  for long-term water security for 
all stakeholders. 

 
48 
 
5.12 The Committee feels that the cleaning of any river is a two-step process. 
Firstly, we need to ensure that all discharges into the river are treated 
properly and secondly, the river has sufficient water all round the year to 
maintain its environmental flow. While a lot of emphasis is laid on the first 
aspect that is treatment of discharge into the river,  maintaining the 
environmental flow of the river  is often ignored.  The Committee, therefore, 
recommends that a comprehensive reassessment of the entire river's flow 
requirements should be initiated, moving beyond the single metric of lean -
season flow. This assessment  should establish a dynamic environmental flow 
regime that specifies not only the minimum quantity but also the timing, 
duration, and quality of water required to mimic the river's natural 
hydrograph, including necessary pulses for fish migration and floodp lain 
rejuvenation. This scientific blueprint could serve as the basis for future water 
allocation decisions.  
5.13 The Committee opines that a  paradigm shift in urban water 
management is essential for Delhi. The Committee, accordingly, recommends

CHAPTER – 6 
 
REJUVENATION OF YAMUNA FLOODPLAINS 
 
6.1 Floodplains are vital river ecosystems comprising wetlands, forests, and 
grasslands that are essential for a river's health. They perform critical functions like 
recharging groundwater and acting as natural sponges to mitigate floods. These 
highly productive areas provide invaluable services like water filtration and 
recreational space. However, human activity and climate change have severely 
degraded them, reducin g their capacity to handle floods and replenish aquifers. 
Protecting these floodplains is therefore fundamental to preserving the life of the 
river itself. 
6.2 The Committee examined the27 thReport of the Department -related Standing 
Committee on Water Resou rces (2023 -24) to get a deeper understanding of  the 
issues related to floodplain of River Yamuna. The gist of the Report is as under:- 
The floodplains are established not merely as adjacent land but as an integral 
component of the riverine ecosystem, functioning as a natural sponge that captures 
monsoon excess to mitigate floods and recharge aquifers, while also serving as rich 
biodiversity hubs that support specific flora and fauna, facilitate nutrient deposition, and 
maintain the river's hydrological  balance. However, this critical foundation is being 
fundamentally undermined by a cascade of issues, beginni ng with widespread 
encroachment. Haryana reported  a total floodplain area of 24,406 acres across Karnal, 
Panipat, and Jagadhri, claiming no encroac hments or wetlands, while Delhi's situation is 
more quantifiably critical, with a total floodplain zone of 9,700 hectares, of which only 
1,675.10 hectares are currently available for restoration work, and 161.95 hectares are 
explicitly acknowledged as encroached, despite the Delhi Development Authority (DDA) 
having successfully retrieved 477.79 hectares. 
Beyond physical occupation, the floodplains are being bled dry through 
unsustainable groundwater extraction. The Central Ground Water Board (CGWB) has 
identified four specific sectors within the Yamuna floodplain with a potential sustainable 
yield of 85 Million Gallons per Day (386 MLD). The Delhi Jal Board (DJB) has already 
capitalized on this by installing 130 production wells that yield 196 MLD, with plan s to 
draw an additional 190 MLD, which causes the river to frequently run dry during lean 
seasons by depleting the base flow. Simultaneously, the riverbed itself is being physically 
eroded and altered through rampant illegal sand mining.  

 
51 
 
Adding to the physical degradation is the large-scale dumping of construction and 
demolition debris and even biomedical waste directly into the riverbed. The final layer of 
degradation is chemical, embedded in the very sediment of the river. A joint 2019 study 
by the Delhi  Irrigation & Flood Control Department and CSIR -NEERI analyzed sludge 
from eight locations, revealing that the stretch from Kudesia Ghat to Okhla Barrage is 
heavily polluted with heavy metals —including Chromium, Copper, Lead, Nickel, and 
Zinc—particularly during the pre-monsoon period. Alarmingly, at locations like Old Iron 
Bridge, Geeta Colony, and upstream of DND Bridge, pre -monsoon Chromium levels 
were found to be as high as 1,082, 1,001, and 1,809 mg/kg respectively. While the study 
noted that monsoon s couring reduces these concentrations post -monsoon, it crucially did 
not rule out the presence of legacy sludge trapped deeper than one foot and recommended 
deep digging up to 5-6 feet for a conclusive assessment.  
In essence, the Yamuna floodplains are a b esieged ecosystem, suffering from 
simultaneous physical occupation, resource extraction, illegal mining, waste dumping, 
and deep-seated chemical pollution.

CHAPTER – 8 
CONCLUDING OBSERVATIONS AND AN URGENT CALL FOR ACTION 
8.1 The Yamuna River in the National Capital Region is in a state of 
profound ecological collapse, representing one of the nation's most severe 
environmental and governance failures. Decades of plans, j udicial directives, 
and substantial financial investment have yielded negligible results, with the 
river biologically dead through its critical 22 -kilometer stretch in Delhi. This 
crisis stems not from a lack of effort, but from a flawed strategy —a systemic 
reliance on outdated, linear models of waste management and a critical 
disregard for the river's fundamental need for water. The Yamuna's plight is 
a symptom of a broader malaise where the river is viewed as a sewer rather 
than a living ecosystem.  However, this is not an intractable problem. H istory 
and global experience provide a clear path forward. The successful 
rejuvenation of rivers like the Thames in London, which was declared 
biologically dead in the 1950s but now supports thriving salmon populatio ns, 
and the Rhine in Europe, which recovered from severe industrial pollution, 
demonstrates that even the most degraded waterways can be restored.  
Domestically, the remarkable transformation of the Kham River in 
Aurangabad stands as a powerful testament to  what determined civic action 
can achieve. Through citizen -led efforts that focused on desilting, waste 
management, and the construction of check dams, significant improvements 
in water flow and quality were realized, demonstrating that community 
participation is not just beneficial but essential to sustainable river 
management. 
8.2 The path to rejuvenation  of River Yamuna  demands an urgent and 
fundamental paradigm shift. We must move beyond the obsolete model of 

 
60 
 
simply building treatment plants and discharging the effluent back into 
polluted drains. The new agenda must be the creation of a circular water 
economy, where every drop of wastewater is captured, treated according to its 
future use, and reused as a valua ble resource. This requires mandatory reuse 
for agriculture and industry, the formalization of the desludging tanker 
network, and rationalized treatment standards. Simultaneously, industrial 
pollution must be plugged through stringent enforcement, a cluste r based 
approach to zero liquid discharge, and a crackdown on unauthorized units. 
However, even the most advanced treatment will fail if the river is starved of 
water. 
8.3 The single most critical intervention is securing a scientifically 
determined environmental flow. The current token release is ecologically 
meaningless. The basin  States must reach a consensus on the scientifically 
recommended minimum flow. This must be coupled with aggressive demand 
side management in Delhi, including reducing the city's massive water losses 
and augmenting supply through reused wastewater. The impending revision 
of the Yamuna Water Sharing Agreement presents a historic opportunity to 
formally allocate a rightful share of water to the river itself, an essential 
stakeholder ignored for too long. 
8.4 Ultimately, the revival of the Yamuna is a test of our national 
commitment to sustainable development. It requires a unified command, 
breaking down the silos between numerous agencies. The rejuvenation of the 
floodplains, t hrough a ban on groundwater extraction, removal of toxic 
sludge, and large scale ecological restoration, is the fin al but vital piece of this 
puzzle.  

 
61 
 
8.5 The time for incrementalism is over. The Committee urges all 
stakeholders to embrace this comprehensi ve blueprint with the urgency and 
resolve that this national crisis demands, to transform the River Yamuna 
from a symbol of environmental failure into a testament of ecological renewal.

(Para 2.18)

The Committee observes that there is lack of reliable data on actual

(Para 3.31) 
The Committee further observes that a significant portion of Delhi's

collected by desludging tankers which, due to a lack of regulation and disposal

facilities, routinely dump raw sewage into open drains. Consequently, even as

desludging tanker ecosystem. All deslu dging tankers should be registered,

sludge only at designated Sewage Treatment Plants or co -treatment facilities. 
This will ensure that the massive volume of waste from unsewered population, 
which currently bypasses the system, is captured and treated, effectively 
closing a major pollution gateway. 
(Para 3.32) 
The Committee observes that the significant investment in sewage 
treatment capacity by the Central and State Governments is  fundamentally

dialogue must shift from a perception of loss to a shared vision of securing the 
river’s health as a fundamental prerequisite for long -term water security for 
all stakeholders. 
(Para 5.11) 
 

 
70 
 
The Committee feels that the cleaning of any river is a two -step process. 
Firstly, we need to ensure that all discharges into the river are tre ated 
properly and secondly, the river has sufficient water all round the year to 
maintain its environmental flow. While a lot of emphasis is laid on the first 
aspect that is treatment of discharge into the river,  maintaining the 
environmental flow of the r iver is often ignored.  The Committee, therefore, 
recommends that a comprehensive reassessment of the entire river's flow 
requirements should be initiated, moving beyond the single metric of lean -
season flow. This assessment  should establish a dynamic envir onmental flow 
regime that specifies not only the minimum quantity but also the timing, 
duration, and quality of water required to mimic the river's natural 
hydrograph, including necessary pulses for fish migration and floodplain 
rejuvenation. This scientific blueprint could serve as the basis for future water 
allocation decisions.  
(Para 5.12) 
The Committee opines that a paradigm shift in urban water 
management is essential for Delhi. The Committee, accordingly, recommends 
that the Delhi Jal Board must augment its water supply through an aggressive 
strategy for the reuse of treated wastewater. A significant percentage of 
treated effluent, meeting stringent standards, should be used for non -potable 
purposes such as industrial cooling, horticulture, and groundwater recharge, 
thereby reducing the city's absolute dependence on freshwater withdrawals 
from the Yamuna and freeing up this water for the river's ecological 
functions. 
(Para 5.13) 

 
71 
 
The Committee also recommends that the State Government of 
Haryana and the State Government of NCT of Delhi should setup  a dedicated 
River Rejuvenation Fund , financed by a small levy on water -intensive 
industries discharging in the Yamuna River basin. This fund could be utilized 
for initiatives such as financing modernizat ion of irrigation canals to reduce 
water loss, implementing advanced sewage treatment technologies, thereby 
creating a sustainable financial model for the Yamuna's recovery.  
(Para 5.14) 
The Committee observes that the Yamuna River water sharing 
agreement of 1994 is due for revision in 2025 and talks have initiated amongst 
the States which are party to this agreement.  In light of the rapidly evolving 
socio-economic, demographic, and environmental factors, the Committee 
finds 30 -year duration for such a crit ical agreement to be fundamentally 
misaligned with contemporary realities.  The Committee recommends that 
future water sharing agreement should be made for a maximum period of 10 
years only and this period should be explicitly tied to the decadal census cycle, 
with revisions incorporating updated census data as a primary determinant 
for water allocation, thus ensuring that the agreement remains responsive to 
contemporary demographic and resource realities. 
(Para 5.15) 
 
CHAPTER – 6 
 
REJUVENATION OF YAMUNA FLOODPLAINS 
The Committee recommends that the proactive measures taken to 
combat encroachment and illegal dumping must be strengthened and 

 
72 
 
implemented consistently. The successful land recovery and waste clearance 
efforts in Delhi should be replicated across all S tates along the river. 
Furthermore, the patrolling and security infrastructure must be expanded 
beyond Delhi, with all States ensuring strict and  timely penalties for illegal 
dumping and construction in the Yamuna floodplain. 
(Para 6.7) 
The Committee also recommends that the unsustainable extraction of 
groundwater in the Yamuna floodplain must be immediately addressed to 
prevent the river from running dry. The planned drawing of an additional 190 
MLD from the floodplain must be revisited in light of the already exceeded 
sustainable yield. The Committee recommends that a complete ban should be 
enforced on groundwater extraction within the entire Yamuna floodplain.  
(Para 6.8) 
The Committee also recommends that a zero -tolerance policy must be  
implemented against illegal sand mining in the Yamuna floodplain . This 
requires coordinated raids and surveillance by revenue, police, and 
environment Departments to halt the illegal sand mining that is eroding the 
riverbed.  
(Para 6.9) 
The Committee furt her recommends that a comprehens ive sludge 
assessment must be commissioned to determine the full extent of heavy metal 
contamination in the Yamuna floodplain . Based on the findings, a targeted 
sludge removal plan should be implemented, starting from the mo st critically 
polluted stretches. 

 
73 
 
(Para 6.10) 
The Committee also recommends that the afforestation and ecological 
revival of the floodplains must be significantly scaled up. The successful 
planting of native trees and grasses should be intensified, aiming to cover the 
entire restored area with dense, native vegetation. The development of 
biodiversity parks and wetlands, which have proven successful in  bringing 
back bird s and retain water, should be a primary focus of all restoration 
projects, transforming t he floodplains into thriving ecological hubs rather 
than just open spaces. 
(Para 6.11) 
 
CHAPTER – 7 
WATERBORNE DISEASES AND PUBLIC HEALTH RESPONSE IN 
DELHI-NCR 
The Committee acknowledges the comprehensive strategies deployed by 
the Ministry of  Health and Family Welfare . To further strengthen these 
efforts and enhance their impact, the Committee recommends that t he 
Ministry should create a shared public dashboard for real -time correlation of 
water quality and disease data. Moreover, the surveill ance data from the 
IDSP-IHIP portal must be leveraged for predictive analytics through 
geospatial hotspot mapping and district -level risk scoring to enable proactive, 
micro-targeted interventions. To fortify the health system's last-mile response, 
every lo cal health centre should be equipped with a dedicated unit for 
diarrhoea treatment, and staff should practice outbreak response through 
regular periodic drills. 

 
74 
 
(Para 7.6) 
 
The Committee observes that GST on water purifiers is currently levied 
at the rate of 18% . The Committee is of the view that the people of the 
country should not be penalized for trying to arrange safe drinking water for 
themselves.  T he Committee , therefore, recommends that the Government 
should either abolish or lower GST on water purifiers and their consumable 
parts. 
(Para 7.7) 
The Committee opines that the people living in low -income areas such 
as unauthorized colonies and slums are more susceptible to waterborne 
disease. Th e Committee therefore recommends that the Ministry should 
intensify and expand its public health campaigns within these high -risk 
communities. The initiative must evolve beyond a singular focus on "Stop 
Diarrhoea" to adopt a comprehensive, integrated approach that addresses the 
full spectrum of waterborne illnesses, such as cholera, typhoid, and hepatitis. 
(Para 7.8) 
CHAPTER – 8 
CONCLUDING OBSERVATIONS AND AN URGENT CALL FOR ACTION 
The Yamuna River in the National Capital Region is in a state of 
profound ecological collapse, representing one of the nation's most severe 
environmental and governance failures. Decades of plans, judicial directives, 
and substantial financial investment have yielded negligible results, with the 
river biologically dead through its critical 22 -kilometer stretch in Delhi. This 

 
75 
 
crisis stems not from a lack of effort, but from a flawed strategy —a systemic 
reliance on outdated, linear models of waste management and a critical 
disregard for the river's fundam ental need for water. The Yamuna's plight is 
a symptom of a broader malaise where the river is viewed as a sewer rather 
than a living ecosystem.  However, this is not an intractable problem. H istory 
and global experience provide a clear path forward. The su ccessful 
rejuvenation of rivers like the Thames in London, which was declared 
biologically dead in the 1950s but now supports thriving salmon populations, 
and the Rhine in Europe, which recovered from severe industrial pollution, 
demonstrates that even the  most degraded waterways can be restored.  
Domestically, the remarkable transformation of the Kham River in 
Aurangabad stands as a powerful testament to what determined civic action 
can achieve. Through citizen -led efforts that focused on desilting, waste 
management, and the construction of check dams, significant improvements 
in water flow and quality were realized, demonstrating that community 
participation is not just beneficial but essential to sustainable river 
management. 
(Para 8.1) 
The path to rejuven ation of River Yamuna  demands an urgent and 
fundamental paradigm shift. We must move beyond the obsolete model of 
simply building treatment plants and discharging the effluent back into 
polluted drains. The new agenda must be the creation of a circular wat er 
economy, where every drop of wastewater is captured, treated according to its 
future use, and reused as a valuable resource. This requires mandatory reuse 
for agriculture and industry, the formalization of the desludging tanker 
network, and rationalized  treatment standards. Simultaneously, industrial 

 
76 
 
pollution must be plugged through stringent enforcement, a cluster based 
approach to zero liquid discharge, and a crackdown on unauthorized units. 
However, even the most advanced treatment will fail if the r iver is starved of 
water. 
(Para 8.2) 
The single most critical intervention is securing a scientifically 
determined environmental flow. The current token release is ecologically 
meaningless. The basin  States must reach a consensus on the scientifically 
recommended minimum flow. This must be coupled with aggressive demand 
side management in Delhi, including reducing the city's massive water losses 
and augmenting supply through reused wastewater. The impending revision 
of the Yamuna Water Sharing Agreement  presents a historic opportunity to 
formally allocate a rightful share of water to the river itself, an essential 
stakeholder ignored for too long. 
(Para 8.3) 
Ultimately, the revival of the Yamuna is a test of our national 
commitment to sustainable development. It requires a unified command, 
breaking down the silos between numerous agencies. The rejuvenation of the 
floodplains, through a ban on groundwater extraction, removal of toxic 
sludge, and large scale ecological restoration, is the fin al but vital piece of this 
puzzle.  
(Para 8.4) 
The time for incrementalism is over. The Committee urges all 
stakeholders to embrace this comprehensive blueprint with the urgency and 

 
77 
 
resolve that this national crisis demands, to transform the River Yamuna 
from a symbol of environmental failure into a testament of ecological renewal. 
(Para 8.5)
