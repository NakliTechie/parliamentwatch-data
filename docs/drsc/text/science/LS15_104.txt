R E P O R T
            The Ministry of Environment & Forests is concerned with the planning, promotion and co-ordination of environment and
 forestry programmes in India. The important activities of the Ministry incorporate conservation and control of pollution, afforestation
 and regeneration of forests in degraded areas, protection and conservation of forests and wildlife and protection of environment.
 These tasks are sought to be achieved by the Ministry through environmental impact assessment, eco-regeneration, assistance to
 organizations implementing environmental and forestry programmes, enactment of environmental legislation, formulation of
 environmental policies, promotion of environmental and forestry research, extension, education and training to augment the requisite
 manpower, dissemination of environmental information international cooperation and creation of environmental awareness among all
 sectors of the country’s population. The major activities of the Ministry are spread over four major sectors, i.e.
(a)
    Environment;
(b)   National River Conservation Directorate;
(c)    Forests and Wildlife and
(d)   National Afforestation and Eco-Development Board.

MAJOR SCHEMES OF THE MINISTRY
WILD LIFE PROTECTION
8.         The Ministry in reply to a Question, apprised the Committee that “Poaching and illegal trade in wild animals”, has become a
 lucrative trade because of increasing demand for various wildlife products across our borders. The pecuniary gain from the illegal
 trade was very high. The prices fetched by some of the wildlife products in the clandestine international markets were as follows:
(i)         Tiger bone        - US $2425/Kg.
(ii)        Rhino horn        - US $1150/ Kg.
(iii)       Musk               - US $50,000/Kg.
(iv)       Ivory                - US $ 400/Kg.
8.1       There were reasons to believe that routes and conduits of illegal wildlife trade were largely the same as those for smuggling of
 narcotics, arms, gold and other commodities. The masterminds running these operations operate either from Metro cities or areas
 quite far from the forest areas.
8.2       During its visit to Andaman & Nicobar Islands the Committee was apprised of poaching and smuggling of sea cucumber and
 other marine products by Vessels from Myanmar, Indonesia, Thailand and other neighbouring countries. There were reports of large
 scale felling of trees in Diglipur and Mayabunder Islands by old revenue settlers and encroachers as well. One of the reasons
 attributed to this was existence of ineffective enforcement infrastructure. Therefore, the Committee recommends that the Ministry
 should create necessary enforcement infrastructure both within protected areas and outside protected areas to prevent poaching.
8.3       The Committee was apprised of various measures taken by the Central Government to control poaching and illegal trade in
 wild animals and its derivatives as follows:
(i)
      Central Bureau of Investigation have been empowered under the Wild Life (Protection) Act, 1972 to apprehend and
 prosecute wildlife offenders. Anti- poaching efforts were being coordinated with INTERPOL.
(ii)    Ministry of Home Affairs has written to all the State Governments to lend a helping hand to the field formations to curb

9.         The Committee believes that the “Wild Life (protection) Act, 1972”, is a comprehensive Act. The provisions regarding
 punishment for violation of the Act are quite stringent. However, these do not appear to have been enforced effectively because of
 the inadequacy of field formations both in number and in terms of efficiency in detection of wild life crime and successful
 prosecution. Intelligence gathering infrastructure is more or less absent. The problem appears to have been further compounded by
 the ban on filling of vacant posts and the tardy flow of funds to the field level.
9.1       The Committee recommends that the Ministry should take up immediate steps in co-ordination with various State
 Governments at the highest level to fill up all the vacant posts at the field level and provide the enforcement staff with adequate
 mobility, communications network and sophisticated arms.
9.2       The Committee notes with concern that a number of policy recommendations are yet to be considered by the Union
 Government.  For example, the recommendation of the Subramaniam Committee for the Prevention of Illegal Trade in Wildlife has
 been lying with the government since 1994.  The Ministry should examine the Report in right earnest and devise a policy for
 conservation –– both for scientific or participatory management.
Bt. COTTON  & ISSUES RELATED THERETO
10.       In India, Cotton was grown on approximately 9 million hectare of land, highest in the world, accounts for about 20% of the
 world acreage.  India stands 3
rd in the lint production, producing only 319 kg/ha lint yield compared to the world average of 603
 kg/ha.  Cotton is highly susceptible to insects and consumes over 50% of the total insecticides used in the country.  India spends
 nearly Rs.1,200 crores worth of pesticides for controlling various pests of cotton.  Approximately, 60-70% of the pesticides are used
 only for control of the dreaded pest namely bollworm against the world average of 30%.  It is estimated that in India about 20-30% of
 the productivity of cotton is lost due to the damage of insect pests.
10.1     In genetically engineered foods, genes from bacteria, viruses, other plants or animals are inserted into crops such as corn,
 canola, soyabeans, potatoes, tomatoes, squash, and papayas to achieve favourable characteristics including higher yield, faster
 ripening, pest control and herbicide tolerance.   Similarly,  Transgenic Bt. Cotton is a  technology that has been proposed as
an alternative to overcome the problem of bollworm, reduction in pesticide use and improving the environment.
10.2     A gene from the soil borne bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis   has been introduced into the cotton plant.  This gene encodes a
 protein, Cry1A (c)  that has specific insecticidal properties to control bollworms.  This gene was transformed into cotton plants by
 M/s. Monsanto, USA and M/s. Maharashtra Hybrid Seeds Co. Ltd. (Mahyco), Mumbai (in which Monsanto has a 26 per cent
 stake)  has introduced it into Indian cotton germplasm by traditional breeding methods.  This transgenic cotton on which cotton pests
 namely bollworm feeds, interferes with the digestive tract of insect and kills it.  The new plant has no effect on other insects or pests.
10.3     Studies like gene flow, aggressiveness and cross-ability with wild near relatives of cotton, conducted with transgenic cotton
 revealed that the possibility of gene flow in nature becomes negligible and therefore there is a least chance of effecting the
 biodiversity and less chance of possibilities of cross pollination in nature.  The toxicity and allerginicity studies conducted on birds,
 fish, mammals including ruminants (goats, cows and buffaloes) on Bt. Cotton established that it is as safe as non-Bt. cotton has no
 impact on non-target insects and on bio-flora.  The Bt. cotton hybrids were further characterized for the absence of the Terminator
 gene and confirmed that these hybrids are not having any terminator gene sequences.  Composition analysis of Bt. Cotton showed
 that it is comparable with non-Bt. cotton. Contained limited field studies and large sale field studies in farmers’ fields and also in
 ICAR system indicated that Bt. cotton showed distinct agronomic advantage over non-Bt. cotton.
10.4     The economic benefit of Bt. cotton is estimated to be substantial when compared to current practices of cotton cultivation. 
 Significantly higher benefits are expected to be derived from the yield savings of the Bt. cotton in addition to savings in the cost of
 pesticides.  The farmers are expected to gain 50% to 70% of the economic benefits generated from the technology. Small farmers are
 more likely to adopt this technology as they do not have to spray every 3 to 4 days and 20 to 25 times.
10.5     According to various News reports over 4,000 hectares in Gujarat, and 190 hectares in just one district of Andhra Pradesh,
 Karimnagar, are under Bt cotton production.  There are reports that the Karimnagar plantation is intended for seed production, which
 will be used for plantation in some 80,000 hectares next year in the Kharif season.
10.6     In India today, clearance of a GMO goes through two Committees: Review Committee on Genetic Manipulation (RCGM) of
 the DBT, and the Genetic Engineering Approval Committee (GEAC) of the Ministry of Environment & Forests.  On 26.3.2002, the
 GEAC of Ministry of Environment & Forests had examined the data generated on Bt. cotton and had cleared three Bt. cotton and
 hybrids for commercial cultivation with certain conditions.

OLIVE RIDLEY TURTLE AT GAHIRMATHA BEACH IN ORISSA
12.       The issue of large scale Olive Ridley Turtles being killed by the Fishing Trawlers has attracted the  attention  for   quite  
 sometime and the Committee   too  has  noted   with   concern.
According to various news reports, experts have long established that turtle excluder devices (TED) are the only known way to reduce
 turtle casualty.  A turtle excluder device is a simple grid like instrument made of steel, aluminum or iron.  It has interspaced bars and
 a flap made of net attached to it, which allows the turtle to escape, while allowing fish to pass through between the bars into the bag
 of the trawler net. 
13.       The Committee is of the view that the non-usage of TEDS is turning the Gahirmatha beach in Orissa into the largest graveyard
 for turtles. Although, the use of turtle excluder devices (TEDS) has been made mandatory in the State, but trawler operators continue
 to flout norms with impunity.  The Ministry of Environment & Forests should take-up this issue on priority basis in coordination with
 the State Governments and take steps to strictly enforce the usage of turtle excluder devices (TED).
TAJ PROTECTION MISSION
14.       The Committee was informed that there were ten ongoing schemes, which were to be completed during this year’s budgetary
 support of Rs. 25.00 crores but as the funds are provided by Union and State Government of Uttar Pradesh on matching basis i.e.
 50:50 basis, there exists every likelihood of delays in completion of Projects.
14.1     According to News reports, the Taj continues to reel under the onslaught of suspended particulate matter (SPM) and sulphur
 dioxide (SO2), despite the closure of polluting units in its vicinity from December 31, 2001,.  According to the latest data of the
 Archaeological Survey of India (ASI), the SPM was 286.3 microgrammes per cubic metre on December 10, 2001.  But on January 9,
 2002, even after the closure of all industries, the SPM level was as high as 522.8 microgrammes per cubic metre.  Likewise, the SO2
 level in the air was 21.3 microgrammes per cubic metre on December 9, 2001, while it was recorded at 23 microgrammes per cubic
 meter on January 8, 2002. 
15.       The Committee is of the view that the levels of both the pollutants might not have come down due to the pollution load of the
 Mathura Oil Refinery.  The rise in SPM and SO2 levels has also been caused because of the heavy vehicles going via Agra.  The
 Ministry should also examine recommendations of the Vardarajan Committee, appointed by the Supreme Court to look into Taj
 pollution, which remain unimplemented while chalking out various programmes under the Taj Protection Mission.
VEHICULAR POLLUTION CONTROL MEASURES
16.       The Committee is aware that as regards the use of CNG as a transport fuel with special reference to public transport in the
 National Capital Region, the Supreme Court in continuation of its order dated 28
th July 1998 delivered a landmark ruling on April 5,
 2002, after an exhaustive deliberation with the parties concerned.  According to the order, all public transport vehicles would now
 run on CNG in Delhi.  Another marked difference was other polluted cities such as Lucknow, Pune and Jodhpur finding mention in
 the verdict, with the Supreme Court suggesting that they, too, switch over to CNG for running their public transport. The Court has
 chalked out a clear-cut strategy wherein 800 diesel buses would be phased out every month in the city from May 2002 onwards
 against a corresponding installed production capacity of 1100 CNG buses.
16.1     The apex court’s verdict is a sharp departure from its earlier approach of setting a deadline for the government and expecting it
 to comply with the order.  Emphasis was laid this time on making “public health”, the core issue through the application of the
 ‘precautionary principle’ and by getting into the ‘polluter pays’  principle.  The Supreme Court has also imposed
a stiff penalty on diesel bus operators who, in defiance of the Court’s earlier order, have continued to run their vehicles on the
 polluting fuel.  A fine of Rs.500 per bus per day was being charged from April 6 2002.  This will be hiked to Rs.1000 from May 6
 until there is a complete shift to CNG.  The Supreme Court has also slapped a penalty of Rs.20, 000 on the Union government for
 repeatedly seeking modification of its order.
16.2     The “polluter pays principle”, is adopted as an underlying economic code for environmental policy.  According to it the
 polluters were liable to take steps to prevent in advance, or at least reduce, any inconveniences rising from their activity.  They were
 also responsible for any damage they cause to the environment.  The principle fixes the accountability of industries for the pollution
 they generate.  It emphasizes the fact that the costs of measures to prevent, control and reduce damage resulting from pollution
 should be borne by the polluter.  The principle does not mean one can pay for pollution and purchase the environment.
17.       The Committee is of the view that the Ministry of Environment & Forests have adequate powers under section 5 of the Air
 Act, 1981, and the Environment (Protection) Act to enforce more stringent standards for pollution at hot spots.  Apart from the
 instance of 1997, when it notified the first ever fuel quality norms, the Ministry has never made its presence felt.  It has never
 discouraged the use of diesel by the Indian auto sector.  There has thus been no show of commitment or initiative on the part of the
 Ministry.
17.1     The Ministry of Environment & Forest’s 1997 white paper on pollution in Delhi is little more than a common position of all
 the other Union government agencies concerned, such as Ministry of Industry, Ministry of Surface Transport and Ministry of
 Petroleum and Natural gas.  It does nothing more to push for CNG than propose government tradition of making toothless but
 politically correct suggestions.

17.2     The Committee is of the view that Environmental regulation through Courts has serious drawbacks. Judicial intervention is

 The Traffic Police as the Press reports suggest, are exploiting the situation by forcing the operators to grease their palm.  The

FOREST CONSERVATION, DEVELOPMENT & REGENERATION
18.       Members have raised the controls of large areas of densely forested regions in the country by Naxalites on more than one
 occasion in the Committee. In these forested areas, the state and forest administration dare not enter. Naxalites use the growing
 alienation of tribal people against forest laws to gain ground. They build check dams where governments cannot because of
 restrictive forest conservation laws. They secure tribal rights over non-timber forest produce and dispense quick justice — at
 gunpoint. There are also massive financial gains for the Naxals in looting state forests. Poachers to timber mafia and protection
 money from paper industry, all contribute to their "revolution". According to reports by ensuring people’s access to forest and
 distributing forestlands for cultivation, today Naxalites have established parallel governments and nearly 10.03million hectares (mha)
 of forests in the country are under the control of Naxalites.
18.1     According to the 1999 Forest Survey Report ,  over 0.4 mha of forests in the country were under encroachment — 60 per cent
 of which were in the Naxalite-affected areas and in  almost all the Naxalite-affected districts, forest loss has been attributed to
 encroachment due to ‘local disturbances’.
18.2     In a meeting with the Standing Committee on Developments of  Meghalaya Legislative Assembly the Committee was
 apprised of the imposition of   an interim ban on felling in forests on December 12, 1996. Having India’s one-fourth of forests, the
 seven states are worst affected as a large chunk of their population earns its livelihood from forests. The ban has come at a time when
 the region was losing 31,700 hectares every year, mostly due to the government and timber traders’ nexus. It also made the ban
 conditional: by putting in place a sustainable way of using the forests. The proposal makes sense for a region, where traditional
 village institutions control and manage 80 per cent of forests.  For a region that largely depends on forests for economic and
 livelihood survival, the ban has come short of a death sentence. The economic loss is unimaginable, particularly for poor forest
 dwellers that form a majority in the seven states — Arunachal Pradesh, Mizoram, Nagaland, Assam, Tripura, Manipur and
 Meghalaya.
18.3     The Supreme Court has observed that till date, a clear definition of forests is not given in the existing legal instruments —
 Forest Conservation Act (FCA), 1980, Forest Conservation (Amendment) Act, 1988, and the Indian Forest Act, 1927. Under Section
 2 of the FCA forests have been defined as "reserved forest, protected forest or any area recorded as forest in the government record".
 The court also stated that forest should be understood according to its dictionary meaning — "a large area of land thickly covered
 with trees, bushes, etc". Then it ordered every state to form an expert committee, which would identify forests, irrespective of class or
 ownership; all wood-based industries and assess the sustainability of forests. The ruling also reinforced the formulation of rules
 before any felling was resorted to. Though private plantations and social forestry programmes, were excluded from the ban in the
 first ruling, the court brought these areas in the ambit of the ban in its next ruling.
18.4     State governments have been slow in formulating the working plan, mandatory for carrying out felling and as a result only 14
 per cent of the plans have been completed in the last five years and some states still do not have a single working plan in place.
 Manipur and Mizoram did not have even a single working plan in place till October 2001. Similarly in Nagaland, where timber is the
 main source of livelihood for local people, the state formulated working plans for just 0.1 per cent of forests This is despite Ministry
 of Environment & Forests setting up a regional centre in Shillong for easy access.
18.5     The Supreme Court have not been happy the way governments were trying to frustrate the implementation of the ban. 
 Therefore on February 18, 2002, the Supreme Court slapped a fine of Rs.5,000 on the Union government for not framing a
 comprehensive scheme regarding forestland approved for deforestation. Similarly, state governments too were being pulled up for
 misguiding the court.

 country.  Besides, the categorisation of forests into reserved, protected and village forests, which are essentially administrative
 categories, does not help in their growth.  The “State of the Forest Report”, does not give a detailed classification of India’s forests,
 the percentage of natural or pristine and plantation areas, which would be of interest and use to both environmentalists and wood
 based industry. The neglect of forests has resulted into loss of produce with reported smuggling by mafias, fires – which are termed
 deliberate. This has affected their growth as it discourages participation of the people.  While tribals in some parts of the country
 have a right to enter the forests and take a part of the produce, mainly wood, for meeting their bonafide requirements, it appears to
 the Committee that this has not helped in the growth of forests because the denser the forest, the more is the alienation — tribal
 people are refused entry into forests; they cannot cultivate land they believe is theirs. It is simple. People want livelihood through
 forest but government wants control over forests.  The Committee therefore feels that it is time the forests are taken out completely
 from the bureaucratic control and managed by direct participation of the people preferably through cooperatives.  There is thus a need
 to take a dynamic initiative for the management of the forests so that the objectives of the forest policy could be met with direct
 involvement of the people in the management and growth of forests.
20.       The Committee was informed that people could collect Tendu leaves and minor forest produce for their livelihood under the

 granted rights to collect minor forest produce from Scheduled Areas under Panchayat (Extension to Scheduled Area) Act, 1996. The
 Ministry of Environment & Forests has already written to the various State Governments to take necessary action in this regard.
21.       The Committee further observes that the Forest (Conservation) Act is only regulatory in nature and has enough flexibility to
 meet the development needs of the State Government.  Therefore, there is no need to amend the Forest (Conservation) Act. 
 However, there is a need to re-examine issues under the Indian Forest Act, 1927 so that it takes care of the definition of forests.
SUNDERBAN MANGROVES
22.       The Committee was informed that the “Sunderban Mangroves” are the most extensive mangroves in the country and form the
 largest single mangrove of the world.  The area occupies 1434 sq. kms, which includes 578 sq. kms of dense mangroves, and 856 sq.
 kms of sparse mangroves.  It occupies 44% of the total mangrove area of the country.   The salient features of the Sunderban
 biosphere reserve include:
(i)
                  It has the  largest floral and faunal diversity and tiger population.
(ii)                It is selected as a World Heritage Site and a Biosphere Reserve in the Global Network of UNESCO.
(iii)               It is the home of several endangered, threatened and endemic floral and faunal species and acts as a fish nursery and
 supports coastal fisheries along Bay of Bengal and Indian Ocean. It provides protection to the inland habitations
 against fury of cyclones and acts as a sink for the mega metropolitan pollutants.
22.1.    The Sunderbans is represented with 26 species of mangroves covering 14 genera and 11 families and 34 species of mangrove
 associates covering 28 genera and 18 families. 
22.2.    Taking into consideration the ecological and economic significance of mangroves in Sunderbans and the threats faced by these
 mangroves, Government  has identified this area for intensive conservation and management under the Scheme on Conservation and
 Management of Mangroves and Coral Reefs since 1987.  The main objectives of the management action plan for conservation of
 Sunderban mangroves include:

 long term conservation of Sunderbans, the Ministry of Environment & Forests should devise Management Plans which may involve
 joint effort of  India and Bangladesh.

JHUM/SHIFTING CULTIVATION
26.       The Secretary informed the Committee that as per estimates, about 10 million ha. of forest areas have been subjected to
 Jhum/Shifting cultivation. Shifting cultivation is practiced in tribal dominant areas mostly in the North-Eastern States where it
 accounts for most of the forest degradation. The increase in the population of Shifting Cultivators and declining productivity of forest
 soils have resulted in the Shifting Cultivation cycle shortening from about 20 years to 4 years. Such intense farming on poor quality
 soil has made the system ecologically fragile. The National Forestry Action Programme prepared for next 20 years clearly
 incorporates the objectives defined in the National Forest Policy, 1988 to control the Shifting Cultivation practice through  following
 measures:
·
        Alternative avenues of income
·        Suitably harmonised with the right land use practices
·        By propagating improved agricultural practices
·        Social forestry and energy plantations
27.       The Committee believes that the National Forest Action Programme in respect of shifting cultivation needs to be rationalised
 by Settled Cultivation with tree farming under various agro-forestry systems. Rationalisation of Shifting cultivation should be
 approached in a holistic manner, covering the socio-cultural and economic aspects and issues relating to the land tenure.
