70th Report on Demands for Grants (1999-2000)
of the  Ministry of Environment and Forests
 
 
            The mandate of the Ministry of Environment and Forests extends to planning, promotion and co-ordination of environmental
 and forestry programmes.  The main activities of the Ministry relate to conservation and control of pollution, survey of flora, fauna,
 forests and wildlife, afforestation, and re-generation of degraded areas, protection of environment and research concerning these
 subjects. The Ministry discharges its mandate through environmental impact assessment, eco-regeneration; assistance to organisations
 implementing environmental and forestry research programmes; enactment of environmental legislation; formulation of
 environmental policies; promotion of environmental and forestry research, extension, education and training to augment the requisite
 manpower; and dissemination of environmental awareness.

PRESERVATION OF ENVIRONMENT & CONTROL OF POLLUTION
 
8.         The environment policy framework comprises of the National Forest Policy (1988), the Policy Statement for Abatement of
 Pollution (1992) and the National Conservation Strategy and Policy Statement on Environment & Development (1992).  The National
 Conservation Strategy provides for the integration of environmental considerations in the policies and programmes of different
 sectors.  It emphasises sustainable life styles and the proper management and conservation of resources.  The Pollution Abatement
 Policy stresses the prevention of pollution at source.  It encourages the development and application of the best available practical
 technical solutions.  The policy conforms to the “Polluter pays ” principle.  It underlines the protection of heavily polluted areas and
 river stretches.  The Forest Policy stresses the maintenance of environment through preservation and restoration of ecological
 balance.  The policy seeks to substantially increase the forest cover in the country through afforestation programmes.
9.         The statutory framework for the environment includes the Indian Forest Act, 1927, the Forest (Conservation) Act, 1982, the
 Water (Prevention & Control of Pollution) Act, 1981 and the Environment (Prevention) Act, 1986.  Other enactments include the
 Public Liability Insurance Act, 1991, the National Tribunal Act, 1995 and the National Environment Appellate Authority Act, 1997. 
 Implementation is undertaken by various agencies of the Central and State Governments.
10.        On the question of steps taken to control the vehicular pollution, the Committee was informed that a national programme for
 control of vehicular pollution involves the following measures:-
 (i)        Progressive tightening of emission norms;
            (ii)        Improvement in fuel quality/introduction of cleaner fuels and
(iii)       traffic management, improvements in road network, public transport system and introduction of mass rapid transport
 system.
 
11.        The Government have drawn up a major programme for improvement in the quality of automotive fuels. Low-lead petrol had
 been introduced in the four metro cities of Delhi, Mumbai, Calcutta and Chennai with effect from June 1994.  Unleaded petrol was
 introduced in the four metro-cities from 1
st April, 1995.  Since 1 st  September, 1998 only unleaded petrol is available in the city of
 Delhi.  This has resulted in reduction of about 60% in the ambient levels of lead in Delhi.  Low sulphur diesel was introduced in Agra 
 Since 1st  September, 1996 and in Delhi from 15th August, 1997.
12.        In accordance with a notification issued on 31 st December, 1998, premixed oils are being supplied in the retail fuel outlets in
 Delhi to prevent the sale of low quality oils.  Specifications of 2 stroke engine oil has been notified which is effective from 1 st April,
 1999, with a view to reducing levels of pollution from 2 stroke engine vehicles.  Cleaner automobile fuels such as CNG have also
 been introduced in Delhi and Mumbai.  Unleaded petrol (ULP) has also been introduced in the capitals of all States, UTs and major 
 towns since 1 st June, 1998.  It is planned to make ULP available throughout the country by 31 st March, 2000.  Low sulphur diesel
 (0.25%) will be supplied in other parts of the country progressively.
13.        An inter-sectoral committee has been constituted during 1997 to finalise more stringent emission norms effective from 1 st
 April, 2005 for vehicles at the manufacturing stage.  The said  committee will finalise (i) the emission norms for all categories of
 vehicles at the manufacturing stage, (ii) the fuel quality specifications of automotive commercial fuels required, (iii) the reference fuel
 quality specifications at the test stage and (iv) inspection and maintenance programme required for the effective operation and
 maintenance of the vehicles, meeting such norms, in the in-use stage.
14.        In some urban areas, system of synchronized signals and traffic management has been introduced.  Awareness campaigns have
 been launched to educate the public about the statutory punitive provisions related to vehicular pollution, health hazards and
 maintenance measures for the control of pollution from vehicles.  Service stations and workshops have been authorised for pollution
 checking/tuning of polluting  vehicles and issuance of “Pollution Under Control” certificates. Pollution checking facilities are being
 enhanced.

16.        The Committee was informed that the quality of fuel being produced  in  the refineries  generally meets specifications
 prescribed by the Ministry of Environment & Forests under the Environment (Protection) Act,1986 and the specifications  laid down
 by the Bureau of Indian Standards. BIS standards are formulated by experts which include representatives of oil companies, expert
 organisations like the Indian Institute of Petroleum and vehicle manufacturers. These standards are constantly upgraded. In accordance
 with provisions contained in the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 the Ministry of Environment and Forests has with a view to
 control vehicular pollution notified fuel quality specifications for 1996 and 2000 for emission related parameters. These specifications
 have been recommended by expert committees set up by the Ministry of Environment and Forests. Presently, the Ministry and the
 Central Pollution Control Board are examining issues related to fuel quality norms for the year 2005 under the Auto-Oil Programme. 
 The Indian Institute of Petroleum, Dehradun and the Indian Oil corporation, Research & Development Centre at Faridabad have not
 reported any deviance from stipulated standards.  However, some doubts have been expressed with regard to the quality of fuel at the
 retail outlets. Fuel adulteration is checked by the State Governments.  Besides, two fuel testing laboratories are being set up which will
 be located at Noida and Gurgaon.  These laboratories would be managed by the Indian Institute of Petroleum, Dehradun, Association
 of Indian Automobile Manufacturers and Indian Oil Corporation, Research & Development Centre, Faridabad.

18.        The Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) under the National Ambient Air Quality Monitoring (NAAQM) Programme is
 monitoring air quality in 290 stations.   The data indicates that levels of sulphur-dioxide and oxides of nitrogen at most of the locations
 are found to be within prescribed limits.  However, levels of suspended particulate matter (SPM) are high at some places and the
 levels are found critical especially in the residential areas of the urban centres.  The higher levels of SPM are mainly due to the natural
 dusty conditions and the increase of vehicular traffic using diesel as fuel.  It has also been found  that city of Howrah is experiencing a
 critical situation on all the three parameters of SO2, NOx and SPM which may be due to the high density of population and increase in
 the vehicular emission load.
19.        The mitigative measures taken by the Government for prevention and control of urban air pollution include the following:
Emission Standards have been notified under the Environment (Protection) Act for about 40 categories of industries.  Besides,
 standards for ambient air quality have also been notified.  Regular monitoring of the ambient air quality and high polluting industries
 in the urban areas is being carried out by the concerned State Pollution Control Boards/Pollution Control committees.
Gross emission standards for on-road vehicles and mass emission standards for all categories of new vehicles have been notified under
 the Central Motor Vehicles rules, 1989 and are being enforced by the Department of Transport in various urban areas.
For control of pollution from automobiles, stricter emission standards, introduction of unleaded petrol in major cities have been
 undertaken.
For control of pollution from vehicles, emission norms for both petrol and diesel vehicles at the manufacturing stage were introduced
 for the first time in 1990 and made tighter in 1996.  More stringent emission norms have also been notified and will come into effect
 from 1
st April, 2000.
Ban on sale of loose 2-T oil from 31st December, 1998 in Delhi.
An Action Plan for controlling pollution in Delhi has been prepared and is being implemented by the State Government agencies and
 the Environment Pollution (Prevention & Control) Authority for National Capital Region. Similarly,an Action Plan for Mumbai
 metropolitan region has been prepared and an authority is being constituted to implement the Action Plan.
Action Plans have been drawn up for restoration of environmental quality in critically polluted areas located in various urban areas.  
 
20.        The Committee observes that in order to evolve a better monitoring structure for fuel quality control the Ministry
 should chalk out a reform programme which should include technical and medical experts in the field. The Ministry should
 take immediate steps to incorporate Fuel quality Standards for parameters related to emission in the Environment Protection
 Act (EPA) in order to make them mandatory.
 
21.        The committee is strongly of the view that the Central and State Pollution Control Boards should have a more
 systematic approach to measure ambient air-quality, with a source-wise inventory and local concentrations. The information
 should be collected on a regular basis, standardised for Metros and for all urban centres and  should be regularly displayed
 publicly. Besides,  gases like benzene and ground-level ozone levels in Indian cities should also be monitored. The Central and
 State Pollution Control Boards should also monitor the local impact of air pollution on health by commissioning
 epidemiological studies and create a comprehensive database for toxic effects of each of the emission gases.

 The Water Act, 1974 states that the main function of the CPCB shall be to promote cleanliness of streams and wells in different areas
 of the States.  Other functions of the CPCB, under the Water Act, relate to advising the Central Government, coordinating activities of

23.        The CPCB has been assigned commensurate functions under the Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1981.  Under
 the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986, powers to close industries operations and processes have been delegated by the Central
 Government to the CPCB. The Central Pollution Control Board carries out various programmes of the Ministry on a regular basis. 
 The performance of CPCB is reviewed by the Ministry every year during finalisation of its annual plan and programmes.
24.        The CPCB is also assisting many of the Authorities constituted/being constituted under sub-section (3) of Section 3 of the
 Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 and functioning under the supervision and control of the Central Government e.g. the
 Environment Pollution (Prevention and Control) Authority for the National Capital Region, the Environment Pollution (Prevention
 and Control) Authority for the Taj Trapezium Zone, the Environment pollution (Prevention and Control) Authority for Mumbai
 Metropolitan Region etc.
25.        The State Pollution Control Boards (SPCBs) are under the administrative control of respective State Governments.  The
 functions of the SPCBs under the Water Act, 1974 relate to planning for abatement of water pollution, advising State Governments,
 information dissemination, investigations and research, inspections, standards setting etc.  The SPCBs have been assigned
 commensurate functions under the Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1981. Powers to close industries, operations and
 processes have been delegated by the Central Government to the State Governments under the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986.
 The Central Governments has also delegated powers to the SPCBs to close down industrial units handling hazardous waste in
 violation of statutory provisions.
26.        There is a wide variation in the staffing pattern, organisational structure, state budgetary support, cess targets and collection
 efficiency of the SPCBs.  The performance of the SPCBs is reviewed by the concerned State Governments.  Also, the activities of the 
 SPCBs are reviewed during periodical conferences of the Chairman and Member-Secretaries of the CPCB and SPCBs.
27.        The Committee was apprised that there have been a number of studies about the structure and review of the SPCBs.  However,
 these reports have been highly prescriptive without taking into consideration the resource constraints.  At the same time, many
 deficiencies in the SPCBs
 
relating to inadequate infrastructure and lack of resources have been recognised.  Steps taken for Central/State Pollution Control
 Boards include the following:-
(i)         Increasing water cess  rate to augment their financial resources.
 
(ii)        Strengthening of Central/State Pollution Control Boards for    enhancing their monitoring and enforcement abilities in
 terms of acquisition of equipment and refurbishing of laboratory facilities.
 
(iii)       Providing training to the officials of Pollution Control Boards under the World Bank aided Industrial Pollution Control
 and Prevention Project and Environment Management Capacity Building Project.
           
28.        The Committee observes that the Pollution Control Boards are regulatory agencies for enforcement and
 implementation, of various provisions of the environmental statutes.  Enforcement continues to be the main thrust of the
 Boards, though many of the Boards are also  engaged in promoting new technologies and pollution prevention measures, but
 they are plagued by many problems. The Committee is strongly of the view that many of the problems in the SPCBs are due to
 frequent change of personnel particularly their Chairman and Member Secretaries. Sometimes the Chairman of a Pollution
 Control Board is a non-technical person such as MLAs, or from Central Services such as IAS and Forest Service Officers and
 the like have been appointed to these positions.
 29.       Therefore, the Committee recommends that the Ministry of Environment & Forests should impress upon the State
 Pollution Control Boards to allocate the post of Chairman/Member Secretaries and other important functionaries of the  State
 Board to a technical expert in the field  and they shouldn’t be subjected to frequent transfers and must have adequate tenure
 of functioning with the Board. The Committee is also  of the view that though there is a need to have organic linkages between
 the Central and State Pollution Control Boards, there is also need to have a select cadre of professionals including experts in
 the field to man them.
30.        At present  the capacities of the Pollution Control Boards are limited in terms of technical manpower and
 infrastructure.  Their capacity has to be  suitably augmented to enable them to address to various pollution related problems. 
 The need for strengthening of the Pollution Control Boards also arises in wake of the expanding areas of work relating to
 environmental protection e.g. hazardous substances, environmental impacts etc. and emerging areas e.g. bio-medical wastes,
 fly-ash etc. At the apex level the Central Pollution Control Board is only an advisory body producing enough technical
 information.  In order  to strengthen SPCBs, it is necessary that CPCB should be empowered  under Section 5 –A of the
 Environment Protection Act (EPA) not only to interact directly  with State PollutionControl Boards, but its instructions
 should become obligatory and act as a directive rather than mere advice as at present.

31.        It has been stated  that the total area of recorded forests in the country is 76.5 million hectare which is 23.27% of the total
 geographic area (328.7 million ha) of the country.  However, the forest cover assessed by the Forest Survey of India on the basis of
 satellite imageries in the State of Forest Report 1997 is 64.33 million ha (19.27% of total area of the country).  The Forest Survey of
 India prepares a comprehensive State of Forest Report including National Forest Vegetation Map once in every two years.  Besides, it
 prepares Thematic Maps (depicting forest types, species composition, crown density etc.) through use of Remote Sensing Data every
 10 years.  These estimates are at district, state and country level and serve the base line information of forest cover and its change. 
 The report and data generated are of  immense  help in policy and planning at National and State level.  The State of forest report 1999
 is yet to be published by the Forest Survey of India.  The State of Forest Report 1997 (SFR 1997) provides information on the State of
 India’s forests during the interregnum 1993-95 and the dynamics of the forest cover in the country over the previous assessment (SFR
 1995) based on visual and digital interpretation of the satellite data pertaining to the period 1993-95.  The report provides information
 for the first time on the forest cover situation in the hill and tribal districts of the country, growing stock of the standing wood in the
 selected forest areas of the country and non-forest areas of Haryana.           
32.        The National Forest Policy of 1952 had envisaged 20% of plain area and 60% of hill area of the country to be under forest and
 tree cover average of which works out to 33% under forest/tree cover.  The National Forest Policy, 1988 also set a national goal of
 having one-third of the total land area under forest and tree cover.  Though the government has taken up programmes of afforestation
 and regeneration, the inadequate financial resources and demands of ever increasing population of human beings (950 million) and
 cattle (450 million) have been the major constraints in achieving the goal set out in the National Forest Policy.  The financial
 allocation to the forestry and wildlife sectors has never increased beyond 1% of the total Plan allocation of the country whereas
 according to one estimate, average annual withdrawal of forest products (recorded and unrecorded) worth around Rs.30,000 crore. 
 Forestry operations on cost-benefit  ratio of 1:2 requires at least Rs.15000 Crore annually as against around Rs.800 Crore average
 annual allocation during the 8
th Plan.
33.        The Ministry further stated that they  have achieved  some success in increasing the tree cover by extensive social forestry
 programmes but such areas are generally in small blocks below 25 ha which would not be assessed by the Forest Survey of India (FSI)
 because of its limitations of interpretation of satellite imageries.  However, FSI has taken up the task of assessment of such smaller
 areas under tree cover.  It is estimated that the area under such plantations is around 14 million ha.  Exact figures would come out after
 the total assessment.  Adding this estimated figure, the total area under forest/tree cover becomes 78.33 million ha which is around
 23.8% of the total area of country.
34.       The Committee was informed that to achieve the national goal as envisaged in the National Forest Policy, 1988, the
 financial allocation to the forestry sector has to be raised to minimum 3% of the total Plan outlay.  Besides, Government may
 also consider the formulation of population policy to reduce the total demand of forest. The Committee is constrained to
 observe that the National Forest Policy of 1952 had, on an average, envisaged 33% of the land in the country to be under
 forest cover.  Even after a lapse of a period of 47 years what we have achieved is a little more than half way mark and the total
 area under forest cover merely works out to 19.27%.  The Committee feels that the task of increasing or extending the forest
 cover should be the least expensive project and can afford considerable employment opportunities to rural population.  Going
 by this yardstick, the Committee feels that the budgetary provision spent all these years has been a sheer wastage.  There is
 thus a need to review and recast the forestry programme.  It is all the more necessary to contain effects of global warming.
 This can be done by increasing the forestry cover which would produce better  climatic changes  all over the country.
 
 
 
 
FOREST FIRES
 
36.        The Committee drew the attention of the Secretary, Ministry of Environment & Forests to the UNDP assisted pilot project on
 Modern Forest Fire Control Methods (MFFCM) & desired to know the extent to which the techniques derived therefrom had been
 successfully implemented in the country and whether the forest fires have in any way been significantly reduced or checked.  The
 Committee also wanted to know what was the present state of Air Operations Wing.  The Secretary informed the Committee that the
 Air Operation Wing (AOW) was created during the implementation of UNDP assisted pilot project on `Modern Forest Fire Control
 Methods’ to introduce latest fire fighting techniques in the country, which included use of helicopters and fixed wing  aircraft.  The
 aircraft and helicopters were given by the UNDP in  1984.  Piper Senneca fixed wing aircraft crashed on 25.08.1994.  Lama and Beljet
 helicopters are grounded for the past three years and no crew  are engaged at present.  The continuation of AOW with these two old
 helicopters was not considered viable and a decision has been taken by the Ministry to discontinue the AOW.  The matter for disposal
 of helicopters was taken with the Pawan  Hans Helicopters Ltd. (PHHL).  Initially, PHHL showed interest in purchase of these old
 helicopters but very recently expressed its inability to take these helicopters as they are not useful to them.  Now this Ministry is
 taking action for disposal of assets of the AOW.

COASTAL REGULATION ZONE (CRZ) & CONTROL OF MARINE POLLUTION
 
47.        The Committee was apprised by the Secretary that the Ministry has brought out a notification dated 19 th February, 1991 under
 the Environment (Protection) Act regulating activities in the coastal areas of the country.  The entire coastal area has been classified
 into four zones viz. CRZ-I, CRZ-II, CRZ-III and CRZ-IV.
 
CRZ-I  -relates to ecologically sensitive and important areas wherein no new construction activities except essential
 operational activities are permitted.
 
CRZ-II            - relates to already developed areas in municipal limits, where in activities  are permitted beyond the existing
 structure/roads as per the local norms.
 
CRZ-III- area are relatively undisturbed including those in rural areas or      areas
within  municipal limits which are not substantially built up.
 
CRZ-IV- relates to areas in Andaman Nicobar and Lakshadweep Islands.
 
48.        The Secretary informed the Committee that under the notification certain activities are prohibited within the CRZ and certain
 activities are permitted subject to clearance from the Ministry/other authorities.  The CRZ notification also stipulated that the coastal
 States/Union Territories shall prepare Coastal Zone Management Plans (CZMPs), identifying and classifying the CRZ areas within
 their respective territories in accordance with the guidelines given in the notification and obtain approval of the Central Govt. in the
 Ministry for the same.  The CZMPs for all coastal States and Union Territories have been approved by the Governemnt with certain
 conditions and modifications on 27 th September, 1996. The Ministry has constituted a National Coastal Zone Management Authority
 as well as State/Union Territory Coastal Zone Management Authorities for each of the 13 coastal states and Union Territories.  The
 Authorities have the powers to take measures for protecting and improving the quality of coastal environment and preventing; abating
 and controlling environment pollution in coastal areas.
 
49.        The Committee was apprised that the control of marine pollution is an inter-Ministerial matter.  The Departments/Ministries 
 concerned with the subject are Department of Ocean Development, Ministry of Environment & Forest, Ministry of Surface Transport,
 Ministry of Petroleum & Natural Gas and Ministry of Defence. Initially the Government of India has constituted a Committee on
 Control of Marine Pollution to co-ordinate various activities relating to prevention and control of marine pollution under the
 Chairmanship of Secretary, Environment & Forests in November, 1983.  In 1985, the Chairmanship was transferred to the Department
 of Ocean Development (DOD).  In a meeting of the Committee of Secretaries held in November, 1993, the role of various
 Departments and agencies dealing with marine pollution was  discussed and decisions were taken as  follows:-
 
Department of Ocean Development       Monitoring of marine pollution all over the sea.
 
Ministry of Environment & Forests         Prevention and control of marine pollution from land based sources.
 
Ministry of Surface Transport                Prevention and control of pollution from ships and offshore oil platforms; Monitoring and
 combating of oil pollution in major ports
 
Ministry of Petroleum & Natural Gas     Monitoring & combating of oil pollution in and around offshore oil platforms and coastal
 refineries.
 
Ministry of Defence                              Combating oil pollution all over the sea except in port limits, oil platforms and refineries.

WILDLIFE MANAGEMENT & UPKEEP OF ANIMALS
 
52.        The Committee was informed by the Secretary, Ministry of Environment & Forests that the Wild life (Protection) Act. 1972 as
 amended in 1991 assigns the conservation of wildlife to be the main objective of zoos in the country.  The conservation objectives can
 be achieved through following activities:-
(a)        Supporting the conservation of endangered species by giving species, which have no chance of survival in wild, a last
 chance of survival through coordinated breeding under ex-situ conditions and raise stocks for rehabilitating them in
 wild as and when it is appropriate and desirable.
 
(b)        Inspire amongst zoo visitors an empathy for wild animals, an understanding and awareness about the need for
 conservation of natural resources and for maintaining the ecological balance.
 
(c)        Provide opportunities for scientific studies useful for conservation in general and creation of database for sharing
 between agencies involved in in-situ and ex-situ conservation’s
 
(d)        The zoos should continue to function as rescue centres for orphaned wild animals, subject to the availability of
 appropriate housing and upkeep infrastructure.
 
53.        In order to achieve the objectives of conservation by zoos, the Act provides for creation of Central Zoo Authority to oversee
 the management of zoos and enforce minimum standards and norms for housing, upkeep and health care of zoo animals.  The
 Authority has also the responsibility for providing financial and technical inputs for improvement of zoos, organize training for zoo
 personnel and encourage research on various aspects related to zoo management.
 
54.        In pursuance of the objectives and functions assigned to the Central zoo Authority, the Authority has carried out creation of the
 Central zoo Authority.  Only four zoos have yet not been formally evaluated , but assessment about these zoos through inspection
 reports of various officers of State Government and NGOs is available. Findings of the evaluation are briefly summarised as below:
 
(i) Housing of animals in most of the zoos is not upto the required standards.  In many cases, space available is inadequate and
 environment that could allow the animals relevant biological stimuli to exhibit their natural behavior is wanting.
 
(ii) Many zoos do not have regular veterinarians, the expertise on health care of zoo animals, particularly the infant care is
 lacking.
 
(iii) The collection for endangered species in most of the zoos are genetically non-viable.  The number of species represented
 by single animals is quite substantial.
 
(iv) Population control measures are wanting and the number of specimen of prolifically breeding species in most of the cases
 exceeds the carrying capacity of the enclosures.  This many times leads to infighting injuries and deaths.  Even otherwise,
 excessive number of animals are a drain on previous resources of zoos.
 
(v) Education potential of zoos is not fully utilised due to lack of appropriate technical skills in most of the zoos is wanting.

 by this yardstick, the Committee feels that the budgetary provision spent all these years has been a sheer wastage.  There is
 thus a need to review and recast the forestry programme.  It is all the more necessary to contain effects of global warming.
 This can be done by increasing the forestry cover which would produce better  climatic changes  all over the country.
 
(Para 34)
 
7.         The Committee is not inclined  to fully share the perception of the Secretary that because of financial constraints some
 of the proposals for control of forest fire could not be implemented.  The Committee had earlier  expressed its reservations on
 the callous attitude of the Ministry towards  handling of  two helicopters in its 53rd report.   The Committee in its 3 rd, 22nd,
 30 th and 53 rd reports had dealt with this aspect and emphasised the need to raise budgetary outlays so as to provide for
 employment of modern methods of fire controls.   While the Committee does not see anything wrong in containing fires by
 traditional methods, it feels that these are largely preventive methods.  Once the forests are in grip of fire it would require fire
 engines fitted with snorkels which can cover the height of trees, water pipes and other fire extinguishing instruments etc.  In
 these times these equipment’s are necessary and the concerned agencies should be equipped to deal with any kind of fire
 accident.  A provision of Rs.15 crores for the entire ninth plan period and that too to be shared by 11 states is a grossly
 inadequate provision which  may not   help solve even the  basics of   the problem.
 
(Para 42)
8.         The Committee recommends that an agency be created at the apex level in the Ministry to coordinate all the activities in
 this area with the State governments concerned so that an integrated approach is adopted to eliminate the monster of fires. 
 Fire ditches should be dug to prevent flames reaching wider areas.  The Committee is aware of the fact that often a generous
 provision is made for buying implements  for digging ditches but the pace of implementation is too slow and by the time forest
 fire spreads most of the efforts either remain on the paper or go in vain. 
 
(Para 45)
9.         The Committee is also aware of the fact that a lot of non-governmental organisations in the name of protecting trees
 and the nature  are funded by the government  but apart from obtaining funds, they do little else.  Not a single representative
 of them has been noticed around the places of forest fires, leave alone anything done by them to tackle the fires.  Therefore the
 Committee recommends that  the Ministry  should set up a proper monitoring mechanism in coordination  with the State
 Governments for controlling forest fires.
 
(Para 46)
 
 
10.        The Committee expresses its satisfaction over the coordination of efforts between the Ministry of Environment &
 Forests and various other Departments of the Government to control marine pollution. The Committee recommends that the
 monitoring mechanism should be strengthened so that in times of crisis the threat of marine pollution is averted.
 
(Para 51)     
 
11.        The Committee observes  that in present form most of the zoos are not imparting any conservation message to the
 visitors. Infact the Zoos located in Trivandram, Goa and Mumbai are indeed in bad shape with dilapidated and unhygienic 
 enclosures with some of the animals sick & unattended.  The Committee feels that indicating the  name of the species displayed
 does not help  in creating any awareness amongst visitors regarding the wild life and its conservation.
 
(Para 59)
12.        The Committee recommends that as most of the zoos do not have a veterinarian and a whole time officer as in-charge  
 the measures  to post a regular veterinary officer an ACF as in-charge have to be taken on priority basis. The Committee is
 constrained to observe that frequent changes at the level of zoo director, curator and zoo veterinarian  take place which only
 results in lack of expertise and mismanagement of the zoo.  This needs to be avoided. Zoo should be allowed to get expertise in
 zoo management to help in effective management of the zoo.
 
(Para 60)
13.        The Committee recommends that outdated and dilapidated enclosures should be replaced with modern and naturalistic
 ones  which can meet the biological requirements of the species displayed.
 
(Para 61)
14.        The Committee observes that there is a need for comprehensive legislation on biological diversity to cover the unique
 gene-pool of the country. In this connection, the Committee is happy to note that the Ministry has taken a step in the right
 direction towards establishment of Indian Institute of Biodiversity in Arunachal Pradesh.
 
(Para 65)
