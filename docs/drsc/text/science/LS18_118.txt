I N T R O D U C T I O N
 
I, the Chairman of the Department-related Parliamentary Standing Committee on Science & Technology,
 Environment & Forests, having been authorised by the Committee to present the Report on its behalf, present this One
 Hundred-eighteenth Report on the Conservation and Environmental Management Plan for Dal-Nagin Lake, Srinagar, under
 National Lake Conservation Plan of the Ministry of Environment & Forests.
2.         The Report of the Committee deals with the Environmental Management Plan for Dal-Nagin Lake, Srinagar and
 recommends a comprehensive management plan for its restoration.
3.         The Committee initially, decided to examine aspects related to pollution in the lake, in May, 2001 and visited
 Srinagar for an on the spot study on 23.05.2001 and followed it up in June, 2003 on 6
th of June.  The Committee thereafter
 held two meetings  one with the Ministry of Environment & Forests and representatives of State Government and Advisor
 (Environment) in the Planning Commission on 24 th June 2003. Another meeting was held with an expert on 7 th August
 2003.  In the meeting held on 17th December, 2003, the Committee considered the draft report and adopted the same.

R E P O R T
 
            Dal is a Himalayan urban lake comprising of five basins and a myriad of inter- connecting channels. It is one of the
 most beautiful lakes of India and the second largest lake in the capital city of Srinagar of the State of Jammu and Kashmir.
 Mountains on its three sides surround the lake and at the foothills of the Zabawan mountain-an offshoot of the Himalayan
 chain the Dal Lake is located. The famous Moghul gardens around the lake have been laid along the shores during 16-17th
 century and their number was about five hundred but now only a few of these have survived. Dal Lake is unique in having
 hundreds of houseboats that afford an opportunity to tourists to reside on the lake in an atmosphere of peace and tranquility.
 The boats are served by Shikaras, which more or less resemble the gondolas of Venice but are smaller in size and are
 tastefully decorated. Besides the Moghul monuments, the campus of the University of Kashmir is also located along the
 shores of the lake. Overlooking the lake are two hillocks that house the famous temples of Shankaracharya and Hari Parbat.
 A perennial inflow channel enters the lake from the north and supplies about 80% of the water. Towards the southwest side
 an outflow channel drains the lake water into a tributary of the River Jhelum. Parallel to this exit is a stone-lined canal,
 which connects the lake with the tributary. This channel is used for movement of boats in and out of the lake and prevents
 inundation of floating gardens during high floods.
2.         This Lake has historically been the centre of Kashmiri civilization and has played a major role in the economy of the
 state through its attraction of tourists as well as its utilization as a source of food and water.  Traditionally the markets of
 Srinagar have thrived from the famous floating gardens and irrigated land within the lake area.
3.         Of late there has been great concern about the fast deterioration of this lake’s ecosystem.   The gradual reclamation of
 the lake to provide building and vegetable growing land and the increase in the area of floating gardens have combined with
 natural processes to reduce the area of open water within the lake area.  The present open lake area of only 12 km
2 (approx.)
 is but a vestige of what was there even in the 18 th century, and is little over half of the area existing at the turn of this
 century. The shrinking of the lake is due to "siltification" - where the feed rivers dump huge silt deposits into the water
 catchment, a process dramatically accelerated by local deforestation. Primarily, however, it is sewage of the one million
 residents of the nearby city of Srinagar, compounded by the human waste created by the 48,000 Lake Dwellers, which is
 'suffocating' the lake and contaminating its water mass. Raw sewage is feeding an already rapid growth of algae and raising
 weed growth to critical and unmanageable levels.
4.         Increases in agricultural activity and the reduction of plant cover on the hillsides surrounding the lake with the
 consequential increase in surface erosion and leaching of soil nutrients have added increasing quantities of nutrient-rich

 too apparent.  Lake weed growth is prolific.   As a consequence of such factors, today the health and very life of the Dal
 Lake are threatened and in short, the once watery jewel set within the crown of Himalayan Mountains is inflicted with slow
 death.
5.         Today, critiques often describe “Dal Lake”, as “Dull Lake” and even accuse concerned authorities of preparing for its
 funeral.   They sadly compare it with ‘Anchar Lake’ which is already finished and to ‘Wular Lake’ which was recently
 shown as only knee deep due to extensive silting and area of the lake has drastically reduced by over 60% from original 200
 sq. kms. The vistas have become a sewage canal. Srinagar city’s drains are clogged with human waste. The city has created
 satellite slums around it. Population is increasing at an alarming rate putting pressure for housing them in the land locked
 valley. As a result, mountains are losing their forests, rivers and lakes their limpidity, valley its chinars that stood as
 sentinels of time and living witnesses of the rise and fall of many a kingdom, roads their poplar and willow avenues, Mughal
 gardens the great variety of their flowers.
PHYSICAL FEATURES OF THE LAKE
6.         The Dal Lake is a postglacial lake of shallow depth and is believed by some geologists that it is the remnant of a
 Pleistocene oligotrophic Lake that once covered the entire valley of Kashmir. There are other geologists who opine that it
 was a flood plain lake. The Dal Lake is at an altitude of  1600 m above sea level and lies between 34
o 5’ and  34o 10’ North
 Latitude and 74o 8’ and 79o 7’ East Longitude. With a catchment area of about 300 sq., km the lake is fed by several small
 streams around the shoreline, by springs under the lakebed and by one large stream, Telbal Nalla on the northern end of the
 lake.  Outflow from the lake is though a weir and lock system, Dal Gate at the south-western corner into Chinar Bagh which
 is a loop channel of Jhelum River and through Amir Khan Nalla into the Anchar Lake.
7.         The Dal Lake is multibasined with Hazratbal, the Bod-Dal, and the Gagribal and the Nagin as its four basins. The
 Hazratbal basin is situated between the Telbal and the Nishat.  On the eastern side of the basin is situated Nagin Bagh, while
 on its western side two famous Mughal gardens are located. The area of the Bod-Dal extends from Kotarkhana to Nishat
 Bund.  A small island, Ropa Lank, in its centre is the conspicuous feature of this part of the lake.   The Gagribal basin
 extends from Kotarkhana to Nehru Park.  This part of the lake touched the outer fringes of Shankaracharya hill before 1930
 when the present Boulevard Road was constructed.   The Nagin basin, which is separated from the main lake by a narrow
 strip of marshy land, is connected with the Hazratbal basin on the northwest.
8.         Morphometrically, these basins differ markedly in regard to their area, volume, maximum depth and indices of
 shoreline development, etc. Among different lake basins of Dal Lake the largest in area is the Hazratbal (3.54km
2) and the
 smallest the Nagin (0.89km2), but the Nagin is the deepest (6.0 m) and the Gagribal the shallowest (2.5 m).  The lake basin
 walls are essentially convex towards the water as the volume index figures are less than unity. The ratio between and mean
 and the maximum depth ranges between 0.20 and 0.25, indicating the gentle slope of the lakebed. The total water surface of
 the Dal Lake is 11.45 sq. km and total volume estimated at 9.83 X 10 6  m 3.  Floating gardens covers a sizeable portion
 (approx.0.3 km.) of the lake extending from the Hazratblal basin to the Gagribal, on the western side.  The lake has been
 extensively manipulated in this part to form man-made islands separated by canals and areas of water covered by floating
 gardens. Natural deposition of silt from the Telbal Nalla has also created extensive marshes around the northern end of the
 lake.   Many dwellings have been built on these islands and reclamations, apart from the areas used for agriculture, are
 extensively covered by willow and poplar trees.
9.         The catchment area of Dal Lake can be broadly divided into five subcatchments: (i) Dachigam, (ii) Telbal, (iii) Lake
 Hillside, (iv) Srinagar North, and (v) Srinagar City.   Among these subcatchments, Dachigam is the largest in area (145 x
 106 m2) and comprises mainly the Dachigam Game Reserve and south-facing slopes.  The Dachigam Game Reserve is a
 restricted area and has good forest and ground cover but the south-facing slopes have been denuded of forest cover due to
 overgrazing of cattle and in the past by burning off.  It is evident that denudation of this area is resulting in significant
 contribution of silt to the Dachigam Nalla which drains this catchment area.  At the outlet of the catchment, the flow of the
 Dachigam Nalla is diverted to the irrigation canals in Telbal catchment, the Harwan.  The balance continues to flow through
 Telbal Nalla into Dal Lake. The Telbal catchment, which begins at the foothills of the south-facing hills, has been denuded
 by overgrazing.  The gentle slopes in lower reaches of this area are, however, extensively used for paddy cultivation. The
 Lake Hill side catchment drains into Dal Lake via a number of small streams around the east and south sides of the lake.  
 Around the lake shore, the lower land slopes of this catchment have been utilized for rice cultivation, orchards and gardens. 
 Beyond this, the land rises very steeply to 1000 m above the lake.   The steep expose slopes do not support any vegetation,

SOURCES OF POLLUTANTS
10.       One of the main factors contributing to the environmental deterioration of the lake comes from the pressure of human
 settlements along its shores and within the lake itself. With the growth in population, the pressure on the lake and it’s
 surrounding as potential settlement areas has increased.   Agricultural activities within floating gardens and reclaimed land
 have also contributed to the pollution of the lake. A serious issue of contention is that islands have been formed and
 residences built on them, when the title to the water surface in fact restricts the use to water only.  Similarly some areas
 have been converted into solid horticulture land.  Despite increased vigilance on the part of the authorities and restrictive
 legislation and ordinances, a small number of people are continuing to develop and extend their illegal encroachments.
11.       Apart from 250 houseboats a number of villages have come up in the floating gardens i.e Tang Bagh and Chowdri
 Bagh. Treated water is available only in some areas at present.  Most of the houseboats use the lake water, which is elevated
 into a bolding tank in the roof with a hand pump.  Sewage and sullage waste is discharged directly into the lake.   Solid
 wastes such as vegetable scraps and paper are dumped into the lake.  Toilets used by the local residents have been
 constructed over water.  The Dal Lake receives pollutants from these point sources as well as non-point or diffuse sources. 
 The point sources include (i) sewage and other human wastes from the houseboats and residential huts within the lake area
 (ii) sewage outfalls from the adjoining settlements including some tourist spots. The non-point source, which cannot be
 exactly pinpointed, includes the run-off from agriculture fields and erosion from the catchment area.
POINT- SOURCES
12.       Continuing pressure of human settlements along the shores and within the lake itself could be singularly identified as
 one of the major causes of pollution in respect of organic load.  Over the years, a number of settlements have come up in the
 floating gardens from Tang Bagh to Chowdri Bagh.  It has been estimated that about 10,500 people live within the house
 boat area and almost the same number of people live outside this area on islands, boats etc.   The total population living
 within the other catchments of the Dal Lake is about Rs.2.5 lakhs.  As per the records of the State Town and Country
 Planning Department, there has been an increase of 25,040 people within the catchment area during 1971-77 (the population
 increased from 1,66,040 to 1,91,000) and since then about 50,000 persons have been added to this area over the passage of
 time.
13.       There are several hundreds of houseboats in the area adjacent to Boulevard Road from Dal Gate to Kotal Khana. It is
 said that houseboats represent a long tradition and basic infrastructure of tourism. The number of houseboats has
 considerably increased in recent years without significant improvement in commensurate facilities of sanitary waste
 disposal.  As a result, sewage, human wastes and sullage waste including solid wastes are directly discharged into the lake. 
NON-POINT SOURCES
14.       Run-off from the agricultural fields and erosion of hillsides in the catchment area are the major non-point sources of
 pollution causing silting and loading of nutrients. It has been mentioned in the proceeding pages that the catchment area of
 the lake can be broadly sub-divided into five sections viz. (i) Dachigam-Telbal area (ii) Lake Hill side (iii) Srinagar North,
 (iv) Srinagar City and (v) littoral area of the Dal Lake.  Among  these, Dachigam is the largest in area (145 X  10
6 m2) and it
 comprises mainly   Dachigam Game Reserve and south facing slopes with Telbal area at its foothills. Dachigam Game
 Reserve has a reasonably good forest and ground cover but its south facing slopes have been denuded due to overgrazing
 and fire.  It has resulted in increasing movement of silt to Dachigam Nalla, which drains this catchment area.  A major
 portion of the flow from this catchment area is diverted to the Harwan Reservoir and the water treatment plant at Harwan. 
 However, considerable quantities of flow laden with silt and nutrients are also led through the Telbal Nalla into the Dal
 Lake.  The gentle sloping lower reaches of the Telbal area are extensively used for paddy cultivation and as such the run-
off-from these fields significantly contribute to the concentration of soluble phosphorus.
15.       The ‘Lake Hillside’ catchment drains into the Dal Lake through a number of small streams in the east and south sides
 of the lake.  Around the lake shore, the land slopes of this catchment have been utilized for paddy cultivation, orchards and
 gardens.  Beyond this, there is steep gradient in land slope about 1000 m above the lake level, which is heavily grazed and
 denuded.
16.       The  ‘Srinagar North’ comprises mainly outer suburbs of Srinagar city that is extensively used for paddy cultivation.  
 The topography is very flat and it drains through slow flowing canals and open drains which, in parts, are grossly polluted
 with sewage and rubbish.

18.       The Lake itself forms the ‘Dal Lake’ sub-catchment and it includes floating gardens, marshes and land areas within

 Srinagar City are mostly provided from the area.
LOADING OF POLLUTANTS
19.       Dr. C.L. Trisal who obtained his doctorate from Kashmir University on “Dal Lake”, has stated that the problem of
 pollution is mainly due to the addition of nutrients particularly phosphorus and nitrogen at an accelerated rate.   These
 nutrients have been chiefly responsible for recurrence of dense macrophytic growth and deterioration of water quality.  
 "Eutrophication", is a scientific process in which the lake changes colour due to the blossoming of an algae called
 Euglenineae, which only happens when the lake is sufficiently rich in organophosphates i.e nitrates, phosphates, and
 potassium found in raw sewage and agricultural effluents. As the water becomes overloaded with nutrients, the algae
 spreads and oxygen levels falls consequently resulting in the death of plant and animal life. Man-made eutrophication as
 opposed to natural eutrophication is linked to the addition of major plant nutrients viz. phosphorus and nitrogen derived
 from human wastes, detergents, fertilizers, agricultural activities, etc. which thrives as a result of the floating population on
 the lake.
20.       According to a research study, carried out by ENEX- a group of consultants from New Zealand who prepared a report
 for the Commonwealth Fund for Technical Cooperation in 1978, among the non-point sources, the Dachigam Telbal
 subcatchment contributes significant amount of nutrient input to the lake  (5.3 tonnes P/year and 228 tonnes N/year) and a
 comparison of the nutrient input from both point and non-point sources reveals that Srinagar city and Dachingam-Telbal
 sub-catchments are mainly responsible for heavy load of nutrients going into to the lake system.
21.       Prof. (Dr.) Helmut Kroiss of the Vienna University of Technology submitted a report entitled Dal Lake Conservation
 Project in 1998’, in which he has also observed that “Dal Lake: The beauty of Kashmir is in a critical state since waste
 water, solid waste and fertilizer discharge to the lake have caused increased eutrophication with excessive growth of weed
 and algae in most of the areas”.
22.       However, the picture is slightly different when nutrient outflow pattern of the Lake is taken into consideration.   The
 two principal outflows that carry water out of the Dal Lake are Amir Khan Nalla    and the Dal Gate.  The Amir Khan Nalla
 carries water from the southern end of the lake into the River Jhelum.  Together, these represent an average outflow of
 47.3x  106  m3/year. It has been calculated that these outflows carry 1.7 tonnes of phosphorus and 42.4 tonnes of nitrogen
 out of the lake each year.  Besides, the nutrient inputs from Srinagar North are directly discharged through the Dal Gate and
 Nalla Amir Khan and as such do not enter the Dal Lake.  Thus, Dachigam-Telbal area, and the houseboat area of Dal Lake,
 are chiefly responsible from nutrient loading into the Lake.
23.       Based on the data collected for preparation of a report for the Commonwealth Fund for Technical Cooperation by
 ENEX of New Zealand Inc., the nutrient balance of the lake indicates that there is a net positive inflow of 15.5 tonnes of
 phosphate and 322 tonnes of nitrogen per year. The total outflow of nutrients from the lake ecosystem has been estimated to
 be 10 tonnes of phosphorus and 275.6 tonnes of nitrogen per year.  Thus, there is a net build up of 5.5 tonnes of phosphorus
 and 88.9 tonnes of nitrogen annually into the lake. 
24.       The Committee is of firm belief that Conservation of Dal Lake is a national issue.  It forms the axis of life for the
 Kashmir valley and therefore it is not only important from the point of view of ecology, but also from the point of
 augmenting economy of the people and ensuring peace in the region.  The Committee is therefore deeply worried about the
 deterioration of water quality and other associated problems arising out of development of eutrophic conditions, which have
 reduced the environmental, recreational and aesthetic value of this lake. The Committee therefore is strongly of the view
 that in order to formulate a plan of action to control pollution, the principal sources of these nutrients have to be identified
 and the rate of accumulation of these nutrients in the lake basin should be precisely known.
25.       The Committee was informed by the Vice Chairperson of the Jammu and Kashmir Lakes and Waterways
 Development Authority that the threat to the Dal Lake is not only posed by the encroachment problem but also by the silt
 within the nutrients which becomes a land mass over a period of years. 
26.       Hence, the Committee strongly recommends the following of these procedures and suggests acquiring a precise
 knowledge about the rate of accumulation of these nutrients at the lake basin:
·

 needed on war footing basis; and
·                                            Outright prevention of dumping of garbage in the lake should be accomplished without any further

 24th June 2003 at its headquarters in Delhi. In this context the Committee, on 7 th August 2003 also decided to have

28.       During the Committee’s deliberations on 24
th July 2003 the Committee was informed that the Planning Commission
 has been giving a lot of importance to the cleaning up of Dal Lake and its conservation and they were awaiting a Detailed
 Project Report (DPR) prepared on the basis of the Report of Dr. Helmut Kroiss, Professor at Institute for Water Quality and
 Waste Management, Vienna University of Technology in March, 1998. Pending the DPR, the Planning Commission was
 granting funds under the head of additional assistance every year. From 1997-98 onwards, the Planning Commission have
 released a total of Rs.102.00 Crores over a period of five years with the expectation that some basic work will be started by
 the Government of Jammu & Kashmir and when the DPR is finalized, that would form of the basis of  future action plan.
 This amount was not routed through the Ministry of Environment & Forests and was released to the State Government
 directly under central assistance.  The Advisor of the Planning Commission submitted before the Committee that the
 Commission did not monitor the way in which the State Government utilised the fund.  Apart from the amount released by
 the Planning Commission, the Environment & Forest Ministry also sanctioned an amount of Rs.4.00 Crores to the State
 Government for preparation of DPR and therefore the total sum received by the State Government of Jammu & Kashmir
 amounted to Rs.106.00 Crores in all. The Jammu & Kashmir Government have submitted a Utilization Certificate for
 Rs.86.00 crores.  Rest of the amount i.e. Rs.20.00 crores is earmarked for land acquisition during the current financial year
 (2003-2004). In the Committee’s meeting on 24
th July 2003 the Secretary, Ministry of Environment & Forests, Advisor,
 Planning Commission and representatives of Jammu & Kashmir State Government officials and the advisor in the Planning
 Commission impressed upon the Committee about the need to achieve synergy by linking the work done in the last five
 years with what is being proposed to be done in the next three years on the basis of DPR. The Planning Commission official
 asked for a minimum period of two months to verify the utilization certificates submitted by the J&K LAWDA.
29.       The Committee expressed surprise that the Planning Commission did not have any control over the money released
 by it to the State Government to start the basic work and therefore recommends that central assistance must be backed by
 regular monitoring.
30.       The Committee while examining the National Lake Conservation Plan (NLCP) of the Ministry of Environment &
 Forests observed that the NLCP was initiated in 1994 for cleaning important urban lakes infested with high levels of silting
 and pollution. Initially, ten lakes were identified for cleaning etc.–Ooty, Kodaikkanal, Powai, Dal, Sukhana, Sagar, Nainital,
 Udaipur, Rabindra Sagar and Hussain Sagar.  However, work has started only in one Lake and project reports for three
 Lakes namely Ooty, Powai and Kodaikanal have been prepared and approved till date.  The progress regarding other Lakes
 is extremely slow because of  persistent delays in the finalization of Detailed Project Reports (DPRs), tendering procedures
 and award of contracts.
31.       Dal Lake is no exception. In 1997, the Dal Lake Conservation Plan with an outlay of Rs.297.90 crores was placed
 before the Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs (CCEA) for its consideration. The CCEA, which met in December 1997
 approved the proposal in principle and directed to bring up the matter for consideration after appraisal of the scheme in light
 of a firm tie-up of external assistance. However, CCEA approved an amount of Rs.20.00 crores to be spent on land
 acquisition, which was considered necessary for speedy implementation of the scheme.

MEASURES FOR IMPROVEMENT OF THE LAKE
37.       The Committee understands that the lake has reached a critical stage as the nutrients are deposited in the sediments
 rather than in the lake water.  Since macrophytes have an adaptive advantage to derive nutrients from lake sediments, the
 recurring growth of these weeds is obvious in the lake ecosystem.   The macrophytes provide a biological sink to the
 nutrients, and thus play a very important role in suppressing the plankton population.   Continual expansion of macrophytes,
 have accelerated the process of eutrophication, as they have the capacity to translocate nutrients from the sediments into
 water thereby increasing nutrients available to the plankton and floating plants such as “Sylvinia-Lemna” complex.  With
 the subsequent release of nutrients from sediments, growth of plankton will be accelerated.   In conjunction with floating
 plants, such growth might cause anaerobic conditions which would make the lake unsuitable as a source of drinking water.
38.       The Committee therefore, recommends that any management programme to be adopted for the conservation of the
 lake ecosystem should primarily aim at minimizing the inflow of nutrients into the lake. Some of the measures which the
 Committee strongly feels would be helpful in diversion of pollution and would reduce the inflow of pollutants to a great

41.       Working with French engineers in 1992, Clean-Flo restored the ‘Minuere River’ in Fourmies, France, for which the
 city won two prestigious environmental awards from the French Government.  The key to the process is a special
 formulation, termed C-FLO by the company. Equipment is set up on the shore and at the bottom of the water body. The
 entire lake is artificially filled with oxygen starting from the bottom. Oxygenation kills anaerobic bacteria which live in the
 absence of oxygen, and are the source of toxic hydrogen sulphide, sulphuric acid and ammonia gas. The sulphate in
 sulphuric acid already in the lake is absorbed by microbes, plants and aquatic animals. Low-oxygen microbes convert nitric
 acid to inert nitrogen gas. Carbon in the sediment is used as an energy source, so carbon dioxide is another end-product.
42.       Generally when the Lake runs out of oxygen at the bottom, there is a massive release of phosphorus and nitrogen that
 breed weeds and algae, from the bottom sediment. Exposure to sunlight and oxygen in the atmosphere kills disease bacteria
 and toxic gases accumulated at the bottom are released. Putting oxygenated surface water on the bottom of lakes binds
 phosphorus and nitrogen to inorganic sediments like clay. This reduces the content of these two compounds in water by up
 to 97 percent and neutralizes acids so that the lake can begin its recovery.
43.       A characteristic feature of this mechanism is that no human intervention is required to operate the equipment. Once
 started, they run all day. In five to six years, a few parts in the air compressor, and in 7-10 years, bearings may wear out.
 Turning off the equipment for five days results in an algal bloom. It requires minimal maintenance - annual maintenance
 costs typically range between 10 and 20 percent of the equipment cost. The cost is not at all high, according to ‘Clean-Flo
 Laboratories, Inc.’, U.S.A., and the larger the water body, the cheaper it is. The cost is one-tenth the cost incurred in
 dredging or nutrient diversion. In India, the Kachrali Lake cleaning project in 'Inane, Maharashtra, and a few lagoons for the
 Leela Group of Hotels in Goa, have adopted Clean-Flo systems and have shown a marked improvement in the clarity of
 water as well as adequate oxygen levels even though “water- hyacinth seeds”, are present in the four-hectare Kachrali Lake.
44.       The Committee also recommends that the Ministry of Environment & Forests may examine such mechanisms/
 processes developed by ‘Clean-Flo Laboratories, Inc.’, U.S.A. which was started in 1970 and operates with a special
 formulation, termed “C-FLO” by the company. Beneficial microbes, which feed on organic sediment, ooze and peat at the
 bottom, are introduced. These microbes convert the pollutants into carbon dioxide and water. ‘C-FLO’ also adds organisms
 that are natural food for aquatic insects. 
45.       The Committee notes that certain variety of exotic fishes introduced in some water bodies in West Bengal have been
 found to be acting as cleaning agents by eating the away pollutants.  The Committee suggests that such fishes may also be
 tried in Dal Lake so that pollution and contamination levels could be brought down in a natural way.  Besides, the
 Committee suggests that both the Ministry of Environment & Forests and the J&K Government may explore the
 biotechnological options to address the issue of conservation of the lake.   Further, the Committee feels that “polluter must
 pay” principle may be applied to make the polluters responsible for their actions in spoiling the lake.
REHABILITATION & RESETTLEMENT PROGRAMME OF LAKE DWELLERS
46.       Some of the dwellers who have settled in the Dal Lake areas form part of the history of the Lake.  They have been
 doing cultivation on floating gardens and demb lands since late 19
th century and had property rights on the marsh land and
 related water channels. The Lake water is being used for irrigating vegetable fields, which have grown in number during
 recent years. The present maximum depth of the lake (Nagin basin) is 6 m. Many aquatic plants growing in the lake are used
 as food, fodder and compost by people whose main occupation has been to collect wild products of Dal Lake and grow
 vegetables for the city consumption. They were registered as tenants and revenue was collected daily from them by the state
 administration. The cultivators of the Dal Lake were called Mir Behris. In due course, the number of dwellers has been
 continuously growing and the families have got extended and the occupations have diversified. The lake dwellers claim to
 be part and parcel of the lake ecosystem.  But ironically the lake ecosystem has come under severe pressure due to their
 activities.
47.       Relocation and rehabilitation of population living inside the lake constitutes one of the most important measures to
 conserve it.  All other efforts to clean the lake may not effectively address the conservation issue without dealing with the
 problems caused by the people who are settled inside it.  The Report of Prof.(Dr.) Helmut Kroiss, which has been mentioned
 earlier, observed that “all actions within the lake such as deweeding, local aeration and skimming of algae help to fight the
 symptoms of the degradation process but will not be able to restore the desired water quality and sustainable, ecological
 development for the future”.  The same Kroiss report had also observed, “the actual population of about 50,000 people
 living in the lake area without proper sanitation will not allow sustainable development.   It has to be doubted that there
 exists an economically feasible technical solution to restore the lake and to provide adequate social development of the
 people living in this area at the same time.  It is therefore recommended to carefully consider the relocation of the majority
 of the population actually living within the lake to an area outside the lake”.

 the report however acknowledged that, “transfer of this population to new housing areas is a complex and sensitive process

 bottom, are introduced. These microbes convert the pollutants into carbon dioxide and water. ‘C-FLO’ also adds organisms
 that are natural food for aquatic insects. 
(Para 44)
11.       The Committee notes that certain variety of exotic fishes introduced in some water bodies in West Bengal have been
 found to be acting as cleaning agents by eating the away pollutants.  The Committee suggests that such fishes may also be
 tried in Dal Lake so that pollution and contamination levels could be brought down in a natural way.  Besides, the
 Committee suggests that both the Ministry of Environment & Forests and the J&K Government may explore the
 biotechnological options to address the issue of conservation of the lake.   Further, the Committee feels that “polluter must
 pay” principle may be applied to make the polluters responsible for their actions in spoiling the lake.
(Para 45)
12.       The Report of Prof.(Dr.) Helmut Kroiss, which has been mentioned earlier, observed that “all actions within the lake
 such as deweeding, local aeration and skimming of algae help to fight the symptoms of the degradation process but will not
 be able to restore the desired water quality and sustainable, ecological development for the future”.  The same Kroiss report
 had also observed, “the actual population of about 50,000 people living in the lake area without proper sanitation will not
 allow sustainable development.  It has to be doubted that there exists an economically feasible technical solution to restore
 the lake and to provide adequate social development of the people living in this area at the same time.   It is therefore
 recommended to carefully consider the relocation of the majority of the population actually living within the lake to an area
 outside the lake”.
(Para 47)
13.       Describing the relocation of the population as “the only economically feasible method to reduce pollution of the lake”
 the report however acknowledged that, “transfer of this population to new housing areas is a complex and sensitive process

(Para 48)
14.       The Committee notes with cautious optimism the observation of the Vice-Chairperson, J&K LAWDA that State was
 committed to removing all encroachments from the lake area.   The Committee therefore recommends that every effort must
 be made to ensure economic opportunities to the people in the area where they are going to be rehabilitated, so that they do

 soon from within the lake and relocated somewhere else so as to set an example before the people that the State Government
 is keen to conserve the lake by relocating those who are residing within the lake premises.  A Member suggested the
 Committee that a blueprint in this regard may be prepared by fixing targets which could be achieved annually or on a short
 term basis.  For instance in the first year some portions of the lake may be earmarked for cleaning by blocking the city waste
 flowing to the lake, treating it and separating the waste out of it before water is released to the lake and physically removing
 people residing inside the lake premises and rehabilitating them.  After assessing the work done on a quarterly basis funds
 may be released for further utilization and the Union and State Governments must do the joint monitoring of the plan so that
 proper supervision and follow up is done for its implementation.
(Para 49)
15.       The issue of conservation of Dal Lake and cleaning its water mass is a sensitive and complex issue.  After all
 people’s livelihood is integrally linked to it.  Therefore a coordinated approach harmonizing the interests of people and
 environment have to be adopted in a time bound manner.
(Para 50)
16.       Agenda 21 of the Rio Summit of 1991 specifically devoted to the issue of public awareness to preserve sources of
 water which are getting increasingly depleted.   Prof. (Dr.)  Kroiss in his important Report observed, “the whole process of
 lake restoration is linked to a common understanding of water protection.   It needs a new behaviour towards waste and
 waste handling”.
(Para 51)
17.       Stating that “…education does not interfere with any decision on the technical measures to be taken” he
 recommended that “educational part of the project should be started without any delay” and schools and media must be
 involved in the whole process”.
(Para 52)
18.       The Committee therefore recommends that following steps must be taken to improve the projects implementation.
(i)
                  Project implementation must be accompanied by close monitoring of the expenditure incurred and funds
 utilized for achieving the result;
(ii)                Funds must be released after evaluating the quality of work done;
(iii)               Setting up of Standing Committees in the Ministry of Environment   & Forests to fix responsibility for time
 and cost overruns;
(iv)              House boat owners must be motivated to extend support to the plan for the conservation of the lake;
(v)                The operation and maintenance of the sewerage system and sewage treatment plant must be carefully gone
 into at the time of construction of such systems.  Mere constructions of such systems without properly
 operating and maintaining them will not serve the purpose;
(vi)              Provision of standby generators for the operation of treatment plants must be made mandatory; and
(vii)             A comprehensive training programme for people who would be employed for operation and maintenance of
 the sewerage system, the ring sewerage system and the treatment plants, as a single unit, must be started at
 the state level.
(Para 55)
19.
 From a series of interactions with the officials of the Environment Ministry and the State Administration the
 Committee has understood the enormity of the situation and therefore underlines that positive commitment and mindset
 on the part of both the Central and the State Authorities must be developed to effectively implement the suggestions put
 forward by the Committee in its foregoing pages so that the eco-system of the lake is conserved for sustaining the
 environment and improving the social and economic situation of people.

 done hundred percent by the Central Government provided;

 a), b) & c) above.
