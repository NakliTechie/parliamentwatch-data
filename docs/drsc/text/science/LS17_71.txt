REPORT
The Chairman, Rajya Sabha in pursuance of the rules relating to the Department-related Parliamentary Standing
 Committees, referred* the Semiconductor Integrated Circuits Layout-Design Bill, 1999, as introduced in the Rajya
 Sabha on the 20 th December, 1999, and pending therein, to the Committee for examination and Report by the 15 th
 February, 2000.
2. The above reference was considered by the Committee in its meetings held on 8 th February, 2000. After some
 discussion the Committee felt that it would not be possible for it to complete the examination of the provisions of the
 Semiconductor Integrated Circuits Layout Bill-1999 ** and submit its report within the stipulated time i.e.15th
 February, 2000. Accordingly, Chairman, Rajya Sabha was requested to grant extension of time to the Committee, upto
 the 29th day of February, 2000, for presentation of its report on the Bill. The request for extension of time was granted
 and notified in the Rajya Sabha Bulletin Part II dated the 23rd February,2000.
3. Electronics and Information Technology is one of the fastest growing sectors that has played a significant role in the
 world economy. This was primarily due to the advancement in the filed of electronics, computers and
 telecommunications. Microelectronics has rightly been recognized as a core strategic technology world over, especially
 for the Information Technology (IT) based society. Design of Integrated Circuit (IC) required considerable expertise
 and several hundreds of man-years of effort depending on the complexity. Such efforts culminate into the layouts for
 ICs, which are used for fabricating the ICs in the fabrication plants. In order to encourage continued investment in
 R&D leading to technological advancements in the field of microelectronics, protection of Intellectual Property Rights
 is of utmost importance.
4. The question regarding the type of the protection to be given to the layout design of Integrated Circuits is relatively
 new. Although prefabricated components of electrical circuitry have been used for a long time in the manufacture of
 electrical equipment (for example radios) large scale integration of a multitude of electrical functions in a very small
 component became possible only a few years ago as a result of advances in Semiconductor Technology. Integrated
 Circuits are manufactured in accordance with very detailed plans or "Layout designs".
transistors were fabricated and packaged separately and then connected on a board to realise on electrical circuit. With
 the advances in microelectronics technology, it has become possible to

integrate several such devices on a small chip of semiconducting material. Such circuits are, therefore, called Integrated
 Circuits (ICs).
5. "Integrated Circuit" means a product in its final form or an intermediate form, in which the elements, at least one of
 which is an active element, and some or all of one of the connections are integrally formed in and which is intended to
 perform on electronic function.
"Layout Design" means the three – dimensional disposition, at least one
of which is an active element and of some or all of the interconnections of an integrated circuit, or such a three
 dimensional disposition prepared for an integrated circuit is intended for manufacture.
6. A layout-design of integrated circuit is a creation of human mind. It is the result of an enormous investment, both in
 terms of time of highly qualified experts and financial investment. There is a continuing need for the creation of new
 layout-designs which reduce the dimensions of existing integrated circuits and simultaneously increase their functions.

 The smaller an integrated circuit, the less is the material needed for its manufacture, and smaller is the space needed to
 accommodate it. Integrated Circuits are utilized in a large range of products, including articles of mass consumption,
 such as watches, television sets, washing machines, automobiles, etc., as well as sophisticated data processing
 equipment.
Whereas the creation of a new layout-design for an integrated circuit involves an
important investment, the copying of such a layout design may cost only a fraction of that investment. Copying may be
 done by photographing each layer of an integrated circuit and preparing masks for the protection of the integrated
 circuit on the basis of the photographs obtained. The possibility of such copying is the main reason for the introduction
 of legislation for the protection of layout designs.
7. Protecting the Intellectual Property Rights (IPRs) for layout design ensures not only an equitable reward for the
 creators but also acts as an incentive for creation. This, in turn, would encourage adequate level of investment of
 human, financial and technological resources in order to produce new technological solutions and original products for
 the benefit of the public, the advancement of technological progress, and the dissemination of this progress through
 technology transfer.
8. In the context of the integrated circuits industry, reverse engineering is the use of an existing layout design in order to
 prepare an improved layout design. Since reverse engineering through scientific evaluation and research is permitted
 for such intellectual property, individuals/enterprises have opportunities to put in efforts and create new original
 designs, provided that an improved layout design thereby created is an advanced form of technology, which is in the
 general interest. Also, protection of Intellectual Property Rights encourages voluntary licensing. If the creator can be
 reasonably sure that his creation will be protected, he would be more ready to part with his creation by allowing its re-
production or other uses against payment.
9. Layout designs of integrated circuits are not industrial designs in the sense of the laws
providing for the registration of industrial designs. This is because they do not determine the physical location, within
 the integrated circuit, of each element having an electronic function. Moreover, layout designs of integrated circuits are
 not normally patentable inventions because their creation does not require an inventive step, although it requires a great
 amount of work by an expert. Hence the need for a special kind of protection.
10. Basically, systems for the protection of IPR of layout designs are comparatively new in the domain of intellectual
 property. From the nature of the layout design of Integrated Circuits, it became clear that they could not be immediately
 and obviously accommodated in the existing systems for protection in the field of Copyrights, Industrial Designs or
 Patents. The existing practices of providing protection through the methods of Copyright and Patents do not
 appropriately accommodate the requirements of protection of Intellectual Property Rights for the layout design of
 integrated circuits. This is because in the context of layout design of integrated circuits, the concept of "originality"
 means that the creation has originated from the intellectual efforts of the creator, whether it is "novel or not"; whereas
 as per the Patent Law the idea should be original as well as novel. The copyright law is too general to accommodate the
 original ideas of scientific creation of layout designs of Integrated Circuits. Hence, the need for the Bill. The experience
 world over shows that the creator of the layout design is more confident towards the transfer of technology by allowing
 its reproduction under the environment of guarantee of protection.
11. The majority of countries had attached significance to protection of Intellectual
Property Rights in the Semiconductor Integrated Circuits. While USA has added a chapter to their Copyright Act for
 layout design, many countries e.g. Japan and Germany have provided "sui generis" protection of layout designs of
 integrated circuits which is contained in a separate Act of that country. Several other countries, mainly in Western
 Europe, are engaged in the preparation of specific laws for the protection of the layout designs of integrated circuits.
12. In 1985, World Intellectual Property Organisations (WIPO) commenced its examination of the question of the type
 of legal protection to be given to layout designs of integrated circuits. It became clear that an international multilateral
 treaty was necessary. The treaty would have to be established, as one of its basic rules- the national treatment with

 respect to the protection of the layout designs of integrated circuits. Accordingly, each country was obliged to grant to
 nationals and residents of other countries the same kind of protection as it grants to its own nationals.
13. Consequently, a draft treaty was prepared and was considered by the Committee of Experts on Intellectual Property
 in respect of integrated circuits in November, 1985. The draft treaty contains a provision requiring Contracting States to
 grant national treatment. It also contains provisions governing the scope of protection to be given to layout designs of
 integrated circuits, in particular as regards the protected acts. These include the copying of the layout design and the
 distribution of integrated circuits. The draft provisions also cover the duration of the protection and its limitations,
 which include, in particular, reverse engineering, innocent infringement, and the commercial use of articles which have
 already been introduced in the market by the proprietor of the rights or with his consent. The draft provisions also deal
 with the question of how far contracting states may require certain formalities, in particular registration, as a condition
 for protection. Finally, the treaty was concluded under the auspices of the World Intellectual Property Organisations
 (WIPO) on May 26
th , 1989 at Washington.
14. On the domestic front, a high level Expert Group was set up on Trade Related aspects of Intellectual property Rights
 (TRIPS) under the Chairmanship of Secretary, Department of Industrial Development with Secretary, Department of
 Electronics as a member. A sub-group under the Chairmanship of Secretary, Electronics was set up for the area of
 layout design of integrated circuits. The sub group evolved draft legislation following the sui generis approach based on
 inputs provided by WIPO as also the inputs from the experts from the academic institutions, R&D laboratories,
 industry, industry associations and Government departments. Moreover, as one of the signatories of World Trade
 Organisations (WTO), India is also expected to align its law in accordance with the TRIPS Agreement and formulate
 suitable legislation.
15. The Committee observes that the Bill provides for : -
i) Protection of Semiconductor Integrated Circuits Layout-Designs by the process of registration.
ii) A mechanism for distinguishing layout-Designs, which can be protected.
iii) Rules to prohibit registration of layout-Designs which are not original and/or which have been
 commercially exploited; a layout design which has been commercially exploited for more than two
 years anywhere in India or in any convention country can not be registered.
iv) A period of ten years as the term of protection for layout Designs.
(v) Power of registered proprietor for assignment and transmission as also measures against
 infringement.
(vi) Payment of royalty for use of registered layout-Design and also has a provision for determination
 or royalty payment for innocent infringement.
(vii) Dealing with willful infringement by way of punishment with imprisonment for a term which
 may extend to three years, or with fine which shall not be less than fifty thousand rupees but will not
 exceed ten lakh rupees or with both.
(viii) Appointment of a Registrar for registering the layout designs.
(ix)The mechanism of an Appellate Board consisting of Chairman, Vice-Chairman and such other
 members as the Central Government may deem fit.
(x) A transition arrangement till the Appellate Board is constituted.
(xi) A country to be notified as a convention country and also provides for reciprocity with
 convention countries.

16. The Committee considered the Semiconductor Integrated Circuits Layout-Design Bill, 1999, in its meetings held on

17. The Secretary and the representatives of the Ministry of Information Technology apprised the Committee about the
 need to provide legal regime to the layout designs of integrated circuits and the developments leading to the drafting of
 the Bill.
18. The Chairman and Members sought clarifications which pertained inter alia
, to reasons, for not amending the
 existing Copyright Act or Patents Act so to provide legal recognition to integrated circuits layout designs; need for
 bringing the Bill; provisions regarding compulsory licensing; and existence of provisions vulnerable to arbitrariness,
 such as powers assigned to the Registrar particularly with regard to the determination of originality of a Layout Design.
19. The Representatives of the Ministry of Information Technology clarified the points and answered the queries and
 stated that it was mainly because of an international obligation -–India is a signatory of TRIPS Agreement – that layout
 designs of Integrated circuits needed to be protected. At the same time it was necessary to remove the feeling of
 insecurity among several companies which are doing the work of design and creating intellectual properties in the
 country. There was an urgent need to enact a separate legislation, as existing Copyright and Patents Act are not flexible
 enough to confer protection to layout designs.
20. The Secretary (Legislative Department) also made a presentation on the subject matter of the Bill and stated that
 emphasis in the Bill is on originality and not on novelty. The unique feature of the legislation is the compulsory
 registration of IPR, unlike the Copyright Act or the Trade Marks Act where one can have such a right, even if it is
 without registration.
21. The Representative of the Semiconductor Complex Ltd., Mohali (Punjab) also briefed the Committee on layout
 design of Semiconductor Integrated Circuits. The Representative, while welcoming the Bill, suggested amendments
 particularly in the provision relating to registered users. He was of the view that instead of compulsory licensing,
 provisions should be incorporated for a ‘licensed Agreement’ which means that a company or registered proprietor
 should be allowed to execute a commercial agreement with a user, by which user would get a license to use an IC on
 the payment of license fee to such company or proprietor. In this way, burden on the registrar would be eased out. The
 Representative further suggested that the provisions in some parts of clauses 7(2) and 18(6) might cause a little
 confusion and therefore may be deleted.

SUGGESTION
(i) Shouldn’t the qualifications of the Registrar be also spelt out in the Bill?
Government’s response
 The qualifications for the Chairman and the other members of the Appellate Board have been spelt out in the Bill since
 these are statutory appointments in quasi-judicial establishment. The qualifications of the Registrar who will be
 appointed by the Central Government will be spelt out in the rules to be made under proviso to Article 309 of the
 Constitution to regulate his service. It is to be noted in this context that in the case of other Acts also like Patent Act,
 Copyright Act, Trademark Act, qualifications of the Registrar or Controller have been mentioned in the rules made
 under the Constitution. It is, therefore, proposed to follow the same approach in this case as well.
SUGGESTION
(ii) It would be preferable to clearly state in the formulation of Clause 18 that infringement means infringement done
 knowingly and willfully. The following modifications should, therefore, be done:
 In Clause 18(1) "infringed" to be replaced by "willfully infringed". In Clause 18(1) (a) "does any act" to be replaced by
 "Knowingly and willfully does any act".
 In Clause 56 and Clause 57(2) "contravenes" should be replaced by "Knowingly and willfully contravenes".
Government’s response
 We do not agree. Sub-Clause (5) of Clause 18 of the Bill is an exception to Sub-clause (1) of that Clause. The words
 "where such person does not possess any knowledge or has not reasonable ground to know" used in Sub-Clause (5)
 connote that it carves out innocent actions from Sub-Clause 91) (b) from being an infringement. The combines reading
 of Sub-clause (1) (b) and Sub-Clause (5) abundantly clarifies that only willful actions or infringement are included
 under Sub-Clause (1) (b) Such provisions are always read together at the time of interpretation. Therefore, for the
 purpose of nicety of drafting, the words "Knowledge and reasonable ground to know" have been used at only one place
 in Sub-Clause (5) of Clause (18). The same logic applied in the case of Clause 56 and Clauses 57(2).
SUGGESTION
(iii) Why should there by a difference in the retirement ages of the Chairman and Vice-Chairman which are 65 as
 compared to other members for whom it is 62?
Government’s response
 This follows the practice adopted in other similar Appellate Boards as also Tribunals. It is to be noted, in this context,
 that a member has the opportunity of becoming a Vice-Chairman after he holds office for 2 years and thus become
 eligible for the age limit of 65 years.
SUGGESTION
(iv) In Clause 38(2) "misbehaviour" should be replaced by "corruption".
Government’s response
 We do not agree. It may be noted that Article 124(4) of the Constitution has a similar expression for Supreme Court
 Judges. This expression also occurs in various Statutes of the Parliament where similar enquiry is relevant.

(v) Why is the terms of protection 10 years?
Government’s response
 The IC designs and the ICs in which these designs are incorporated usually have a short life cycle. From the earlier life
 cycle of typically around 6 to 8 years, the life cycle has come down to 3 to 5 years due to the faster introduction of
 newer products. Article 38 of the TRIPS Agreement, however, states that the terms of protection shall not be less than
 10 years and not more than 15 years. In view of the above, it was thought reasonable to limit the term of protection to
 10 years.
SUGGESTION
(VI) How and who will decide that the design is original?
Government’s response
 As brought out in Clause 7(2), the design would be considered original if it is the result of the creator’s own intellectual
 efforts and not commonly known to the creators of layout-design and manufacturers of semiconductor integrated
 circuits at the time of its creation. The Registrar will determine originality based on the information available with him
 as also through the mechanism of advertisement of the application. If no objection is received to the claim within the
 stipulated period and the design also satisfies the other criteria in Clause 7, it would be determined as original.
SUGGESTION
(VII) Why is the period of 2 years put into Clause 7 in relation to commercial exploitation?
Government’s response
 The philosophy behind enacting the legislation to protect IPR of layout designs is to encourage the creators to register
 their intellectual property as soon as it is created without even waiting for its commercial exploitation. If a design is
 already commercially exploited, two years is considered a reasonable period for the creator to make up his mind
 whether he would like to avail of the protection offered under the legislation. If he chooses not to register his design,
 even two years of commercial exploitation, no protection would be provided.
SUGGESTION
(VIII) Clause 9 of the Bill should be deleted since it gives the powers to Registrar to withdraw acceptance of the
 application.
Government’s response
 This Clause allows for the possibility that the Registrar after accepting the application but before granting registration
 may determine it to be ineligible for registration under clause 7 and hence may decide to withdraw acceptance. In any
 case, a decision of the Registrar can be appeal-able to the Appellate Board. Hence, this Clause should not be deleted.

SUGGESTION
(XIV) What is the need for Clause 24 & 25 relating to registration as registered user? Would one be required to register
 as user for getting into an agreement with a foreign company for a design which is not registered in India?
Government’s response
 The intellectual property relating IC design is a private property. The registered proprietor who is the owner of this
 property would, besides using the property himself for commercial purposes, like to license it to other users on terms
 and conditions mutually agreed to by them. Clauses 24 & 25 would ensure that the registrar would be aware of the
 authorised users and specific details of the authorisation agreed to between the registered proprietor and the registered
 user. This would also serve the purpose that may commercial use of the intellectual property without registration, as a
 registered user would imply an infringement. It is also to be noted that as per Clause 28 a registered user has the right to
 take proceedings against infringement as if he were the registered proprietor. As per Clause 29, the registered user will
 not have the right to assignment and transmission, which belongs to only the registered proprietor. It may also be noted
 that the concept of registered user is not mandated by TRIPs but is based on the functioning of the intellectual property
 rights.
 
SUGGESTION
(XV) Shouldn’t the scope of Clause 34(4) be enhanced to include the possibility of a person from the industry or
 university becoming a technical member?
Government’s response
 The philosophy behind Clause 34(3) and 34(4) is to restrict appointment as judicial member and technical member to
 persons who are part of the Indian legal Service or those having held civil judicial office for at least 10 years for a
 judicial members and a person who is holding the post of Joint Secretary to the Government of India or higher posts at
 least for 5 years with atleast 5 years experience in the area of semiconductor for a technical member. This is in line with
 the approach followed in the Trademarks Act. The idea is to have persons who have had requisite exposure with the
 Government system to legal/judicial as well as technical aspects.
SUGGESTION
(XVI) Clause 41(2) does not sound technically correct since if a part of the layout design is cancelled the remaining part

Government’s response

 remaining part of layout-design if capable of performing as an IC should be retained as registered. It follows that if the
 remaining part cannot function as IC it would not remain as registered. But the registered proprietor would still have the
 option of redesigning the IC through his own intellectual efforts and seek a fresh registration.

(XIX) Instead of layout, why we should not protect the mask?
Government’s response
 Protection of layout-design is in fact the protection of masks of the layout. It may be noted here that most of the
 countries including Japan, Germany, Australia etc. call this as protection of layout (topographies) while in America,
 they call it protection of masks.
SUGGESTION
(XX) Both the circuits and the layouts are the intellectual property of the creator. Which one the circuit or the layout
 should be protected?
Government’s response
 Yes, both the circuit and the layout-design are the intellectual property. In fact, to realise a circuit on silicon, a layout
 design of the circuit or mask of the circuit is required to be protected. Hence, it is necessary to protect the layout. It may
 be noted that the right confirmed by registration of a layout design shall be available to the registered proprietor of that
 layout design irrespective of the fact as to whether the layout design is incorporated in an article or not.
