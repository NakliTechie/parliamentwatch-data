REPORT
The Industrial sector in India had seen in the recent past many policy interventions by the Government
that include, measures to improve the Ease of Doing Business, Start Up India initiative, Digital India programme,
formulation of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC), the introduction of Goods and Services Tax
(GST) and the umbrella promotion of domestic production under the flagship ‘Make in India’ scheme, etc.
There has been invigorated emphasis on infrastructure and tangible inroads have been made in the construction
of highways, railways, power and other infrastructure sectors. Such decisive action has resulted in impetus
to the Heavy Industries sector which broadly encompasses Machines Tools, Heavy Engineering and Electrical,
Industrial and Textile machinery, Earth Moving Equipments & Tractor and Automobile industry.
2. As per the Economic Survey 2017-18, the effect of the efforts of the Government has been reflected
in the fact that the India has leabt 30 ranks over its previous rank of 130 in the World Bank’s latest ‘Doing
Business Report, 2018’. The industry associations like Automotive Component Manufacturers Association
of India (ACMA) and Confederation of Indian Industry (CIl) are also of the opinion that the recent policy
initiatives and infrastructural ventures of the Government along with the allocations made in the Union
Budget, 2018-19 are to become beneficial in the coming days to the various sectors under Heavy Industry.
According to them, policy initiatives like GST, National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT), and IBC along
with Non-performing asset (NPA) Ordinance and infrastructure projects like Bharatmala (announced in the
Budget 2018-19) aiming to build highways and roads across the country would have cascading benefits for
various Heavy Industry sub-sectors like earth moving, mining, heavy electric equipment, etc.
Department of Heavy Industry (DHI)
3. The Ministry of Heavy Industries and Public Enterprises comprises of the two departments i.e. the
Department of Heavy Industry (DHI) and the Department of Public Enterprises (DPE). The allocation of
work of the DHI entails promoting engineering industry viz, machines tools, heavy electrical, industrial
machinery and auto industry and the administration of 31 CPSEs and 5 autonomous organizations. The
Department also caters to the requirements of equipments for basic industries such as steel, mining, non-
ferrous metals, power, fertilizers, refineries, petrochemicals, shipping, paper, cement, sugar, etc. The 5
autonomous organizations under the Department are:-
(i) Automotive Research Association of India (ARAI);
(ii) Fluid Control Research Institute (FCRI)
(iii) NA TRIP Implementation Society (NATIS);
(iv) National Automotive Board (NAB); and
(v) Central Manufacturing Technology Institute (CMTI).

6. Regarding the operating CPSEs, under the administrative control of Department of Heavy
Industry, the Committee notices that their number has begun to dwindle since 2016-17. From a
total of 31 in 2016-17, they have come down to 24 in 2017-18 and currently the number of operating
CPSEs under DHI have been reduced to 22. According to the data submitted by DPE before the
Committee, amongst the Ministries/Departments of Government of India it is DHI that has seen
the largest attrition rate of CPSEs - 10 that have been approved for closure and 6 that have been
approved for strategic disinvestment.
7. In this regard, the Committee takes into cognisance the fact that depending on the NITI
Aayog’s classification of ‘High Priority’ areas for Government Ownership, CPSEs which are in
profit or those that can very well be revived/restructured can also be strategically disinvested/
closed, if they do not fall under the priority areas. Accordingly, the Committee recommends that
DHI should start classifying CPSEs under it, in tune with the ‘High Priority’ areas identified for
Government ownership. Such classification would help the Department focus its attention and funds
on CPSEs that would be identified for retention under Government ownership as per extant policy.
In the opinion of the Committee, this would in turn give more meaning to the Department’s Mission
and Vision statements.
8. Also, the Committee is of the opinion that it would be prudent for DHI to study the goods/
services offered by the CPSEs intended/identified for closure or strategic disinvestment and
understand the manner in which the void likely to be created in the market due to the absence of
Government ownership of these CPSEs is going to get filled. If such demands are proposed to be
met by imports, including cheap second-hand machinery/goods, rather than by domestic production,
then it must be assumed that the closure/strategic disinvestment would be antithetical to national
priority of promoting domestic production and ‘Make in India’ initiative and would be a matter of
great worry. Accordingly, the Committee recommends that the Department should commission a
research study to find reasons as to why they are losing CPSEs dealing with goods/services under

4
the Heavy Industries Sector. The study should also focus on the market demand currently being
met by these CPSEs and how the said demand would be met in case of their closure/strategic
disinvestment.

11. While examining the Demand for Grants (2018-19) for DHI, the Committee observed that the budgetary
allocation for the Department under the Revenue Expenditure for the last five years has gone up from ` 221
crore (RE) to ` 801 crore (RE) and the Capital Expenditure, during the same period, had gone down from
` 1034 crore (RE) to ` 306 crore (RE). The Committee further observed that the increase in Revenue
Expenditure (which is considered to be non-productive) and decrease in Capital Expenditure (which is
considered to be used for asset cteation) was not a healthy financial trend. In this regard, Secretary, DHI
while agreeing that there is a general perception that Revenue Expenditure is not productive expenditure
stated that the schemes under DHI have both Revenue and Capital Expenditure. Further clarifying this, he
submitted that when a subsidy is given under a scheme, in the long run, it was considered as productive
expenditure because its end result is productive. Because of this, the expenditure on the schemes of
Department of Heavy Industry cannot be classified in the closed compartments of ‘productive’ and
‘unproductive’.

5
12. Further, an analysis of the budget figures of DHI for the last three years shows that there has been
significant fluctuation between the Budget Estimates (BE) and Revised Estimates (RE) figures of the Capital
Expenditure. During 2015-16, the BE under Capital Expenditure of ` 1349 crore went down to ` 664 crore
at RE. In 2016-17, the BE for Capital Expenditure was ` 907 crore and it swelled up to ` 2354 crore at RE.
Again in 2017-18, ` 1615 crore BE went down to ` 306 crore during RE under Capital Expenditure. But the
actual utilisation of the said Revised Estimates under Capital Expenditure during all these years has almost
been complete.
13. In this regard, the Committee would like to suggest that such a wide gap between Revenue
and Capital Expenditure under Revised Estimates (RE) in the Budget allocations of DHI does not
paint an ideal financial picture and needed to be looked at with a holistic/balanced approach. From
the submission of the Department, the Committee infers that huge fluctuation in the Budget
Estimates (BE) and Revised Estimates (RE) in terms of Capital Expenditure figures is broadly due
to delays in approval for the anticipated closure/restructuring packages of CPSEs. Such unforeseen
delays in approval take out large amount of funds at Revised Estimates (RE) stage. This, combined
with the Revised Estimates ceiling prescribed by Ministry of Finance based on pace of expenditure,
which is applicable to the entire Budget allocations, affects the key projects like NA TRiP, FAME-
India, AUSC, etc. Accordingly, the Committee recommends that DHI should initiate appropriate
action and avoid such vast fluctuations between BE and RE allocations in future in consultation with
the Ministry of Finance (MoF).
Automobile Industry of India
14. Automobile Industry was delicensed in July 1991 with the announcement of the New Industrial
Policy, whereas, the passenger car got delicensed only in 1993. The norms for foreign investment and
import of technology have also been progressively liberalized over the years for vehicles manufacturers
to make this sector globally competitive. At present, 100% Foreign Direct Investment (FOI) is permissible
under automatic route in this sector including passenger car segment. The auto-industry is a key employment
generator, in which the Original Equipment Manufacturer (OEM) manufactures the vehicles, the inbound
auto component and logistics industry makes and delivers component and systems and the outbound
logistics and dealer network sells, maintains and distributes the vehicles. Every vehicle produced, generates
secondary and tertiary employment. The industry generates employment of 13 persons for each truck, 6
persons for each car and 4 persons for each three-wheeler and 1 person for two-wheelers. As such the
sector has multiplier effect on economic activity. If the industry produces as per its potential, it could
support employment of over 65 million people by 2026. The Government, recognizing the importance of
the automobile industry in the Indian economy, is currently working with targets set in the Automotive
Mission Plan 2016-26. The DHI is also developing an automotive policy for holistic development of the
sector in India.
15. The Industry produced a total 14.7 million vehicles which included passenger vehicles, commercial
vehicles, three wheelers and two wheelers in April-September, 2017 against 13.4 million in April- September,
2016 (% of growth rate is 9.18%). In April-September 2017, overall automobile exports were 1.95 million,
against the same period i.e. April-September 2016 of 1.76 million (% of growth rate is 10.71%).

17. The aim of NA TRiP is to create world-class automotive testing, validation,. R&D and homologation

twice; to ` 2286.06 crore in April, 2011 and to  ` 3727 crore in July, 2016. The initial scheduled date of
completion of NA TRiP was April, 2011 which underwent many extensions and the current deadline is July,
2019. In this regard, the Secretary, DHI had informed the Committee that out of ` 3727 crore, ` 2610 crore
has been released. Further, it was also submitted that the remaining amount would be mostly spent during
the coming year.

26. As part of the National Mission on Electric Mobility Plan 2020 (NEMMP), DHI formulated a scheme

intended to support the hybrid/electric vehicles, market development and its manufacturing eco-system to

amount has been determined for each category (vehicle - technology - battery type) considering the principles

34. As part of the examination of the Demands of DHI for 2018-19, the Committee heard Auto Component
Manufacturers’ Association of India (ACMA) who flagged certain concerns relating to the Government’s
push for transformation from the existing Internal Combustion Powertrains to Electrical Mobility. The

technologies for e-mobility in key components like batteries/motors/power-train, with Indian manufacturers,
etc. Further, as per the Association the hasty transformation to pure electrical vehicles could result in
significant (over 50%) of component industry getting wiped out, huge loss in jobs, total dependence on
import for key technologies and components. Accordingly, ACMA had requested through the medium of the
Committee that the Government consider only gradual adoption of electric vehicles, thereby promoting
electrification of powertrains, rather than an abrupt shift to only pure EVs. With such an approach, the end
objective of EV deployment can be achieved in a gradual manner without impacting jobs, investments and
growth of the industry. To improve the FAME -India scheme, suggestions were made to broaden its scope
to include funding/grants for xEV component development and R&D along with development of components
for Charging Infrastructure.
35. During the oral evidence of DHI, as part of the examination of their Demands 2018-19, the concerns,
of the Auto Associations regarding the sudden transformation to EV s was sounded to them by the Committee,
Addressing the concern, Secretary, Department of Heavy Industry, replied that the Department has formulated
a proactive new Automobile Policy Document to deal with the problems faced by the auto industry. This
document would act as single mouthpiece to spell out the policies/decisions of various departments/agencies
of the Government having an impact on the Automobile industry. A road map regarding the transformation
to EV technology till the year 2028 would be handed over to the industry 10 years in advance, so that the
transformation could be made smooth. This document, according to DHI, is currently open to stakeholder’s
suggestions. Further, the proposed Automobile Policy Document would leave it to the consumer to decide
whether he opts for Hybrid, Electric or Bharat VI emission compliant vehicle.
36. The Committee understands that the gradual transition to pure EVs is an inevitability
considering the limited fossil fuel reserves and heightened environmental concerns. However, it
has been felt that the path of transformation needs a lot of coordinated and well thought out planning
on the part of the Government, with the partnership of the Auto Industry. A synchronized platform
involving the relevant Ministries and other stakeholders need to be set up for policy formulation
and implementation. Further, the Committee perceives that EV penetration in the Country would
critically depend on the development of indigenous battery technology and the creation/setting up
of charging infrastructure dovetailed by appropriate charging technology. Also, the Committee is of
the considered opinion that the economic ramification of transformation to pure EV technology has
to be given priority by the Government, taking into account the fact that any laxity in proper
planning and implementation could affect millions of jobs in the auto sector. Such an impact on jobs
would be unaffordable to the economy of a developing country like India. The Department should
make concerted efforts in planning a roadmap to re-deploy the emplo yees in the conventional Auto
Sector, who would be displaced with the change over to electric mobility.
37. Taking all of the above into account, the Committee recommends that the Government should
come up with a well spelt out road map based on empirical studies before pushing hard for change
over to pure EV technology. In the opinion of the Committee, gradual shift, taking the route of
promoting appropriate hybrid technology, could be one of the adoptable methods in this direction.

77. The DHI also stated that the NITI Aayog had been entrusted with the responsibility to critically review
the performance of CPSEs and give their recommendations on their future. NITI Aayog takes a holistic
view of all relevant factors viz. the sectors in which the CPSE operates, product profile, its financial and
physical performance over the years, prospects, manpower, economic environment of business, etc.
Subsequently, NITI Aayog had classified certain CPSEs as “high priority” for Government ownership if
they fit any of the following criteria:
(a) National Security: Certain CPSEs serve such national security purposes that the Government
may like to retain direct control over them. CPSEs that are performing critical functions related
to production of defence equipment or nuclear power production fall under this category. NITI
Aayog was of the view that since the defence sector has now been opened to the private sector,
this category should not include CPSEs that are producing tools that may be going into production
of defence equipment.
(b) Sovereign function at arm’s length: Certain CPSEs serve sovereign or quasi-sovereign
functions so that it just happens to be an arm’s length body performing a function that would
otherwise be performed by the Government directly. CPSEs that are in control of the natural
resources that are low on tradability, CPSEs that enjoy specific concessions from the Government
to undertake exploration to secure the country’s energy security/future, CPSEs that perform
Government functions such as minimum prices support, and CPSEs that manage core
infrastructure (airports, railway tracks) may fall under this criterion.
(c) Markets Imperfections and Public Purpose: Some of the CPSEs perform developmental
functions that the Government may consider important, but where the private sector is either
not present or is failing to perform. CPSEs catering exclusively to certain sectors, regions or
populations group with an explicit developmental mandate fall under this category. Such CPSEs
cannot be sold because they are not meant to be purely commercial in nature.
After considering the above criteria, NITI Aayog recommended the closure of some chronically sick
CPSEs under DHI and transfer of some CPSEs to the State Government concerned. They also recommended
strategic disinvestment or merger of some profit making CPSEs. However, the final decision on closure or
disinvestment was taken by the Cabinet/CCEA after detailed study and due consultations with Ministries/
Departments concerned in the larger public interest.

(i) Hindustan Paper Corporation (RPC)
81. HPC was incorporated on 29.05.1970 with the objective of establishing pulp and paper/newsprint
production in the country to make paper available for mass consumption. HPC has two units Nagaon Paper
Mill (NPM) and Cachar Paper Mill (CPM) and subsidiaries, namely, Hindustan Newsprints Limited (HNL)
and Nagaland Pulp and Paper Corporation (NPPC). The first unit of HPC, NPM, was commissioned in
October, 1985 and the second unit CPM came into operation in April, 1988. These two units are the only
100% bamboo-based paper mills in India.
82. HPC was a profit-making company earlier and had been performing reasonably well till the year
2008-09. But thereafter, the Committee had been made to understand that its capacity utilization started
declining due to various external factors beyond the control of the management. The downtrend in performance
started from the year 2009-10 mainly due to shortage of bamboo on account of large scale severe gregarious
flowering of bamboo in NER and also due to a subsequent embargo by Mizoram Government on supply of

RECOMMENDATIONS/OBSERV ATIONS — AT A GLANCE
1. Regarding the operating CPSEs, under the administrative control of Department of Heavy
Industry, the Committee notices that their number has begun to dwindle since 2016-17. From a
total of 31 in 2016-17, they have come down to 24 in 2017-18 and currently the number of operating
CPSEs under DHI have been reduced to 22. According to the data submitted by DPE before the
Committee, amongst the Ministries/Departments of Government of India it is DHI that has seen
the largest attrition rate of CPSEs - 10 that have been approved for closure and 6 that have been
approved for strategic disinvestment. (Para No. 6)
2. In this regard, the Committee takes into cognisance the fact that depending on the NITI
Aayog’s classification of ‘High Priority’ areas for Government Ownership, CPSEs which are in
profit or those that can very well be revived/restructured can also be strategically disinvested/
closed, if they do not fall under the priority areas. Accordingly, the Committee recommends that
DHI should start classifying CPSEs under it, in tune with the ‘High Priority’ areas identified for
Government ownership. Such classification would help the Department focus its attention and funds
on CPSEs that would be identified for retention under Government ownership as per extant policy.
In the opinion of the Committee, this would in turn give more meaning to the Department’s Mission
and Vision statements. (Para No. 7)
3. Also, the Committee is of the opinion that it would be prudent for DHI to study the goods/
services offered by the CPSEs intended/identified for closure or strategic disinvestment and
understand the manner in which the void likely to be created in the market due to the absence of
Government ownership of these CPSEs is going to get filled. If such demands are proposed to be
met by imports, including cheap second-hand machinery/goods, rather than by domestic production,
then it must be assumed that the closure/strategic disinvestment would be antithetical to national
priority of promoting domestic production and ‘Make in India’ initiative and would be a matter of
great worry. Accordingly, the Committee recommends that the Department should commission a
research study to find reasons as to why they are losing CPSEs dealing with goods/services under
the Heavy Industries Sector. The study should also focus on the market demand currently being
met by these CPSEs and how the said demand would be met in case of their closure/strategic
disinvestment. (Para No. 8)
4. In this regard, the Committee would like to suggest that such a wide gap between Revenue
and Capital Expenditure under Revised Estimates (RE) in the Budget allocations of DHI does not
paint an ideal financial picture and needed to be looked at with a holistic/balanced approach. From
the submission of the Department, the Committee infers that huge fluctuation in the Budget
Estimates (BE) and Revised Estimates (RE) in terms of Capital Expenditure figures is broadly due
to delays in approval for the anticipated closure/restructuring packages of CPSEs. Such unforeseen
delays in approval take out large amount of funds at Revised Estimates (RE) stage. This, combined
with the Revised Estimates ceiling prescribed by Ministry of Finance based on pace of expenditure,
which is applicable to the entire Budget allocations, affects the key projects like NA TRiP, FAME-
India, AUSC, etc. Accordingly, the Committee recommends that DHI should initiate appropriate
action and avoid such vast fluctuations between BE and RE allocations in future in consultation with
the Ministry of Finance (MoF). (Para No. 13)

fossil fuels. (Para No. 37)
10. The Committee notices that DCCAI has been entrusted with the function of hand holding
R&D proposals in the Automobile Sector. The source of  funds for the Council till now was the Auto
Cess collected during the sale of automobiles. Going through the utilization of funds by the Council
in the recent past, the Committee finds that the utilization of funds has been below par. The same
fact was pointed out by the Committee during the last year as well. The noncommittal reply of the
Department regarding the specific action taken/proposed by them to increase the fund utilization
under DCAAI for R&D gives the Committee an indication that little has been done in this
regard. (Para No. 43)
11. The Committee also discerns the fact that Auto Cess has been subsumed after the coming
into effect of GST. That puts a stop to the hitherto available source of funds to the Council. In this
regard, the Committee takes note of the Department’s decision to seek the continuance of the
support to the ongoing projects through DCAAI by way of Budgetary Support. Pertinently, the
Committee is also aware of the need of assistance to R&D initiatives in the auto sector which is
slated for huge transformation in the wake of changing mode of mobility. Therefore, the Committee
recommends that the functions of R&D support extended by DCAAI should be continued, despite
the end of Auto Cess, as the same is one of the better avenues for R&D initiatives, alongwith other
proposed funding like Venture Capital Funding. (Para No. 44)
12. The Committee has an unequivocal and firm opinion about the cardinal role the Capital Goods
Sector of the country can play in improving the economy of India. The opinion is same about the
prospects of the Scheme for Enhancement of Competitiveness’s contributions to the Government’s
‘Make in India’ initiatives. While analysing the progress of the scheme, the Committee finds that
the money allocated for the scheme saw a reduction for first time, since its inception, at Revised
Estimates stage in 2017-18. The reasons quoted by the Department for this curtailment was the
delay in receipt of pro-rata industry contribution for the project in few cases and slow pace of progress
of the project. The Committee sought further clarification on the above reasons and their impact on
the effectiveness of the scheme.
(Para No. 55)
13. In reply, the Department assured the Committee on the progress of the projects under the
scheme and stated that projects worth ` 246.64 crore have been approved by the Competent Authority
for the year 2017-18. But, the Committee notices that even though project proposals worth of `
246.64 crore was approved, the allocated fund at Budget Estimates stage for the year was only `
150.00 crore, which was again curtailed to ` 110 crore at Revised Estimates stage. Though the
Department has submitted that the progress of the projects under the scheme is satisfactory, the
Committee does not find that entirely convincing based on the figures shown above. Curtailment at
RE stage usually happens when the utilization of funds is not up to the expected level. Hence, it

40
would not be out of the place to suspect that the progress of projects under the scheme were not up
to the mark. Taking into consideration the number of approved proposals, the Committee observes
that there is no dearth of projects under the scheme, while the problem seems to be with the pace
of implementation and the fund availability. Therefore, taking into consideration the benefits that
proper implementation of the scheme can bring to the nation; the Committee recommends that the
DHI should work on both, implementation of its ongoing projects as well as to secure required funds
from the Ministry of Finance (MoF). Here, the Committee would like to mention that the Department
while answering to the queries of the Committee chose to remain silent on the reasons behind
delayed receipts of pro-rata industry contributions.
(Para No. 56)
14. Regarding the setting up of Test and Certification Centre, the Committee observes that despite
being in the fourth year of the implementation of the scheme, the Department is yet to finalize the
DPIR of its first Certification Centre which is expected to cost ` 100 crore. Considering the fact
that the total financial allocation of the scheme for five years is ` 581.22 crore and projects worth
` 572.22 crore have already been approved, the Committee hopes that the Department would be
able to secure the required fund for the setting up of the proposed Test Centre. Based on the
submissions made before the Committee that, Common Testing Centres/Certification Centres are
the need of the hour, the Committee recommends that the Department should work more on
setting up such centres and should try and seek more fund from M/o Finance to rectify this lacuna
which otherwise can hinder the growth of India’s Capital Goods Sector. (Para No. 57)
15. The Committee finds that the funds for AUSC project during the first year i.e. 2017-18, of
` 121.00 crore (Budget Estimates) as approved by Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs (CCEA)
was curtailed to ` 90 crore at the Revised Estimates stage. The initial query of the Committee
regarding the reasons for this yielded an answer that the reduction in funds was due to a Revised
Estimates ceiling imposed by MoF. However, on further  enquiry it was revealed that the Revised
Estimates (RE) ceiling had been prescribed purely based on the pace of expenditure which has been
really slack in the case of AUSC as well as other projects/schemes under DHI. So, it turns out that
lack of proper progress in AUSC project execution had resulted in funds being taken away at Revised
Estimates stage during 2017-18.
(Para No. 67)
16. For the year 2018-19, the allocation for AUSC is only ` 100 crore whereas the CCEA approved
fund for the year is ` 608 crore. Such humongous difference in the planned expenditure and the
Actual allocation compels the Committee to presume that there are enough reasons to be
apprehensive about the progress of the Mission Mode project as pace of the project execution decides
the fund allocation. Going as per the figures approved by the CCEA, till now, the fund allocation of
the project should have been ` 729.00 crore whereas the actual allocation is ` 90 crore (Revised
Estimates, 2017-18) and ` 100 crore (Budget Estimates, 2018-19) totalling to only ` 190 crore. The
Committee also takes note of the fact that the Department is confident of meeting the timeline of
the project as submitted by them. (Para No. 68)
17. Taking the above situation into account, the Committee cannot but express its displeasure at
this juncture about the way the Mission Mode AUSC supervised by the Principal Scientific Advisor
to the Government of India is progressing. The Committee accordingly recommends that DHI, as
the primary contributor to the project, should take appropriate measures to improve the pace of the
implementation of the project in coordination with other stakeholders.
(Para No. 69)

41
18. The Committee notes that for the first year, within the fold of DHI, CMTI had been earmarked
with ` 10 crore as BE for part payment of salary of the employees of the institute. The Committee
also takes/note of the submission of DHI that the Institute has the potential to spearhead R&D
efforts in the field of Machine Tools and also help bridge the technology gap affecting the players of
Capital Goods Sector in India. It is felt that such an institute should be accorded special status
under the ‘Make in India” programme. The Committee recommends that DHI should pursue this
matter on priority and endeavour to setup more such institutes at different Capital Goods Sector
hub of the country as it was observed that only a meagre 0.5 per cent of entire turnover is being
increased in R&D, vis-a vis an average of 2 per cent investment in developing countries and 5-6 %
investment in the developed nations. Further, the Committee also recommends that the institute
should be allocated budgetary support to strengthen its core R&D activities that could enhance
manufacturing competitiveness in the country.
(Para No. 73)
19. As part of the oral evidence before the Committee in connection with the examination of
Demands 2018-19 of DRI, Secretary, DHI interalia opined that “......sometimes Government’s neglect
may be good for the CPSEs. Maybe they will be healthier on their own.” Further, it is in the knowledge
of the Committee that the Government has decided to engage in business only in a few selected
areas of operation like in the spheres of national security, sovereign functions at arms length like
the exploration of natural resources, etc. and market imperfections and Public Purpose.
Notwithstanding this, the Committee feels that the Government should take a judicious view of the
track record of the CPSEs concerned before applying the “high priority” principle devised by them
to close down/disinvest CPSEs. Also, in support to CPSEs, the Committee takes into account the
deposition of Department of Public Enterprises as part of their Demands for Grants before the
Committee that CPSEs have proved their relevance with their ability to plough in CAPEX (investment)
in recent times, while the private sector held itself back, which had helped the Government keep its
infrastructure investment targets active. Therefore, the Committee is of the opinion that while
precious public money should not be wasted on CPSEs that turn redundant, those public enterprises
which have stood the test of time can and should have some hand holding in the form of budgetary
support from the part of the Government. Besides, by their own Mission statement, the Department
has a mandate to provide necessary support to CPSEs in order to improve their overall performance
and in the opinion of the Committee, DHI should leave no stone unturned to fulfil this responsibility
vested on them.
(Para No. 80)
20. The Committee notes that HPC has been in trouble since the year 2009-10 which began with
the shortage of bamboo, its primary raw material. Further, many other external factors added to
the woes of the company over the succeeding years. Alongwith such reasons the necessity for the
upgradation of plant and machinery also weighed down the performance of HPC. A revival/
restructuring plan of the company has been pending approval of the Government since 2016-17.
During 2017-18, ` 95 crore was allocated as BE in anticipation of approval for the revival, but the
Committee finds that this money had to be withdrawn at RE stage because the revival was yet to be
approved by the competent authority. Funds have again been provisioned for 2018-19 hoping for the
approval. Further, the Committee understands that there has been a new development in the matter
which is the move to engage an Operations and Maintenance (O&M) partner to run HPC. Regarding
approval to the revival/restructuring of HPC, the Committee apprehends that further delay in the
matter can seriously jeopardise the viability of revival of HPC. The Committee expects that the

42
Government will show the requisite urgency in approving the revival/restructuring plan of the
company and hopes that the engagement of O&M partner does not herald the privatisation of HPC.
With respect to the impetus given to National Bamboo Mission, the Committees wants DHI to
coordinate with the Ministry of Agriculture/ National Bamboo Mission/ Ministry of DoNER to ensure
ample raw material supply to HPC by way of methods like captive farming promoted amongst small
and marginal local bamboo farmers as per the provisions of the Mission.
(Para No. 88)
21. Regarding the approval of the modernization plan of HSL, the Committee finds that certain
details of the approval of the modernization plan has not been shared by the Department, for
instance, the date of approval, etc. Further, the Committee observes that the HSL was allocated
` 24.81 crore during 2017-18. However, only  ` 10 crore was retained at RE stage. Balance fund of
` 14.50 crore has been kept in BE 2018-19 for completion of various ongoing projects of up-gradation
in HSL. Here, the Committee finds it ironical that an allocation of an amount of ` 14.50 crore which
was curtailed owing to the company showing negative net worth and decline in sales/turnover, despite
past investment, was reallocated in the very next year to the company as BE. Also, the Committee
notes that the NITI Aayog has recommended to hand over SSL, a subsidiary of HSL to the Government
of Rajasthan. Despite this recommendation of the NITI Aayog, the Committee finds that the
Government has gone ahead with providing budgetary support for the modernization of HSL, including
upgradation projects in SSL. It is observed that this move of DHI is at variance with their usual
stand taken in case of such CPSEs. The Committee, therefore, seeks to know the reasons behind
these actions taken by DID.
(Para No. 93)
22. The Committee’s summarisation of the approach of the Government towards the modernization
of HEC is that the Company is to be strategically disinvested, a private partner is to be brought in
to run the company on JV basis and also to hive off non-core assets like surplus land of HEC to a
separate SPY and be monetized. In this connection, the Committee notes that apart from the idea
of monetization of its land, the other two are new to Committee. Said this, the Committee is yet not
aware whether these proposals of DHI are in conformity with the recommendations of Dr. Saraswat
Committee which have been pending with the Government, for final approval, for more than a year
now. Further, the Committee notes that the Department holds a view that the present financial
condition of the Company and the repeated failures of the past revival packages have prompted
them to mull the new ideas. Here, the Committee is not convinced of the reasons ascribed by DHI,
keeping in view the fact that the after the revival packages of 2005 to 2008, HEC had shown profit
till 2012-13. But it remains a fact that the said revival packages had not focussed on modernization
of plant and machinery of HEC but were mainly debt waivers and financial restructuring. The
Committee believes that the present financial condition of HEC is the result of the lack of foresight
in not supplementing the waivers of debts with modernization plans, which ultimately caused the
Company to slip back to losses again. The Committee opines that as the administrative Department
of HEC, DHI cannot absolve itself the responsibility for the current situation at HEC. Keeping in
view the above, the Committee recommends that DHI should give full weightage to the
recommendation of Dr. Saraswat Committee which had favoured the revival of HEC by approving
the revival plan proposed by the Company, before contemplating measures like strategic
disinvestment. Besides, the Committee is highly critical of the delay on the part of the Government
in arriving at a decision regarding the revival of HEC. Administrative delay of this kind adds to the

43
misery  of CPSEs awaiting revival. It is surprising that revival/restructuring of CPSEs remains long
drawn despite the efforts of the Government claim of hastening them up. (Para No. 99)
23. The Committee wants to cite the case of NEPA as an extreme case of administrative apathy
and indifference towards the revival of a CPSE with an unignorable developmental legacy. The
Committee finds that BIFR, for a company referred to them in 1998, approved a Rehabilitation
Scheme for revival of NEPA in 2014, based on cost estimation as a part of its original Techno-
Economic Feasibility Report (TEFR), formulated in 2010. It will be anybody’s guess that approval of
a cost estimation made in 2010, as such in 2014, plagued by further implementation delay, would
only result in further conundrum for the Company. This is exactly where NEPA finds itself today.
On the Government’s part, they have released their part of funds as included in the total cost of
RMDP of NEPA. However, it was obvious from the submission of DHI that without the loan portion
materializing from the banks, for which the banks were insisting on Government guarantee, the
execution of RMDP would remain incomplete. Now, the revised cost estimate is  ` 434 crore and the
process for securing an approval for this amount from the competent authority is underway. The
progress made towards securing loan, with or without Government guarantee, has not been furnished
to the Committee. Further, the Committee is aware of the recommendation of NITI Aayog with
respect to NEPA which is that the revival process of NEPA may be permitted to complete and then
the Company may be reviewed for strategic disinvestment. The Committee is of the view that if
the current state of affairs is allowed to prevail, the revival of NEPA might remain a mirage.
Accordingly, the Committee recommends that DHI should come up with proactive and fast-tracked
measures, with accelerated inter-departmental coordination, if deemed required, to revive/
restructure NEPA, taking every care to avoid any further delay. It is further recommended that
the decision whether or not to provide Government guarantee for obtaining the bank loan portion
of the funds for executing the RMDP should also not be procrastinated any further because clarity
in this regard will give a better assessment of the availability and sources of funds for the revival
of NEPA .
(Para No. 102)
24. During the presentation in connection with examination of the DFG-2018-19, the Secretary,
DHI has stated that land assets of HCL at Hyderabad and Asansol are slated to be disposed off as per
guidelines framed by DPE. The Secretary also informed the first round of bidding process had been
completed recently, in which few Government Department had taken part, A final decision is yet to
be taken as to whom the landed assets of HCL is to be disposed off, The Committee had time and
again observed that land assets of CPSEs decided for closure should be given to Government
Department for better utility of the same. The Committee accordingly desires that DHI may in
collaboration with the authorities concerned ensure that land assets of HCL may go to proper hands
so that the larger interests of society is served.
(Para No. 104)
