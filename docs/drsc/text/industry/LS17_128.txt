I N T R O D U C T I ON
 
I, the Chairman of the Department-related Parliame ntary Standing Committee on Industry,  having   been
authorised  by the Committee, hereby present this One hundr ed Twenty Eighth Report on  Provisions and Prospects o f
Auto Policy as announced by the Government of India, in March, 2002 pertaining to the Department of Heavy Industry
(Ministry of Heavy Industries and Public Enterprises). 
2.         The Department-related Par liamentary Standing Committe e on Industry in its 47 th Report on Demands for Grants 
pertaining to the Ministry of Heavy Industries and Public  Enterprises had recommended that the Government shoul d
formulate an Auto Policy.  The Committee in its 59 th Report on  Action Taken by the Government on the 
recommendations contained in its 47th Report stressed that an  Auto Policy ma y be formulated by the Government without
further delay.   In response to the Committee’s coveted re commendations, the Department of Heavy Industry in its Action
Taken Note informed that the Government of India have brought out an Auto Policy on 7th March, 2002. 
3.         The Committee took up the Auto Policy as a nnounced by the Government for detailed discussions in order to
examine the developmental features of the policy and to know  whether the provisions enshri ned in the Auto Policy as
announced by the Government on the reco mmendations of this Committee is in  consonance with the conceived an d
original intention of the Committee and fulfilling the practical demands of the automotive industry of the country in
general. To understand the provisions and prospects of the Auto Policy and its implication on various sectors of economy,
the Committee in its series of meetings  held on 24th June and 16th July, 2002 interacted with the Secretaries of Ministry of 
Heavy Industries, Petroleum and Natura l Gas, Road Transport and Highways, Small Scale Industries, Steel, Power,
Commerce, Finance (Revenue).  
4.         In order to have the in-depth understanding of  the requirement of the automotive industry and to examine whethe r 
the Auto Policy as announced by the Governme nt is fulfilling the expect ations of the automotive industry in general, the
Committee under took study visit to Chennai from 20th to 23rd January, 2003 and interacted with   the Auto Manufacturers, 
Auto Components Manufacturers a nd Tyre Manufacturers.  The Committee in its meeting held on 22 nd May, 2003 hear d 
the representatives of the Ce ntral Road Research Institute, TELCO, Ashok Leyland, LML and Hindustan Motors Limited 
on the subject.  Two study groups of the Committee undertook study visit to Pune and Ludhiana simultaneously , from 23rd
to 26th May, 2003.  The Committee fu rther visited Gurgoan on 27 th May, 2003 and interacted wi th the representatives o f 
Sona  Koya and Hero Honda.  The Committee in its meeting held on 24 th June, 2003 heard the representatives of Maruti 
Udyog Limited, Reva Electric Car Company Limited, HMT (Tractors), Apollo Tyres. 
5.         The Committee also sought the suggestions of Auto Tyre  Manufactures Associations (ATMA) and Society of 
Indian Automobile Manufacturers (SIAM) on the Auto Policy which assisted the Committee in its examination of the Auto
Policy.    
6.      The present report is an effort towards suggesting th e Government for comprehensive Auto Policy for the benefits o f
Automotive Industry fulfilling their expectati ons and actual requirements.   It is expected that the Government would give
due consideration for modificat ion in the Auto Policy for the incorporation of the provisions as suggested by the Auto
mobile manufacturers, Auto Tyre manufacturers and Auto Components manufacturers.    
7.         The Committee is thankful to the representatives of the various Ministries and representatives of organizations who
appeared before the Committee.  The Co mmittee also wishes to thank the various Ministries and organizations fo r
furnishing their replies to the questionnaire and the points raised during the course of   oral evidence.   
8.         The Committee at its  meeting  held on  19th August, 2003 considered and adopted the Report.

OBSERVATIONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS OF THE COMMITTEE  
  
APPROACH AND METHODOLOGY ADOPTED   
  
In order to analyze the Auto Policy the Committee adopt ed various approaches and methods. At the outset the
Committee prepared a de tailed questionnaire based on various aspects of the Auto Policy and the questionnaire was 
forwarded to various Departments/Ministries for their commen ts thereon.  On receiving their comments  the Committee
formed its opinion thereon and the replies of the Ministries have been  incorporated in the report at the appropriate place.   
  
Page 6 of 47PARLIAMENT OF INDIA
7/16/2008http://10.21.217.2/book2/reports/indus/128threport/page1.htm

The Committee undertook on the spot study visit to understand the impact and implications of the auto policy on
various structural aspects of the automo tive industry through interaction at various levels.  Study visits undertaken by the
Committee provided the opportunities to unde rstand the status of automotive industry, their problems and the response o f
the industrialist to the Auto Policy. At the same time the study visits also provided the opportunity for on the spo t
observation of the industrial situation and thus helped the Committee to analyze and diagnose the prevailing conditions and
circumstances of the automotive industry on empirical-parameters  . The Committ ee took the first hand information from
the industry and thus helped in the collect ion of data from the primary source. On ce the data pertaining to the automotive
industry were collected the same were anal yzed to have diagnosis of automotive industry which helped in identification o f
the problems of the automotive industry and searching the possible solutions.   
  
While oral evidence of various auto manufacturers and auto co mponents manufacturers undertaken by the
Committee  enabled the Committee to have face to face interaction with the captains of the automotive industry.  Problems
of the automotive industry were heard in  detail and efforts were made to find out  the solutions. Direct interview of the
representatives of automotive industry by the Members of the Committee helped in having across the board discussion on
various aspects of the automotive industry.   Oral evidence  of the representative of automotive industry were recorded an d
the same were used while formulating the report.   
  
            The Committee made its efforts to study the Sectoral -specific status of automotive industry.  Analytical an d 
descriptive methodology was adopted to understand the problem s of various segments of  automotive industry i.e.
manufacturing (Two-wheelers, Three – wheelers, Cars, Tractors, Multi-utility Vehicles, Commercial vehicles).  
  
While analyzing the various segments of the automotive industry the Committee could arrive at a conclusion tha t
Auto Policy must address the various segments of the automotiv e industry i.e. to say the Auto Policy must be sectoral-
specific.   
  
            The Committee also took the help of “compara tive analysis approach”.  For example in manufacturing segments 
the Committee made its efforts to unders tand the Auto Policies of leading countri es in Car manufacturing.  On various
places data relating to automotive industry in India have been compared to the gl obal context in order to have in depth
understanding of the relevance of the Auto  Policy in the national and the global c ontext.  Comparative analysis approach
helped the Committee to understand the st ages of actions required for impleme nting the Auto Policy, the mode o f
implementation – mechanism, timing and duration of a programme of the Auto Policy and the specific segments fo r
implementation of specific course of actions.  
  
            The SWOT analysis  (Strength, Weakness, Opportunities and Threats) adopted by th e Committee intended to
understand the automotive industry in its tota l environment.  It help the Committee with the existing policies and practices
of the automotive industry, identification of the problems of the various segments  of automotive industry in mega, micro
and relevant environment.  While the mega environment helps in unde rstanding the technological and economic
environment of the automotive industry at the domestic and gl obal level, the micro environm ent helped in understanding
the industry specific environment and the Committee arrived at a conclusion for recommendi ng specific course of action
for specific segment of automotive industry.  By the relevant  environment the Committee restricted the scope of its search
for exploring the opportunities of the au tomotive industry for a better tomorrow  as well as finding out the safeguards
against the possible threats of automoti ve industry.  It is through identifyi ng the strength and opportunities of the
automotive industry the Committee have been able to pin point  the Government the “Key Areas for Massive Action”, to 
achieve the objectives of the Auto Policy  by strategic managerial decisions and adhering to the principle of Managemen t
by Objective (MBO).   
  
            The Committee also adopted the holistic appro ach.  After studying the various segments of automotive industry the
Committee made its efforts for tailoring the various segments as a whole. The Committee tried to co-relate the various 
allied sector of the automotive industry i.e. R&D, Banking, In surance, Road Safety, Auto Fuels.   The Committee made its
efforts to have an integrated approach  for understanding the automotive industry a nd to find out ways and means i.e. co-
operative and collaborative attit ude of various allied sectors of automotive industry for the betterment of the automotive
industry.  It is by adopting this approa ch the Committee came to the conclusion th at inter sectoral c ooperation is required
for the future growth and development of automotive industry.

AN OVERVIEW OF INDIAN AUTOMOTIVE INDUSTRY

            Automotive industry has universally emerge d as a significant driver in the economy in the liberalized an d
globalized economy.   Because of the inhe rent strength of automotive industry’s li nkages with down stream industries, its 
role becomes pivotal in the country’s economic and industrial development.   The well-developed automotive industry, 
with deep forward and backward linkages, ably fulfils this catalytic role by producing a wide variety of vehicles: passenger
cars, light, medium and heavy commercial vehicles, multi-utility vehicles such as jeeps, scooters, mo tor-cycles, mopeds, 
three wheelers, tractors etc. 
  
            The automotive industry in India is nearly six decades old, until 1982, only three manufacturers – M/s.  Hindustan   
Motors,  M/s.  Premier Automobiles and M/s.  Standard Motors dominated the mo torcar sector.  Owing to low volumes, i t
perpetuated obsolete technologies and was out sync with the world industry.  In 1982, Maruti Udyog Limited (MUL) came
up as a Government initiative in colla boration with Suzuki of Japan to es tablish volume production of contemporary
models.  After the lifting of licensing in 1993, 18 new ventures have come up of  which 17 are for manufacture of cars. 
Besides the 17 manufactures of cars I ndia has 9 manufactures of commercial vehicles, 14 of 2/3 Wheelers and 10 o f
Tractors and 5of engines. 
  
            The automotive Industry comprising of the au tomobile and the auto component sectors has made great advances
since delicensing and opening up of the sector to FDI in 1993.   The investment in the i ndustry is Rs. 50,000 crore up to
31.3.2000.  The turnover the of automob ile industry exceeded Rs. 63,025 crore in  2000-2001.  Including turnover of the 
auto-component sector, the automotive industry’s turnover exceeded Rs. 81,600 crore approximately.  The industry
provides direct employment to 4.5 lakhs and generates indirect employment of 1 crore.  The contribution of automotive
industry to GDP has risen from 2.77% in 1992-93 to 4.0% in 1998-99.

automotive industry has witnessed a substantial addition in capacity creation since 1993- 94.    The installed capacity of the

            India’s automotive component industry manufactu res the entire range of parts required by the domestic automobile
industry and currently employs about 250,000 persons.  Auto co mponent manufacturers supply to two kinds of buyers (i)
original equipment manufactures (OEM) and (ii) the replacement market.  The replacement market is characterized by the
presence of several small-scal e suppliers who score over the organized players in terms of  excise duty exemptions an d
lower overheads.  The demand from the OEM market, on the other hand, is dependent on the demand for new vehicles. 
  
            Agricultural machinery mainly consists of Agricultural Tractors, Power Tiller s, Combine Harvesters and othe r
machinery and implements.  Due to negligible production of power tillers, combined harvesters and other machineries, this
sector is mainly dominated by agricultural tractors.  At pres ent, there are  14 units in th e organized sector manufacturing
agricultural tractors covering a wide range from lower Horse Power in the range  of 16-20 HP to higher Horse Power of 50 
and above.  The industry made a beginning in 1961 with a to tal production of 880 units.  There has been a substantial
growth in the production of tractors si nce late 1980s and the production has reac hed the level of 2,34,575 in 2000-2001.  
Though tractor industry started producti on by importing technology from renowned manufacturers in USA, UK, USSR,
Germany, Poland, Czechoslovaki a etc.,  over the years, tec hnology has been fully absorbed.  Some tractor manufacturers
have introduced higher Horse Power tracto rs of 75 HP with imported components for meeting the specific requirement o f
high HP category of tracto rs.  The production of tractors in the range of 31-40 HP is around 60%, in the range of 41 HP 
and above is 23% and below 30 HP categor y is 17% of the total production.  Traditionally, Haryana, Punjab and Utta r
Pradesh are the main States for the tracto r market.  The new markets for tractors in the States of Madhya Pradesh, Andhra
Pradesh, Tamilnadu, Maharashtra and Gujarat States are growing at a faster pace.  
  
The auto sector (excluding Tractors ) attained a steep cumulative annual growth of 22% between 1992 and 1997. 
The Tractors achieved a cumulative annual growth of 16% .  Component production grew by 28%.  There has been a
slowdown in the automobile sector in the past two years.  However, the component industry maintained a low but positive
growth rate mainly due to its export pe rformance.  Over the year s, the component industry has maintained a 10% - 12% 
share of exports in the total production.

Industrial Policy-Automotive Sector:- 
  
            Automotive sector is a delicensed sector.  As  such, no licence is required for setting up a new unit except when a
unit is being set up within a periphery of 25 kms of a city  having population of more than one million.  Since July 1991
when new Industrial Policy was announced, the automotive sector was covered in  priority sector i. e. automatic approval
was being granted by the RBI for foreign equity up to 51%.  Government approval through FIPB was necessary for foreign
equity beyond 51%.  However, now even 100% foreign direct investment is being allowed through automatic route by RBI
since January, 2000.  Government approval is necessary only in some exceptiona l cases in case of FDI.  Automatic
approval is also granted by the RBI fo r foreign technology agreements where th e lumpsum payment does not exeed Rs. 7
crores (US $ 2 million) and royalty does not exceed 5% on domestic sale and 8% on expor ts.  Government has furthe r
liberalized the technology transfer agreem ents and now royalty is being allowed by 100% wholly owned subsidiary to the
parent company.  Quantitative Restriction on imports have been lifted from  1.4.2001.  DGFT has issued notification fo r
regulating the import of new and old vehicles taking into account the environmental and road safety. 
  
  
OBSERVATIONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS 
  
            Having gone through the status of automotive industry in I ndia, the Committee finds that Indian auto industry has
the potential to become a key growth dr iver of the Indian economy.  Indian automobile industry has since long been 
governed by regulations.  Imports, collabor ations and equity ventures were re stricted by the Government.  Capacity
expansion was restricted and required licenses were issued by the Governme nt.  Technology transfer from foreign
companies and payments therefore were al so restricted and subject to Government  approval.  However, with the onset o f
liberalization and globalisation process, the global automotive industry is on the move, therefore, the domestic automotive
industry needs to catch up with pace of gl obal development.  For this to be achieved the Government needs to play a more
active role in developing this industry.  The policy levers adopted so far by th e government do not pr ovide a sufficiently
strong support framework to the industry play ers.  Other countries seem to have no t only clearly stated goals, but also a
more comprehensive and coherent  approach to policies.  Successful devel opment of the auto industry in other countries
reveal several principles  for effective policy.  India needs a more proactive auto polic y, for India to achieve the industry
vision. Such an effective auto policy ha s to target the specific needs of each  segment of the industry depending upon its
maturity level and competitiveness.

segment to meet the vision.

ENVIRONMENTAL ASPECTS 
 
The automotive and oil industry have to heave together to constantly  fulfill environment  imperatives.  The

policy covering the other relate d aspects and ensure availab ility of appropriate auto fuel/fuel mixes at minimum social
costs across the country.  Suitable institutional mechan ism will be put in place for certification, monitoring an d
enforcement of different technologies/fuel mixes.  Appropriate fiscal measures will be devised to achieve milestones in the
roadmap for implementation of the auto fuel policy. 
  
The  Government will also encourage the use of short chain  hydrocarbons along with other  auto fuels of the
quality necessary to meet the vehicular emission norms. 
  
There is prime need to support the development and in troduction of vehicles prope lled   by energy sources othe r
than hydrocarbons by promoting appropriate automotive tec hnology.  Hybrid vehicles a nd vehicles  operating with

treasured.  As an impetus for the developm ent of such vehicles, an appropriate  l ong-term fiscal structure shall be put in 
place to  facilitate their acceptance vis-à-vis vehicles based on conventional fuels. 
  
Internationally, the practice is to levy higher road tax on older vehicles in order to discourag e their use.  In   India,

Global Technology Adoption 
  
            The auto industry’s success in global market s is dependent on achieving global quality standards and a familiarity
with global technology standards even in the domestic market.  Taxation and other policies should therefore be engineered
to promote higher levels of technology.  When this happens, industry will be forced to offer higher technology levels even
to domestic consumers in order to remain competitive in the market.  Resultantly indi genous component manufacturers
will become familiar with global technologies and global quality expectations.  This will raise the credibility of India made
automobiles in global markets and make them more acceptable to internationa l consumers.  In reality convincing
customers to buy Indian Automobileswith European safety or emission norms will not be credible as long as Indian
domestic market norms are far below those that we are atte mpting to export.  Demonstrat ed familiarity with global
technology standards will be essential to prove Indian auto manufactu rers’ credentials in a global market.  It is suggeste d 
that Government should actively promote early adoption of global technology and standards by providing import duty an d
excise duty concessions on products adapting such technology and standards before they are mandated.  
  
Promoting Real Indigenisation 
  
            An automobile industry of truly global ca pabilities needs bedrock of a str ong and technologically vibrant auto
components sector.  Most manufact urers in India have paid only lip service to  the cause of the component sector.  These
are many segments of the component industry that are truly world class, but many MNC ma nufacturers keep them out on
specious plea of little consequence only in order to promote component im ports from vendors in the mother country.  The
present import tariff regime completely supports such an approach.  Under WTO also obviously nothing much can be done
to promote real indigenisation.  There is however a variety of non-tariff appr oaches that can be taken in this regard.  
Insurance can be used to promote indigensition.  Presently insurance on new cars is levied ad-valorem basis, irrespective of 
what a claim costs an insurance company.  Typically cars w ith higher real indigenisation le vels have lower spare parts
prices.  Therefore, a policy that reward s lower parts prices will work to encour age indigenisation.   Vehicle insurance
charges should be related to the observed cl aim cost brand wise/model wise  or to the price of a basket of spares typically
consumed in insurance claim cases.  In the present situation owners of cars with lower claims ratios (&lower spares prices)
are in effect subsiding those with higher ratios.  Most private sector entrants in the general insurance sector, as a policy, do
not cover such cars with high spare prices.  Only PSU insu rance companies do.  The IRDA has consistently adopted an
anti-consumer stance by not  allowing free pricing of motor car  insurance.  It has allowed insurance companies to cartilise
in the name of regulation.   Free pricing of insurance will ensure that cars with the lowest claim ratios either through higher
indigenisation and lower parts prices or through better safety-related technology  will attract lower insurance.  This will in-
turn encourage both indigenisation and te chnology upgradation.  Another non-tariff measure to aid the development of a 
strong and technologically competent com ponents industry would be measured to  provide rules for type approval o f
components for safety akin to the CE mark used in Europe.  This will ensure that cheap low quality spurious parts do no t
flood the Indians market particularly from China.

Government policy. Since the passenger car  segment of the Indian automotive indus try is the least mature segment and is
plagued by low demand, high fragme ntation and high acquisition cost, the auto policy needs to re-orient and redress the 
passenger cars to focus on the growth of passenger car segment for commercial harnessing  of  the growth drive r

paradigm.  One of the key determinant of demand is acquis ition cost, which is primarily based on sale price and curren t
income levels.  The acquisition cost in I ndia for passenger cars, in terms of months  of median salary required to purchase
the most popular passenger car, is one of the highest in th e world. Moreover,  50% of th e acquisition cost of a passenge r

            The Committee is of the view that the policy maker, while formulating the policy for the passenger car, has to keep
the global trends for passenger car.   The global auto ma rket is mature and competitive.  In the post liberalized an d
globalized economy, only countries which are integrated into a regional econo mic  block (EU, NAFTA, MERCUSOR,
ASEAN) have been able to achieve signi ficant level of exports due  to comparative advantages and regional supply chains
of OEMs, e.g. Brazil (MERCUSOR), la rge domestic market and Mexico (NAF TA), Czech Republic (EU) low labou r
rates.  India will thus find it difficult to create an export market (or even significant level of exports) driven car industry  at
current levels of domestic capability and demand base ma inly because of lower and decreasing domestic protection
available to the industry under WTO, low current technology base, absence of integration with any regional block SAARC
regional block is not happening, moreover, it is too small.   
  
            However, the Committee feels that to ac hieve the vision of making auto se ctor 10% of industrial production,
passenger cars will have to be the key.  The passenger car segment, which currently accounts for only one-third of the auto 
industry output, will have to grow at a rate  of about 16 per cent per annum in real terms, to contribute about 40% of the
auto industry output.  As is the case in other economies, which are using auto industry as a grow th driver (like Malaysia,
Indonesia etc) passenger car industry output has to grow from the present level of Rs.125 billion to Rs.725 billion by 2010
or from the present level of 400,000 units to close to 2.4 million units.  Given the current rate of per capita income growth
and income elasticity of demand 1.6, the market size will grow to 1.5 million by the year 2010.  So as to reach the target of
2.4 million, the industry as well as the Government need to ta ke initiatives to reduce price,  increase quality and provide
better value to the consumer, improve road infrastructure as well as encourage replacement demand. 
  
            The Committee believes that the opportunities for growth are maximum in passenger cars.  Globally, passenger car
segment is the biggest segment of the auto industry acc ounting for over 4 times the volum e of commercial vehicles. 
Passenger car industry tends to be the product and process technology driver for the manufacturing industry.  Passenger car
sector has a significant multiplier impact due to its widespread forward and ba ckward linkages in the economy.  Passenger
car industry has been the driver  for rubber, plastics, telematics, financing and numerous other industries.  In the auto
industry passenger car segment is the segm ent, which has a truly global supply ch ain and world class products that will
enable India develop an industry that is quality and cost competitive.

TWO WHEELERS SEGMENT 
  
            The Committee is pleased to observe that the India has th e second largest two-wheeler market in the world, which 
has enabled the industry players to reap the benefits of econom ies of scale. Two wheelers se gment is the most mature an d
competitive segment in the auto industry, however, exports as a percentage of domestic demand, are still negligible.  The
Japanese have been pioneers of the two- wheeler market.  Currently, all the majo r markets for two-wheelers other than 
India are dominated by Japanese manufacturers or Japane se-affiliated manufacturers.  Chin a, which is the biggest market
for two-wheelers, is completely dominated by Japanese fi rms, either through joint ve ntures or technology transfe r
agreements. 
  
            The Committee believes that in order to achieve the vision of maki ng auto sector 10% of the industrial production,
the two wheeler segment will have to grow to over 13 million units.  This will require product design/quality
improvements along with market push to ensure that two-whee lers remain the most popular vehicle of choice.  The vision 
is achievable given the past growth ra te of 10% in volume terms.  Even if the domestic industry continues to grow a t
historical growth rates, the domestic demand will be over 11 million units by 2010.  The balance is targeted to be me t
through exports i.e. 2 million units per annum by 2010, whic h will have to reach 20% by 2010 if the vision is to be
achieved. 
  
            The Committee recommends    the following strategies to achieve vision target for two-wheeler segment :- 
  
(i)      Improve the current product quality, range  and features to meet internati onal standards in terms of quality, 
emission and product features. 
(ii)    As Indian firms push into global ma rkets, they can no longer depend on in ternational players with whom they
compete, for product and process technology.  Consequently, they will need to develop indigenous
capabilities for world class product and process design.  The most importa nt critical challenge would be to
develop capabilities to design products suited for targ eted export markets rather than find markets which
suit Indian products.  For example, it is imperative to  bridge the technology gap in  terms of reliability an d
consistency in performance which is driven by both design as well as manufacturing excellence. 
(iii)   To achieve the vision target of 13 million units, Indian players will also have to aggressively focus on exports. 
Given that the key markets which already have strong local players, Indian players will need to eithe r
differentiate their product or target new export markets,  like Latin America, Africa etc.  this will require
massive investments by the Indian Players in sa les and marketing along with setting up extensive
distribution and service network. 
(iv)  As majority of countries, including India are becoming environment conscious, Indian players need to invest in
development of clean and fuel efficient two-wheelers. 
  
The Committee further recommends that the Government should facilitate the negotiati on of bilateral agreement 
for allowing free trade between India and some key markets like, Pakistan, Ch ina and Africa etc. (the proposed free
movement with SAARC/ASEAN can give a thrust to exports) and to initiate a Market Development Fund for brand-
building and enabling distribution of Indian products.  Simil arly, the Government needs to facilitate the import o f

COMMERCIAL VEHICLE 
  
            The Committee finds that the key challenges being face d by the commercial vehicle (CV) industry are obsolete
practices and industry structur e for both goods and passenger tran sportation; gap with internat ional levels of safety an d
emission standards of the CVs and poor infrastructure like poor quality roads, limited transs hipment and container points
and other amenities on the highway s.  Reduced margin triggers off a vicious cycle in the CV industry. Vicious cycle is
working against technology upgradation in Indian CV market and the  lower scrappage rates depress new demand and lead
to high cyclicity.  The Indian trucki ng industry is characterized by small flee t owners.  Indeed single-truck owners 
constitute upto 80% of the total truck fl eet and almost 95% of all trucks are ow ned by operators with less than 5 trucks. 
While the global trends highlight that th e commercial vehicle industry is largely localized with over capacity and pressure
on profits.   The current global practice is to use a combina tion of HCVs and LCVs for effi cient freight movement.  While
existing practice in India MCVs are th e backbone of the cargo movement but gl obally a combination of HCVs and LCVs
is used which is more economical, faster, fuel-efficient and causes less traffic congestion.  Moreover, globally the trucking
industry is increasingly getting sophisticated in terms of technology.   
  
            As regards the vision for Commercial Vehicle I ndustry, it is being envisaged that by the year 2010, combined sales
of LCVs, MCVs and HCVs, the total size of CV industry will be about Rs.440 billion.  More important than size, it is
envisioned that the quality of road transport, and hence the technology and quality levels of CVs will improve substantially
in years to come.  The important implic ation is that a large proportion of old di lapidated vehicles on the road would be
replaced with higher capacity, better performing vehicles, wh ich will also translate into a substantial higher value pe r
vehicle. 
  
            The Committee is of the opinion that the achi evement of vision depends on two ke y drivers i.e. growth in deman d
of CVs including buses and growth in average value of the CVs.   The average value can be increased with a shift towards
higher tonnage vehicles and improvement of technology of all vehicl es.  With the average va lue increase, the issue o f
lower efficiency of MCVs in goods tran sportation and poor customer service le vels in passenger transportation can be
addressed.  The Committee is of the view that unless a co mprehensive set of recommendations and action plan, which
addresses all these root causes  can be addressed simultaneously, the vision a nd modernization of the Indian transportation
industry will not be achieved. This is principally because these root causes are inter-related and action on only one of them 
could actually prove counter-productive.   Therefore, the solution lies not only in selectively mandating improve d
technology standards but simultaneously promoting scrappage so that the age of vehicle in the CV Park is always kep t
young.  The mandating of scrappage may be  implemented for a brief window only till such time as the average age of CV
park is sufficiently young and a proper I&C system is fully in place. Therefor e, the Committee recommends that the
Government should take up a high profile visionary objective of renewing and modern izing the Indian CV park i.e. the
technology and age profile as the first step in taking the transportation industry.  Such an objective would be in consonance
with the ambitious plans to develop a modern East-West and Nort h-South expressways and th e plans for the ‘Golden 
Quadilateral’.

Phasing out of old vehicles: 
  
The Committee observes  that various  states are adopting differ ent norms of phasing out of commercial vehicles. 
While Delhi and Maharastra follow the norms of 5 t o 8 years, other states like West Bengal & Tamil Nadu do not have
any norm for phasing out of vehicles.  Therefore, the Committe e recommends that in order to  maintain required standards
of performance, safety and emissions, a uniform national policy needs to be defined for phasing out of old vehicles. 
Encouraging Gaseous fuels: 
The Committee is of the view that Policy level decisi on is required in regard to phase wise programme o f
implementation in cities requiring immedia te attention, providing adequate in frastructure for making gaseous fuel
available, rational fuel pricing for protecting operational cost of customers. Current relative Vehicle Running cost ( e.g. o f
RTV) on Diesel is Rs. 2.00/km, on CNG is Rs. 1.68/km while on LPG the cost is Rs. 3.42/km.  Financial incentives to
customers for replacing old vehicles i.e. sale tax incentives or cheaper financing options may be provided.  
Encouraging Light Commercial Vehicles as viable Public Transport Medium: 
Public Transport to vastly spread huge rural population is  provided either by State run Buses or private commercial
vehicles of either 5+1 or 9+1 category,  the current vehicle mix is not able to  reach out to interi ors on account of non
viability or run overloaded creating safety  problems. Therefore, the Committee feels that to provide more viable and safe r
Public Transport system, Passenger LCV’s are proving to be a better sustainable option. 
  
A National Level Policy should  encourage Passenge r LCV’s supplementing public  transport system by 
streamlining uniform viable per seat passenger tax, irrespective of the cate gories and encouraging permits on passenge r
load economics basis.  This would  lead to stoppage of unsafe and illegal usages of vehicles; better revenues to the states;
efficient and safer transport facility to rural public.  
Reducing Vehicle Certification Time: 
The Committee was apprised that the certifying agencies ar e taking undue time i.e. 90 to 150 days. Therefore, the
Committee recommends that  with fast ch anging technology and customer requireme nts, adequate resources need to be
added to these agencies so that vehicle certification time is reduced considerably, desirably between 15 to 20 days.  
Shared R&D facility for the Indian Auto Industry: 
The Indian manufacturers have to rely  on foreign R&D experts and facilities to cope with fast changing needs, no
central pool of R&D is available with in the country from where the resource s can be drawn by the industry, on share d
basis, in order to create global competence. Therefore, the Committee recommends that  a central R&D facility needs to be
created within the country, with Governme nt’s assistance.  Competence of intern ational quality and efficiency should be
made available here, to the Indian Auto manufacturers at reasonable prices.

            The Committee observes that the auto industry’s success in global markets is dependent on achieving global quality
standards and a familiarity with global technology standards even in the domestic market.  Taxation and other policies
should therefore be engineered to promote higher levels of technology.  When this happens, industry will be forced to offer

the credibility of India made automobiles in global markets a nd make them more acceptable to international consumers.

long as Indian domestic market norms are far below those that we are attempting to export.  Demonstrated familiarity with
global technology standards will be  essential to prove Indian auto manufacturers’ credentials  in a global market.  The 
Committee recommends  that the Government should activel y promote early adoption of global technology and standards
by providing import duty and excise duty c oncessions on products adapting such tech nology and standards before they are
mandated. 
  
Promotion of electric vehicles:

efforts to promote electricity vehicles.  The Committee is of  the view that the EV tec hnology is viable with recen t
advancements in electronics a nd batteries and is the long term solution to reduce pollution in cities and by using natural
