PART-I 
I. Introductory 
India’s urban landscape is changing rapidly, with existing cities becoming denser 
and new suburban and peri-urban centres expanding at an unprecedented pace. The 
urban population has grown from 25.9 million in 1901 to 377 million in 2011 and is 
expected to reach nearly 594 million by 2036. It is projected that by 2047, nearly 50% of 
India’s population will be living in urban areas and cities may contribute close to 80% of 
the country’s GDP. The rising share of urban population and increasing contribution of 
cities to GDP clearly show that cities are emerging as key drivers of economic growth. 
2. However, this rapid urbanisation brings serious challenges. Existing infrastructure 
is under strain and often inadequate to meet growing demands for affordable housing, 
drinking water, sanitation, waste management, drainage and efficient public transport. At 
the same time, new urban areas require planned and sustained investment to ensure 
balanced and sustainable development. The real challenge before the Government is to 
transform cities into liveable, inclusive and sustainable growth engines while meeting 
the basic needs and expectations of a persistently expanding urban population. With 
these realities in focus, the Government plans its urban policies and schemes and 
supports them through financial allocations. 
3. Urban development is primarily a State subject under Article 243W of the 
Constitution and the Constitution (Seventy-Fourth) Amendment Act, 1992 entrusts 
Urban Local Bodies (ULBs) with functions listed in the Twelfth Schedule, as devolved by 
the respective State Governments. Within this framework, the Ministry of Housing and 
Urban Affairs (MoHUA) plays a catalytic and supportive role by providing policy 
guidance, financial assistance and programme support through its Central Sector and 
Centrally Sponsored Schemes such as Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana-Urban (PMAY-U), 
Atal Mission for Rejuvenation and Urban Transformation (AMRUT), Swachh Bharat 
Mission – Urban (SBM-U), Prime Minister’s Street Vendors’ Atmanirbhar Nidhi, PM e-
Bus Seva, Mass Rapid Transit System and the Urban Challenge Fund, etc.  
4. Achieving the vision of Viksit Bharat calls for close coordination between the 
Centre and the States in a spirit of cooperative federalism, where the Centre provides 
overall policy direction and financial support, while the States and Urban Local Bodies 
(ULBs) are responsible for implementation and innovation at the ground level. In this 

2 
 
framework, the Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs functions as the apex authority of 
the Government of India at the national level for housing and urban development. It 
formulates policies, sponsors and supports programmes, coordinates with various 
Central Ministries, State Governments and other nodal agencies, and monitors 
initiatives relating to housing and urban affairs across the country, thereby ensuring a 
cohesive and integrated approach to urban development.

Vision of the Ministry 
8. The Secretary, MoHUA during the Sitting of the Committee held on 23.02.2026 
stated the vision of the Ministry as under: 
“I propose to briefly acquaint the hon. Committee with our vision and plans for 
planned urbanisation during the year 2026 and the years thereafter. India, as we 
are all aware, is experiencing rapid urbanisation. There has been a dramatic 
increase in the number of large cities. As many as 53 cities in India now have a 
million-plus population, according to Census 2011. These numbers currently 
show a significant rise. We now have almost 62 to 63 such cities. 
While rapid urbanisation brings new opportunities for growth, it also poses 
several challenges. Comprehensive development of the physical, institutional, 
social and economic infrastructure is required to sustain this urban growth. The 
transition has not been accompanied by a commensurate increase in the supply 
of basic urban services, which is a challenge that we are collectively facing. The 
challenge of urbanisation in India is to ensure that service delivery occurs at 
enhanced minimum standards. As the hon. Committee is aware, urban 
development is a State subject, and the Government of India plays a catalytic 
role by providing schematic and programmatic support through various schemes. 
The critical issues of shortages and deficits in funding for urban infrastructure 
have been addressed with an average annual budget allocation between the 
years 2014 and 2024, which has increased by more than three times compared 
to the previous ten-year period. There has been consistent growth in budgetary 
allocations even after 2024. The allocation of the Ministry for the Budget 
Estimates 2026-2027 is Rs. 85,522 crore, comprising of Rs. 50,714 crore under 
the Revenue Head and Rs 34,808 crore under the Capital Section. 
During the Budget of 2026-2027, focus has once again been placed on urban 
development, as it is one of the major items of expenditure. Announcements 
pertaining to MoHUA made in this year's Budget Speech of the hon. Finance 
Minister include a focus on developing infrastructure in cities with over 5 lakh 
population, amplifying the potential of cities to deliver the economic power of 
agglomerations by mapping city economic regions based on their specific growth 
drivers for implementing their plans through a challenge mode, establishing a 
reform cum results-based financing mechanism and encouraging the issuance of 
municipal bonds of higher value for large cities, in addition to the ongoing 
scheme under the AMRUT.” 
9. To a written query as to whether the Ministry has prepared or proposes to 
prepare a unified urban strategy aligned with the vision of Viksit Bharat 2047 that

following reply: 
“A corpus of ₹5,000 crore has been created under a Sub-Scheme of Urban

Scheme will cover all ULBs in Northeastern and Hilly States/UTs and smaller 
ULBs (with population below 1,00,000) in other States/UTs including Tier-II and 
Tier-III cities, enabling them to raise resources from the market and participate 
under the Urban Challenge Fund.” 
 
10. Referring to the High-Powered Expert Committee on Urban Infrastructure 
(HPEC) constituted in 2008 to estimate investment requirements for urban 
infrastructure, and it submitted its report in 2011 projecting needs up to 2031, the 
Ministry were asked whether the Government has constituted any similar High-Level 
Committee or Expert Group to assess urban infrastructure requirements and 
corresponding investment projections beyond 2030/2031, The reply received is as 
under: 
“...The High Powered Expert Committee (HPEC) in ‘Report for Estimating 
the Investment Requirements for Urban Infrastructure Services’ (2011) has 
estimated that this share was around 62-63% per cent in 2009-10 and 
further projected to increase to 75% by 2030. Since then, no such 
Committee is set up by the Government.  As per the report 'Cities as 
Engines of Growth’ by NITI Aayog &ADB (2022), each percentage point 
increase in a district’s urban population share is associated with a 2.7% 
increase in district GDP. Therefore, the cities accompanied by 
industrialization are the main catalyst to derive the economic growth in 
India.”

(ii) Pradhan Mantri Aways Yojana - Urban (PMAY-U) and PMAY(U) 2.0 
 
45. In pursuance of Government’s vision of facilitating Housing for All, Ministry of 
Housing and Urban Affairs (MoHUA) is implementing Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana- 
Urban (PMAY-U) – ‘Housing for All’ Mission since 25.06.2015 for providing all weather 
pucca houses to all eligible urban beneficiaries. Mission Period was up to 31.03.2022, 
which was extended up to 31.12.2025 for completion of already sanctioned houses 
without changing funding pattern and implementation methodology of the scheme 
except CLSS vertical. During the extended phase of PMAY-U, no new houses were to 
be sanctioned. New houses will only be sanctioned under PMAY-U 2.0 scheme. The 
scheme is implemented through four verticals: Beneficiary-led individual house 
construction or enhancement (BLC), Affordable Housing in Partnership with public or 
private sector (AHP), In-situ Slum Redevelopment (ISSR) and Credit Linked Subsidy 
Scheme (CLSS). The Mission is implemented as a Centrally Sponsored Scheme 
(CSS) except the CLSS vertical of PMAY-U which is implemented as a Central Sector 
Scheme.  
46. Based on the learnings from the experiences of implementation of PMAY-U, 
MoHUA has revamped the scheme and launched PMAY-U 2.0 ‘Housing for All’ Mission 
with effect from 01.09.2024 for implementation in urban areas across the country to 
support 1 crore additional eligible beneficiaries in next five years. PMAY-U 2.0 is 
implemented through following four verticals: Beneficiary Led Construction (BLC), 
Affordable Housing in Partnership (AHP), Affordable Rental Housing (ARH) and Interest 
Subsidy Scheme (ISS).

(iii) Affordable Rental Housing Complex (ARHC) 
 
56. MoHUA had initiated Affordable Rental Housing Complexes (ARHCs) scheme in 
July 2020, as a sub-scheme of PMAY-U to provide dignified affordable rental housing for 
urban migrants/ poor, close to their workplace. Construction of a mix of Single Bedroom 
/Double Bedroom with living area, kitchen, toilet and bathroom; and Dormitory Beds 
were envisaged under the ARHC scheme. Further, in order to carry forward this agenda, 
under PMAY-U 2.0, a new dedicated vertical of Affordable Rental Housing (ARH) has 
been implemented for eligible EWS/LIG beneficiaries including urban migrants/ 
homeless/ destitute/ industrial workers/ working women/construction workers/, urban 
poor (street vendors, rickshaw pullers, other service providers etc.), migrants working 
with market/trade associations, educational/health institutions, hospitality sector, 
/contractual employees/amongst others. This vertical will be implemented through two 
models viz. : 
Model-1: Utilizing existing Government funded vacant houses in cities by converting 
them into ARHCs under Public Private Partnership (PPP) mode or by public agencies 
for 25 years. 
Model-2: Construction, Operation and Maintenance of Affordable Rental Housing 
Complexes by Public/ Private Entities on their own available vacant land for 25 years.

fixation. The responsibility for determining, notifying, and revising rent levels

scheme guidelines and local policy framework.” 
 
64. Under the ARHC guidelines, in addition to fixation of rents, a range of other 
incentives and benefits have been provided by both the Central Government and the 
States/UTs and ULBs to encourage development of rental housing for urban migrants. 
The Central Government offers concessional project finance through the Affordable 
Housing Fund (AHF) and Priority Sector Lending (PSL), exemption from Income Tax 
and GST on profits earned from ARHC projects and Technology Innovation Grants (TIG) 
to promote the use of modern and cost-effective construction technologies. States/UTs 
and ULBs support ARHC projects by allowing changes in land-use permissions where 
required, providing up to 50 per cent additional FAR/FSI free of cost, ensuring single-
window approval of projects within 30 days, extending trunk infrastructure up to the 
project site and charging municipal fees at the same rates as residential properties. 
These measures are aimed at reducing project costs, speeding up approvals, and 
improving the financial viability of ARHC projects.  
65. In this regard, when asked about whether Income Tax and GST exemptions 
provided under ARHC guidelines have been operationalised uniformly across projects in 

35 
 
order to ensure that these benefits reduce rents for tenants, the Ministry replied as 
under: - 
“Public and private entities avail the proposed incentives and benefits, 
including applicable Income Tax and GST exemptions, under both models 
of the Affordable Rental Housing Complexes (ARHCs) through the 
concerned Government agencies. The Ministry of Housing and Urban 
Affairs (MoHUA) does not directly administer or disburse such tax-related 
incentives on a project-wise basis.  However, we are taking up with 
Department of Revenue, Ministry of Finance for such benefits.” 
 
66. The Ministry, when asked whether all States and UTs have effectively 
implemented required policy measures such as land-use permission changes and 
provision of 50 per cent additional FAR/FSI, and how many ARHC projects have 
benefited from these relaxations, replied as follows: - 
“Land use regulation, building permissions and fixation of FAR/FSI fall 
within the jurisdiction of the respective State Governments/UT 
administrations and Urban Local Bodies. Under the Affordable Rental 
Housing Complexes (ARHCs), States/UTs have been advised to facilitate 
necessary policy measures, including suitable land-use changes, provision 
of additional FAR/FSI (up to 50 per cent free of cost), and other local 
incentives such as fee exemptions, to enhance project viability. 
Implementation of these measures varies across States/UTs depending 
upon their respective urban development regulations, local planning 
frameworks and policy notifications. The Ministry does not centrally enforce 
or uniformly operationalise such relaxations, as these are governed by 
State/UT-specific statutes and building bye-laws. There are variation across 
States in adoption of such policy measures.  
Further, project-level details regarding the extent to which additional 
FAR/FSI or land-use relaxations have been availed are maintained by the 
concerned State/UT implementing agencies and Urban Local Bodies.”

timely provision of trunk infrastructure has a direct bearing on expeditious 
project completion, operationalisation of ARHCs, and achievement of 
optimal occupancy levels. Project-level details regarding infrastructure

“As per the operational guidelines of ARHCs, Municipal services such as

operational cost burden on concessionaires/entities, thereby supporting 
financial viability of projects and enabling affordable rental levels for urban 
migrant/poor.  However, the levy and notification of municipal charges fall 
within the jurisdiction of the respective States/UTs and Urban Local Bodies 
(ULBs). This is being pursued with all the States.”  
 
69. The Committee have been apprised that under the ARHC scheme, a Technology 
Innovation Grant (TIG) is provided to incentivise the adoption of innovative, sustainable, 
green and disaster-resilient construction technologies and materials. The objective of 
TIG is to promote faster project execution, sustainable, cost efficiency, improved 
structural quality and durability, and enhanced living conditions for beneficiaries. The 
grant encourages the use of modern construction systems such as precast 
components, monolithic concrete technologies, light-gauge steel framing, energy-
efficient building materials, and other certified alternative technologies that reduce 
construction timelines while ensuring safety and environmental sustainability.  
70. Further, the Committee have been apprised that the implementation and uptake 
of ARHC projects across States/UTs are influenced by multiple contextual and 
institutional factors. These include availability of suitable encumbrance-free land parcels 
in proximity to industrial or employment hubs; assessed demand for rental housing 
among urban migrants and economically weaker sections; timely grant of statutory 
approvals and building permissions; availability and readiness of trunk infrastructure 
such as water supply, sewerage, drainage, electricity and road connectivity; and overall 
financial viability of projects in the local rental market context. Further, many of 
incentives are to be provided by States/UTs and Municipal authorities. 
Operationalization of such incentives vary across States.

(vii) Prime Minister Street Vendor’s AtmaNirbhar Nidhi (PM SVANidhi) Scheme 
103. Prime Minister Street Vendor’s Atma Nirbhar Nidhi (PM SVANidhi) Scheme was 
launched as a Central Sector Scheme on 01.6.2020 to facilitate collateral free working 
capital loan to street vendors to restart their businesses who were affected by the 
pandemic. As per the submission made by the Ministry, initially the scheme was 
launched for period upto March, 2022. The lending under the PM SVANidhi Scheme 
was extended till December, 2024 with credit guarantee and interest subsidy claims on 
all loans to be paid till March, 2028. Subsequently, the Union Cabinet, on 27th August 
2025, approved the restructuring and extension of the PM SVANidhi scheme till March, 

55 
 
2030. Liabilities under the components like credit guarantee, interest subsidy for loans 
and incentives for digital transactions etc. will be serviced until March 31, 2033 while 
credit guarantee claims for credit card will be serviced until March 31, 2035. The Union 
Cabinet approved a total outlay of ₹7,332 crore for FY 2025-26 to FY 2034-35 for the 
restructured scheme. Further, implementation and monitoring of the scheme is the joint 
responsibility of Ministry of Housing & Urban Affairs (MoHUA) and Department of 
Financial Services (DFS). 
104. The Committee have been apprised that the objective of the scheme is to 
address the vulnerabilities of street vendors through a multi-pronged approach. This 
includes:  
i. Access to formal credit to street vendors (SV); 
ii. Digital adoption among SVs and build their credit history; 
iii. Extend scheme benefits to census towns, peri-urban areas etc. in a graded 
manner; 
iv. Social security to SV families through convergence with central schemes; 
v. Capacity building of SVs with a focus on entrepreneurship, financial literacy, 
digital skills, marketing and hygiene & food safety with FSSAI; 
vi. Develop Street Food Hubs meeting global standards that promote local 
culinary heritage in high tourist footfall areas.

133. The Secretary, MoHUA during the Sitting of the Committee held on 23.02.2026, 
explained the Urban Challenge Fund (UCF) to the Committee as given below: 
“Hon. Finance Minister in her speech said that, “Cities to implement their plans in 
a challenge mode with a reform cum results-based financing mechanism.” It was 
hinting at the Urban Challenge Fund. Regarding the performance linked urban 
financing mechanism as hon. Committee is aware, for the past few years, we 
have been emphasising on reform. This time the reform and the performance is 
also getting underlined by the bankability of it, so that the private capital can also 
start assessing it and start evaluating it. The last two points are again significant, 
that the central transfers are to shift from scheme based grants to performance 
linked urban block grants. The cities should now start looking at balance sheets 
rather than utilisation certificates. We have to look at performance and not at 
what we have expended and therefore certify. 
Urban Challenge Fund again is a shift in thinking. The shift in thinking is that 
development cannot happen without the participation of people and capital 
markets. So far, whatever money has gone into the urban sector has been 
budgetary money and we are aware that budgetary money has a certain 
limitation. But the capital markets, people have much more wealth than in 
occasions Governments have. So how do we tap into the capital markets is the 
methodology of the Urban Challenge Fund which also has a very interesting 
feature. I will request my colleague to speak on that subsequently. 
As you rightly mentioned, Central Government will support to the extent of 25 per 
cent any viable bankable project built around three broad pillars, which is city as 
economic growth engines for water storage, sanitation and cities as growth hubs. 
The State Government or the local entity is supposed to contribute 25 per cent. 
The bankability comes in 50 per cent. That means either banks are giving, capital 
markets are giving or maybe you can come out with some innovative financial 
instruments. 
The idea is that, as we all know today, the Indian capital markets, the Bombay 
and other stock exchanges roughly have around four and a half trillion dollars of 
money. That is not the money that is available with the Central Government nor 
the State Government. So, this is a methodology of attracting that money to flow 
in the direction of urban areas by providing them investable areas. 

70 
 
Once this process rolls, it is our hope, with your support, we will be able to 
persuade Government to give us more and more money. As the Committee is 
aware, at this juncture I would like to again rephrase. There is a study that was 
carried out some time back by the World Bank which said that India's urban 
sector requires about 838 billion US dollars. I believe, sir, it is close to one trillion 
US dollars. 
Now that is money that we have to attract through these innovative financial 
solutions. That is where Urban Challenge Fund comes in as a very different 
approach to attracting private capital towards both building of infrastructure, 
managing of urban services, managing of urban areas, managing of urban-like 
areas, spaces and many other things. In order to ensure that there is 
geographical equity, government has also sanctioned a Rs.5,000 crore.”

PART-II 
OBSERVATIONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS 
 
Recommendation No. 1 
Need for Proper and Realistic Budgetary Planning 
 
The Committee note that while the Total Central Government Outlay 
has increased steadily from ₹39,44,909 crore in 2022-23 to ₹53,47,315 crore 
in 2026-27, the share of the Ministry has not grown proportionately. The 
Ministry’s share, which stood at 1.94% in 2022-23 and 1.91% in 2025-26, has 
declined to 1.60% in 2026-27 which is the lowest in the five-year period. 
Further, against the projected outlay of ₹97,644.51 crore for 2026-27, the 
approved Budget Estimate is ₹85,522.39 crore, reflecting a reduction of 
₹12,122.12 crore (12.41%). The Committee also note that such reductions 
between projected outlay and approved Budget Estimates have been 
persistent over the years i.e. 22.82% in 2022-23, 11.52% in 2023-24 and 
17.25% in 2024-25, with the only exception being the year 2025-26.  
 
The Committee also note that despite increasing urbanisation and 
rising demand for housing, water supply, sanitation and urban transport, 
the Ministry’s relative share in the Central Budget has declined and its 
projections are routinely curtailed at the approval stage except in FY 2025-
26. The Committee feel that such persistent gaps between projected outlay 
and Budgetary allocation are linked to the actual utilisation trends, 
highlighting a systemic mismatch between projection and implementation 
capacity. With the need to address the issues of such stark gaps between 
the Ministry’s budgetary projections and actual utilisation, the Committee 
recommend the Ministry : 
(i) to adopt a more realistic and evidence-based forecasting mechanism 
aligned with actual implementation capacity at the State level;  
(ii) to formulate and present a clear and time-bound roadmap for 
progressively enhancing Ministry’s share in the Total Central Outlay, 

79 
 
commensurate with the expanding urban infrastructure needs and 
services, delivery obligations aligned with the vision of ‘Viksit 
Bharat’ in 2047; and 
(iii) to institute robust expenditure monitoring and mid-year review 
mechanisms to ensure timely utilization of allocated funds under key 
schemes and other urban infrastructure programmes, enabling 
corrective action within the same FY to prevent persistent 
underutilization. 
 
Recommendation No. 2 
Need for Timely Utilisation of Funds  
 
The Committee note that the gap between Budget Estimates (BE), 
Revised Estimates (RE) and Actual Expenditure (AE) of the Ministry has 
widened significantly in recent years. While utilisation remained close to 
RE in 2022-23 and 2023-24, the position deteriorated in 2024-25 and 2025-
26. In 2025-26, the BE of ₹96,777 crore was sharply reduced at RE stage to 
₹57,203.78 crore (a reduction of nearly 40%). The actual expenditure as on 
20.02.2026 stands at ₹40,967.62 crore, which is only 71.62% of RE implying 
that nearly 28.38% of the revised allocation is to be spent in the last 39 
days of the financial year. Scheme-wise analysis shows uneven 
performance. While AMRUT and PMAY (Urban) show utilisation above 79–
80% of RE, schemes such as PM-eBus Sewa (17.87%), CITIIS 2.0 (0.37%), 
PM SVANidhi (60.99%), DJAY (45.60%) and PHE (40.29%) show slow 
progress. Further, unspent balances under various schemes amount to 
₹5,806.27 crore, with substantial balances under Smart Cities Mission 
(₹2,012.64 crore), SBM (₹1,238.90 crore) and AMRUT (₹1,213.01 crore). The 
amount surrendered has also risen sharply from ₹1,157.31 crore in 2022-23 
to ₹32,291.34 crore in 2024-25 indicating serious issues in expenditure 
planning and utilisation. 
The Committee take note of the Ministry’s submission that 
expenditure depends substantially on the spending capacity of States 
under the SNA-SPARSH mechanism, which requires States to incur 

80 
 
expenditure before the Central share is released. The Ministry has also 
cited delays in approval of major schemes such as the National Urban 
Digital Mission (NUDM), Urban Challenge Fund and the Scheme for 
Industrial Housing, involving an outlay of ₹13,750 crore, as factors 
affecting utilisation. While these constraints are acknowledged, the 
Committee are of the considered view that repeated large-scale reductions 
at the RE stage, uneven scheme performance and growing unspent 
balances point to deeper issues in budgeting realism, inter-governmental 
coordination and monitoring. The Committee, therefore, recommend that 
the Ministry: 
(i) to undertake a thorough review of Budgeting exercise so as to better 
align Budgetary Estimate, Revised Estimate and Actual Expenditure;  
(ii) to address procedural bottlenecks under fund flow mechanisms 
such as SNA-SPARSH;  
(iii) to fix scheme-wise spending targets at regular intervals, ideally 
every quarter, and review them closely to ensure timely use of funds; 
(iv) to strengthen institutional capacity of States and Urban Local Bodies 
(ULBs) for improved fund absorption; and  
(v) to ensure that expenditure is evenly paced throughout the year 
avoiding year-end concentration, so as to uphold both fiscal 
prudence and implementation quality.

(Urban)

(PMAY-U) has achieved significant scale in terms of sanctions (122.22 lakh

for 2025–26 was drastically reduced at the Revised Estimate stage from 
₹23,294 crore to ₹7,800 crore, and actual expenditure as on 05.01.2026 is

only ₹3,874.84 crore, reflecting slow fund utilisation. Although the Ministry

weaknesses in expenditure planning and implementation pace. 
The State-wise data also reveal uneven physical and financial

The Committee, therefore, recommend the Ministry that: 
(i) Budget Estimates under the scheme be projected more realistically 
so as to avoid sharp reductions at the Revised Estimate stage and 
low actual expenditure at the end of the financial year; 
(ii) a strengthened review and coordination mechanism be put in place 
with periodic progress assessment to ensure that central assistance 
is translated into completed dwelling units within the stipulated 
timeframe; and 
(iii) the adequacy of existing central financial assistance per dwelling 
unit be reviewed and suitably enhanced, wherever necessary, 
keeping in view rising construction costs, so that beneficiaries are 
not burdened and projects are completed in a timely and sustainable 
manner.

Recommendation No. 5 
Ensuring Wider and Balanced Implementation of ARHC across States 
 
Affordable Rental Housing Complex (ARHC) scheme was introduced 
as a sub-scheme of PMAY (U) in July, 2020 and since September, 2024 is 
being implemented as a vertical under PMAY(U) 2.0 through two models i.e. 
Model-I, which envisages conversion of existing government-funded 
vacant houses into rental housing through PPP or public agencies for 25 
years; and Model-II, which provides for construction, operation and 
maintenance of new rental housing units by public or private entities on 
their own land for 25 years. However, progress under both the models has 
been uneven. Under Model-I, against 83,534 vacant houses identified, only 
5,783 units have been converted into ARHCs, while 7,413 units are under 
process, leaving a very large stock of vacant houses unutilised. States/UTs 
such as Delhi and Maharashtra, despite having 29,112 and 32,345 vacant 
houses respectively, have reported no conversion under the scheme. The 
Committee further note that out of 5,783 houses converted under Model-I in 
eight cities, 4,605 units have been occupied by beneficiaries so far, while 
1,178 units remain vacant. Under Model-II, although 83,298 units have been 
approved nationwide, only 36,450 units have been completed and occupied 

84 
 
so far, with the overwhelming share concentrated at Sriperumbudur, Hosur 
and Chennai in Tamil Nadu. Many approved projects in other States remain 
at early stages of completion. This indicates slow progress, limited 
geographical spread and inadequate reporting on actual utilisation of 
assets created. 
The Committee are of the view that the objectives of ARHC, which 
aims to provide dignified, affordable rental housing to urban migrants, poor 
and other vulnerable groups close to their workplaces, cannot be fully 
realised unless vacant houses are swiftly converted and newly approved 
units are completed and occupied in a time-bound manner. The Committee, 
therefore, recommend to the Ministry:  
(i) to prepare a time-bound State/UT-wise action plan for conversion of 
vacant houses under Model-I, particularly in States/UTs with large 
inventories such as Delhi and Maharashtra and actively resolve 
administrative or coordination issues hindering implementation; 
(ii) to establish a system to oversee and ensure that the houses lying 
vacant in various States/UTs which are being considered under 
Model-I of AHRC are first appropriately retrofitted, repaired and 
upgraded, wherever necessary, to make them habitable; 
(iii) to set strict timelines for completion and operationalisation of 
approved units under Model-II, with periodic review of construction 
and occupancy status; 
(iv) to strengthen monitoring and coordination with States/UTs and 
private partners so that both conversion of existing vacant houses 
and construction of new rental units translate into functional, fully 
occupied housing complexes serving the intended beneficiaries; and 
(v) to undertake a comprehensive national assessment of demand for 
affordable rental housing for urban migrants and to fix clear 
State/UT-wise targets for the number of ARHC dwelling units.

Recommendation No. 6 
Need for affordable Rent Fixation and Operationalisation of Incentives 
under ARHC 
 
The Committee note that under the Affordable Rental Housing 
Complexes (ARHC) scheme, Dwelling Units may consist of Single/Double 
Bedroom units (30/60 sqm) and Dormitory beds (10 sqm), with basic 
facilities such as kitchen, toilet and bathroom as prescribed in the 
operational guidelines. Further, as per the guidelines, rent under Model–I 
which permits conversion of existing government-funded vacant houses, is 
to be fixed by the local authority based on a local survey conducted prior 
to issuance of the Request for Proposal (RFP). Under Model–II which 
allows construction, operation and maintenance by public/private entities, 
the initial affordable rent is to be fixed by the concerned entity based on a 
local survey. Rent is permitted to be enhanced biennially by 8%, subject to 
a maximum cumulative increase of 20% over five years. 
The guidelines further provide for various fiscal and non-fiscal 
incentives to improve project viability and ensure affordability. These 
include concessional project finance, Income Tax and GST exemptions, 
Technology Innovation Grant (TIG), additional FAR/FSI up to 50% free of 
cost, land-use relaxations, single-window approvals, levy of municipal 
charges at par with residential properties and provision of trunk 
infrastructure up to the project site by States/UTs without additional cost. 
The Committee observe that rent fixation is entirely decentralised 
and the Ministry does not prescribe uniform rent rates nor maintain 
centralized data regarding rent levels across cities. Similarly, 
implementation of incentives such as additional FAR/FSI, land-use 
permissions, municipal charge parity and trunk infrastructure provision 
varies across States/UTs depending upon local regulations and policy 
notifications. With regard to fiscal incentives, including Income Tax and 
GST exemptions, the Committee are apprised that the Ministry does not 
directly administer these benefits project-wise and has indicated that the 
matter is being taken up with the concerned authorities. 

86 
 
The Committee are of the view that while flexibility to States/UTs is 
necessary given that land and housing are State subjects, the core intent of 
the Scheme to provide “affordable” rental housing to the urban migrants 
must not get sidelined. The Committee are also of the view that the 
absence of a broad guiding framework and centralized monitoring of rents 
may lead to variations that affect affordability for migrant workers. The 
Committee further observe that uneven implementation of incentives such 
as additional FAR/FSI, trunk infrastructure, tax exemptions and municipal 
charge parity may adversely impact project viability and could ultimately 
result in higher rents. 
In view of the above, the Committee recommend the Ministry : 
(i) to assess feasibility of issuing indicative rent benchmarks to ensure 
‘affordability’ for urban migrants while retaining flexibility for local 
conditions and to develop a centralized system to capture State-wise 
data on rent fixation, revisions, occupancy levels and incentives 
availed under both models of ARHC; 
(ii) to ensure that ARHC projects are prioritised in locations near major 
employment hubs and industrial areas to ensure higher occupancy 
and reduced commuting burden on beneficiaries; 
(iii) to direct States/UTs to strictly implement provisions relating to free 
additional FAR/FSI, land-use relaxations, residential-rate municipal 
charges and timely provision of trunk infrastructure as mandated in 
the operational guidelines; and 
(iv) to actively pursue operationalisation of Income Tax and GST 
exemptions and ensure that such fiscal benefits translate into 
affordable rents for tenants.

Strengthening Financial Planning, Monitoring and Timely Completion under 
AMRUT 2.0 
 
The Committee note that although the Budget Estimates for AMRUT 
2.0 were kept at ₹8,000 crore in 2023-24 and 2024-25 and enhanced to

₹10,000 crore in 2025–26, the Revised Estimates were scaled down in

Need for Adequate Financial Allocation and Expedited Remediation of 
Legacy Dumpsites under Swachh Bharat Mission (Urban) 2.0 
 
The Committee note that under the Swachh Bharat Mission (Urban) 
2.0, notable progress has been reported in door-to-door collection (96.54%

On the financial front, there is a consistent gap between the

any, will be projected at the Revised Estimates/Supplementary Demands 
stage, the Committee feel that such reduced allocations in the final phase 
of the Mission may constrain implementation and delay completion of 
pending works. 
At the same time, in respect of legacy dumpsites, out of 2,510.78 lakh 
metric tonnes (LMT) of identified waste, only 1,568.01 LMT or about 62.5% 
has been remediated and 8,587.67 acres reclaimed, leaving 942.77 LMT yet 
to be treated and 6,671.03 acres of land to be reclaimed. While some 
States/UTs have achieved full remediation, large quantities of waste remain 
pending in States such as Maharashtra, Karnataka, West Bengal, 
Rajasthan, Delhi and Tamil Nadu. The pace of remediation is uneven and 

89 
 
substantial work remains to be completed within a limited timeframe. The 
Committee note that the Ministry has launched the Dumpsite Remediation 
Accelerator Programme (DRAP) on 8th November, 2025, with the aim of 
completing remediation of all dumpsites by 31st March, 2027. 
As the Mission is in its final phase with the goal of making all cities 
“Garbage Free” by 2026, the Committee emphasise that adequate financial 
support and faster remediation of legacy dumpsites are essential for 
achieving this objective. Any delay in addressing funding gaps and 
pending waste treatment may hinder timely completion of the Mission. The 
Committee, therefore, recommend to the Ministry that : 
(i) Adequate and realistic assessment of funds should be made at the 
Budget Estimate stage itself in line with projected requirements and 
physical targets and any drastic reductions at the Revised Estimate 
stage should be avoided; 
(ii) A clear, State/UT-wise time-bound action plan with quarterly targets 
should be prepared to clear the remaining legacy waste under the 
DRAP and special monitoring and support should be provided to 
States where large quantities of waste are still pending;  
(iii) it should be ensured that within two years every ULB has ‘waste 
treatment plants’ (WTPs) with a capacity proportionate to the amount 
of waste generated currently while also envisaging future needs, to 
avoid growth of new dumpsites and landfills; and   
(iv) greater emphasis should be put on sustainable and scientific 
methods such as waste-to-energy, bio-mining, composting and 
material recovery, so that legacy waste is properly processed rather 
than merely shifted. States with large pending quantities should be 
encouraged to adopt value-recovery models to generate energy, 
reduce landfill burden and ensure productive use of reclaimed land.

Recommendation No. 10 
Assessment of ULB’s vendor carrying capacity for PM SVANidhi 
 
The Committee note that the Pradhan Mantri Street Vendors 
Atmanirbhar Nidhi (PM SVANidhi) was launched as a positive intervention 
by the Central Government for members of the lowest strata of urban 
society, namely, the street vendors who were hit hard by the Covid 
Pandemic. One of the tacit achievements of the Scheme was the 
implementation of the Street Vendors Act, 2014 across States/UTs by 
intertwining the requirements of the Scheme with the provisions of the Act. 
Continuing the momentum created by PM SVANidhi in terms of bringing 
street vendors into the fold of formal financial system and also promoting 
digital transactions, in August 2025, the scheme has been extended in a 
restructured manner till 2030 with a budgetary allocation of Rs.900 crore in 
BE 2026-27and Department of Financial Services (DFS) has also been taken 
on board.  
However, the Committee observe that out of 73.33 lakh loans 
disbursed in the first tranche, 28.03 lakh of total disbursed loans are still 
outstanding which is 38.23 percent of the total disbursed loans, indicating 
that 28.03 lakh street vendors are yet to repay their first tranche of loans 
and are not eligible for the second tranche of loan. Further, the Committee 
have also been informed that beneficiary target under the restructured 
scheme is 1.15 crore including 50 lakh new beneficiaries.  
The Committee understand that the scheme is demand driven, 
intending to cover all eligible street vendors willing to avail loans, and 
since the vendor population is dynamic (with new vendors entering & 

92 
 
exiting regularly), the Ministry does not have data on precise number of 
street vendors in the country. Nonetheless, it is important that the scheme 
should not lead to an unintentional surge in the number of street vendors 
in urban areas, thereby further congesting the limited public space or 
vending zones and footpaths. The Committee, thus recommend that  
(i) the Ministry should direct all the States/UTs, through Urban Local 
Bodies (ULBs), to assess the carrying capacity of designated 
vending zones and ULBs managed spaces to determine the number 
of street vendors that can be accommodated at a time and formulate 
a policy in this regard; 
(ii) Further, the ULBs through States/UTs must be strictly directed to 
issue Letter of Recommendation (LoR)/Certificate of Vending (CoV) 
to new vendors for the purpose of availing loan under PM SVANidhi 
only after conducting the stated ‘ULBs vendor carrying capacity 
assessment’ survey/exercise; 
(iii) It should be ensured that vending zones are strategically located in 
high footfall and commercially viable areas, in accordance with the 
Street Vendors Act, and PM SVANidhi guidelines to ensure 
sustainable business opportunities for vendors.  
 
Recommendation No. 11 
Need for Expedited Operationalisation and Effective Utilisation of Funds 
under CITIIS 2.0 
 
The Committee note that CITIIS 2.0 was approved in May 2023 with 
the objective of promoting circular economy–based integrated waste 
management at the city level, climate-oriented reforms at the State level 
and institutional strengthening at the national level. However, despite the 
programme being operational for nearly two years, financial progress 
remains significantly slow. 
Against a proposed outlay of ₹1,139.8 crore for 2026–27, only ₹300 
crore has been allocated. Further, actual expenditure has been minimal, 
i.e., ₹9.25 crore in 2024–25 and ₹0.17 crore in 2025–26, primarily because 

93 
 
the programme is still in the project preparation and maturation phase. The 
Committee also observe that although 18 cities have been selected under 
Component 1, project components and costs are yet to be finalised and 
implementation activities have not commenced. Similarly, under 
Component 2, while Tripartite Agreements have been signed with 21 
States/UTs, fund disbursement is linked to Disbursement Linked Indicators 
(DLIs) and expenditure remains limited. 
The Committee further note that the programme is supported 
through substantial external funding from Agence Française de 
Développement (French Development Agency) (AFD), Kreditanstalt für 
Wiederaufbau ("Credit Institute for Reconstruction") (KfW) and the 
European Union. Although no commitment charges or financial burden 
have arisen so far, continued delays in grounding projects may risk 
inefficient fund utilisation and affect the intended outcomes within the 
stipulated timeframe. 
In view of the above, the Committee recommend that the Ministry: 
(i) should expedite finalisation of project components and cost 
estimates for the 18 selected cities and ensure commencement of 
on-ground implementation with a clearly defined, time-bound 
framework for both city-level and State-level components; 
(ii) should  strengthen coordination with participating States/UTs to fast-
track approvals, procurement and execution, while ensuring that 
budget projections are realistic and aligned with actual 
implementation capacity to avoid persistent under-utilisation and 
wide gaps between outlay and expenditure; and 
(iii) Closely monitor phased drawdown of external loans and strictly link 
it to physical and financial progress so as to ensure timely utilisation 
and prevent any potential financial implications, thereby 
safeguarding the intended objectives of CITIIS 2.0.

Recommendation No. 12 
Focus of smaller Urban Local Bodies (ULBs) under the Urban Challenge 
Fund 
In the Budget 2025-2026, the Union Government proposed to set up 
an Urban Challenge Fund of ₹1 lakh crore to implement the proposals 
under three verticals, namely, (i) ‘Cities as Growth Hubs’, (ii) ‘Creative 
Redevelopment of Cities’ and (iii) 'Water and Sanitation'. According to the 
Ministry, this fund will finance up to 25 per cent of the cost of bankable 
projects with a stipulation that at least 50 per cent of the cost is funded 
from the market sources such as bonds, bank loans, and Public-Private 
Partnerships (PPPs) and the remaining 25 percent are financed by States, 
ULBs or through other sources. 
The Committee understand that urban infrastructure requires 
substantial funding, which so far have been supported primarily by 
budgetary grants. The Committee appreciate that against this backdrop, an 
innovative shift has been proposed, aiming to generate ₹4 lakh crore for 
urban development over the next five years through a challenge based, 
market-linked, reform driven and outcome-oriented scheme by 
encouraging States and ULBs to raise funds from the market for financing 
urban infrastructure projects. The Committee have also noted that these 
changes will be accompanied by reforms in accounting system followed by 
States/Cities/ULBs. Further, to enable participation of fiscally weaker Urban 
Local Bodies, a Credit Repayment Guarantee Sub-Scheme has been 
approved under the Urban Challenge Fund. A corpus of ₹5,000 crore has 
been created under this Sub-Scheme to provide credit repayment 
guarantee support. The Sub-Scheme will cover all ULBs in Northeastern 
and Hilly States/UTs and smaller ULBs (with population below 1,00,000) in 
other States/UTs, enabling them to raise resources from the market and 
participate in the Urban Challenge Fund.  
The Committee observe that the Ministry were not able to raise 
desired funds through Public Private Partnership (PPP) under earlier 
Schemes, such as the Smart Cities Mission. Further, the success of any 

95 
 
Scheme depends on the delivery capability and absorption capacity of the 
ULBs. The bigger ULBs will naturally be in a better position to avail 
themselves of the benefits of the UCF compared to the smaller ones. The 
Committee, thus recommend the Ministry :  
(i) to specifically focus on smaller cities and those cities which were 
not covered under the Smart Cities Mission so that the intended 
benefits generated from the Scheme are evenly distributed; and 
(ii) for early finalisation of guidelines and operational modalities of the 
Scheme. 
 
Recommendation No. 13 
Need for Ensuring Prior Approval and Policy Clarity in Rollout of New 
Urban Schemes 
The Committee observe that several major initiatives of the Ministry 
such as the National Urban Digital Mission (NUDM), the Scheme for 
Industrial Housing, the Site Adjacent Factory Employee (SAFE) 
Accommodation under Pradhan Mantri - National Industrial Workers 
Accommodation Scheme (PM-NIWAS), Deendayal Jan Aajeevika (Shehari) 
(DJAY-S) and the proposed Municipal Bonds scheme, are yet to receive 
final approval of the Cabinet. At the same time, substantial budgetary 
allocations have been made in successive years, even though the 
contours, guidelines, funding patterns and implementation frameworks of 
these schemes are still under formulation. 
In the case of NUDM, the Cabinet Note has been returned for revision 
and the Mission framework is under reconsideration, yet allocations were 
provided in 2024-25 and 2025-26 with sharp reductions thereafter at RE 
stage. Similarly, DJAY-S has concluded its pilot phase and awaits Cabinet 
approval for full-scale rollout, while fresh allocations have been made in BE 
2026–27. The Industrial Housing/PM–NIWAS initiative was announced with 
₹2,500 crore in 2025–26 and ₹400 crore in 2026-27, despite the scheme 
remaining at the conceptual stage. The Municipal Bonds scheme has been 
introduced with a token allocation of ₹100 crore even though its guidelines 

96 
 
are under preparation and requisite approvals of the Cabinet and 
Expenditure Finance Committee are pending. 
The Committee feel that announcing schemes, making budgetary 
allocations and even operating pilot projects for indefinite periods without 
final statutory approval and clearly notified guidelines lead to uncertainty 
in implementation, under-utilisation of funds, frequent revisions in budget 
estimates and lack of accountability in outcomes. Such an approach also 
creates confusion among States/UTs and ULBs regarding eligibility, 
funding support and long-term sustainability. Moreover, it may give rise to 
a perception that policy initiatives are characterised more by 
announcements and preliminary allocations than by timely execution on 
the ground. The Committee emphasise that all announced initiatives 
should be implemented promptly so that they translate into tangible 
outcomes and benefits for citizens. 
The Committee, therefore, recommend that : 
(i) proper due diligence must be undertaken by the Ministry before 
making full-scale budgetary allocations for any new scheme. 
Allocations should be provided only after the scheme’s contours, 
funding pattern and implementation guidelines have been finalised 
and approved by the competent authority. Any token provision, if 
considered necessary, should be clearly justified and accompanied 
by a defined timeline;  
(ii) Proposals such as NUDM, DJAY-S, Industrial Housing/PM–NIWAS 
and Municipal Bonds, which are currently under consideration for 
approval, should be placed before the Cabinet/EFC at the earliest, 
with a clearly defined timeline for their operational rollout; 
(iii) Pilot initiatives should have clearly defined objectives, evaluation 
frameworks and clauses, and their learnings should be formally 
incorporated into the final scheme design before scaling up.

Recommendation No. 14 
Promoting effective coordination and integration among urban sector 
schemes to prevent duplication and overlapping 
 
The Committee observe that multiple schemes of the Ministry 
operate within closely related thematic areas affordable housing for 
workers under Affordable Rental Housing Complexes (ARHCs) under PMAY 
(U) and the Scheme for Industrial Housing/SAFE Accommodation under 
PM–NIWAS; sewage and wastewater management under AMRUT and 
Swachh Bharat Mission (SBM-Urban); solid waste management and 
circular economy initiatives under SBM-U and City Investments to 
Innovate, Integrate and Sustain (CITIIS-2.0) and Urban Challenge Fund 
(UCF); and urban livelihoods and employment through PM SVANidhi and 
DJAY-S. While each scheme has distinct objectives, their operational 
domains often intersect at the level of States/UTs and Urban Local Bodies 
(ULBs). 
The Committee feel that, in the absence of a clearly articulated 
convergence framework, such overlap may lead to duplication of funding, 
fragmented implementation, inefficient utilisation of resources and 
confusion among implementing agencies and beneficiaries. There is also a 
risk of multiple schemes financing similar infrastructure components or 
targeting overlapping beneficiary groups without proper integration of 
objectives and outcomes. 
The Committee, therefore, recommend the Ministry to prepare and 
notify a comprehensive convergence framework clearly delineating the 
scope, target beneficiaries, admissible components and funding patterns 
of each urban sector scheme so as to prevent overlap, promote 
complementarities and ensure optimal utilisation of public resources;

Recommendation No. 15 
Need for Long-Term Urban Infrastructure Planning and Investment Strategy 
 
  The Committee sought to ascertain whether the Ministry has 
prepared or proposes to prepare a unified long-term urban strategy aligned 
with the vision of Viksit Bharat 2047, integrating infrastructure creation, 
governance reforms, climate resilience, economic growth and social 
inclusion across metropolitan, Tier-II and Tier-III cities. The Committee have 
been apprised that the High Powered Expert Committee (HPEC), in its 2011 
report, had estimated urban infrastructure investment requirements up to 
2031 and projected urban population share to increase to 75% by 2030. 
Since then, no similar Committee has been constituted. The Ministry further 
referred to the 2022 report “Cities as Engines of Growth” by NITI Aayog and 
Asian Development Bank (ADB), which highlights the strong correlation 
between urbanisation and GDP growth. The Ministry submitted that various 
ongoing missions such as AMRUT 2.0, SBM-U 2.0, PMAY-U 2.0, Metro Rail 
Projects and PM e-Bus Seva are contributing towards improving quality of 
life and supporting the vision of Viksit Bharat 2047. 
 
  The Committee note that while several flagship schemes are being 
implemented and financing support mechanisms have been introduced, 
these interventions are largely scheme-driven and sector-specific. The last 
comprehensive and consolidated assessment of urban infrastructure 
requirements was undertaken by the HPEC in 2011, with projections only 
up to 2031. No updated national-level assessment has been undertaken to 
estimate infrastructure demand, financing gaps and institutional 
requirements beyond 2030 in a holistic manner. Given the rapid pace of 
urbanisation, the growing economic centrality of cities, emerging climate 
risks and the stated national goal of achieving ‘Viksit Bharat 2047’, the 
absence of an integrated long-term urban investment and strategy 
framework may lead to fragmented planning, sub-optimal allocation of 
resources and financing stress in the future. 

99 
 
 
  In view of the above, the Committee recommend the Ministry to 
constitute a new High-Level Expert Committee to comprehensively assess 
India’s urban infrastructure requirements, financing needs, governance 
reforms and capacity-building imperatives up to 2047 aligned with the 
vision of ‘Viksit Bharat 2047’. A forward-looking and evidence-based 
roadmap would enable coordinated planning, better fiscal preparedness 
and balanced urban development across metropolitan, Tier-II and Tier-III 
cities.
