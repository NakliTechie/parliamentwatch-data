INTRODUCTION 
 I, the Chairperson of the Standing Committee on Housing and Urban Affairs 
(2025-26), having been authorized by the Committee, present the Thirteenth Report  
(18th Lok Sabha) on the subject, ‘Census Criteria for Defining Urban Areas’ relating to 
the Department of Urban Development, Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs. 
2. The Committee examined this subject with a view to assessing the adequacy of 
the existing definition of ‘urban’ in the context of rapid urbanisation and changing 
settlement patterns. The Committee focused on the distinction between Census-based 
and administrative definitions of urban areas, and the need for a definition that more 
effectively captures the emerging transition from rural to urban areas and facilitates 
appropriate planning, governance and delivery of urban services. 
3. The Committee were briefed on the subject by the representatives of (i) M/o 
Housing and Urban Affairs, (ii) Registrar General and Census Commissioner of India on 
17 October 2025.  
4.  The Committee wish to express their thanks to the officials of the Ministry of 
Housing and Urban Affairs and Registrar General and Census Commissioner of India 
for appearing before them and furnishing the information that were sought in connection 
with the examination of the subject. 
5.  The Committee would also like to place on record their deep sense of 
appreciation for the invaluable assistance rendered to them by the Officials of Lok 
Sabha Secretariat attached to the Committee. 
6.  The Committee considered and adopted Draft Report at their Sitting held on  
16 September 2026. 
7.  For facility of reference, the observations/recommendations of the Committee are 
highlighted in bold letters in Part II of the Report.

PART-I 
I. INTRODUCTORY 
India is experiencing one of the fastest urban transitions globally. The urban 
population is projected to grow from 377 million (31%) in 2011 to 876 million (53%) by 
2050. However, urban areas occupy merely about 3% of total land area while 
contributing around 60% of the GDP and are expected to contribute approximately 80% 
of GDP by 2047. This rapid urbanisation brings with it enormous opportunities for 
growth, accompanied by complex challenges relating to planning, infrastructure, 
governance and equitable service delivery. Along with rapid urbanisation, there has also 
been a continuous rural urban migration which is also adding to the urban congestion. 
2. The Census of India categories the geographical India into ‘rural’ and ‘urban’. But 
there exists a mismatch between the Census definition of ‘urban’ and the administrative 
definition of ‘urban’. This leads to a definitional challenge in India’s urban framework. 
Terms such as statutory towns, census towns and outgrowth areas are used frequently, 
often interchangeably, across policy circles. However, each carries specific meaning 
and distinct administrative implications. In the ongoing Census 2027, the existing criteria 
for defining ‘Census Towns’ has been retained as used in Census 2011 to ensure 
comparability.  
3. The Committee felt that there is a need to revisit the definition of ‘urban’ areas in 
India. As urbanisation does not happen in silos and with the current rapidity with which 
the physically defined boundaries between ‘rural’ and ‘urban’ are increasingly becoming 
blurred; it is felt that India may face challenges in effectively planning for urbanisation 
goals if the definition of ‘urban’ does not evolve with changing settlement patterns. With 
this objective, the Committee took up the subject for detailed examination and report. In 
the process, the Committee obtained background materials and took oral evidence and 
obtained post-evidence written replies from the Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs 
(MoHUA) and the Office of Registrar General & Census Commissioner of India (ORGI). 
Based on the written replies and oral depositions of the Ministry and the Census 

 2
Commissioner of India, the Committee have examined various issues pertaining to the 
subject matter as enumerated in the succeeding paragraphs followed by the Committee’ 
Observations/Recommendations. 
II. Existing definition of ‘urban’ 
4. ‘Urban’ area in India comprises of ‘Statutory Towns’ (ST) and ‘Census Towns’ 
(CT). There are two varying agencies which are defining ST and CT in India. 
(i) Census definition of ‘urban’- As per the Census of India, ‘urban area’ 
constitutes Statutory Towns and Census Towns. The Registrar General and 
Census Commissioner of India provide the definition of ‘Census Towns’. The 
Census of India applies criteria like population size, density of population and 
percentage of male work force in non-agriculture sector in identifying urban areas 
termed as ‘Census Towns’ at every ten years interval. 
(ii) TheStatutory administrative definition of ‘urban’- The State Governments 
notifies the ‘Statutory Towns’ and thus, decide about the civic status of towns 
such as municipal corporations, municipality, cantonment board, notified area 
committee, etc. Notifying of the ‘Statutory Towns’ by the State Governments is 
not related to census criteria of defining ‘Census Towns’ or with ‘Census Towns’ 
per se. 
5. To the question as to why the definition of ‘urban’ matters, the representative of 
MoHUA explained as given: 
(i) Funds- The definition of ‘urban’ determines fiscal transfers from the Centre to 
States and ULBs, and miscalculation can distort allocations; 
(ii) Functions and Functionaries- Requirements in urban areas are distinct from 
those in rural areas (planning, infrastructure, staffing)’ 
(iii) Frameworks- A standardised definition ensures accurate urban measurement 
and international comparability; 

 3
(iv) Future- Appropriate definitions are needed to capture the rural-urban 
continuum and peri-urban growth and manage transitions. 
6. Highlighting the importance of the definition of ‘urban’, the Secretary, MoHUA 
submitted before the Committee: 
“……the way urbanization has evolved in this country, and the rapidity with which 
it is progressing, has enormous positive consequences, potential for growth, as 
well as challenges. Sir, even as we stand, going by certain conventional 
definitions, India is 36 per cent urban. 
There are some international definitions, while we have not accepted them, who 
estimate that we have perhaps already crossed 50 per cent. So, the way we 
define urban is also going to have implications for policy formulation, for 
implementation, and for our spatial imagination….” 
7. On the issue whether the current definition can capture the true level of 
urbanisation, the Secretary, MoHUA stated as follows: 
“But as we are all aware, urbanization cannot be captured, in my considered view, 
by these parameters alone. There are many more aspects, many more 
definitions that we might need to look at as we go forward. And I'm aware that the 
Census of India primarily is a demographic exercise, trying to assess population, 
how much population has evolved, where are people living, what are the various 
activities. And for us, while the census is very important, as an important data 
source, we also serve, at the very outset, I would like to submit, we may have to, 
with the guidance of the hon. Committee, have to evolve parameters that are 
proxy indicators of urbanization as we go forward. For instance, if we were to 
take only density as one of the elements, then many of our villages are denser 
than many cities, because that is the way village sites are established. If we were 
to take a criterion such as what is the non-farm employment, today more and 
more increasingly, close to around 40% of female labour force participation is 
taking place, which also needs to be acknowledged in the future. Third, the 
phenomenon of migration for livelihood purposes, of youngsters coming to more 
urban areas for delivering services, for livelihoods, etc., is another thing that is 
impacting. And also, what is going to be important is the ability of the urban areas 
to support economic activity. And the economic activity that urban area is 
capable of supporting, in contrast to rural areas, is that it can support complex 
activity, where more than one or two or three types of material, inter-dependency 
on large geographies, where services, goods, materials coming from maybe 
across the globe can come together and work, which can happen in an urban 
area, but may not happen in a rural area so efficiently. So, these are the new 
realities that we have to deal with. 
….I must also admit that our understanding, in my view, is not complete. We are 
still trying to identify what would be the right parameters.” 

 4
“If we go by the ‘census’ definition, in 2011, we had statutory towns which were 
4,041 in number constituting 84 per cent of the total urban population. Census 
towns were 3,892 constituting 15 per cent and urban out growths were 1,031 
constituting one per cent.” 
(i) Census Definition of ‘urban’  
8. The first Census in India was conducted in the year 1872 non-synchronously. In 
the year 1881, a Census was taken for the entire country simultaneously. Since then, 
Census has been conducted once in every ten years. The last Census was conducted 
in 2011. Census 2021 could not be conducted due to worldwide spread of COVID-19. 
The next Census will be undertaken in 2027 which would be the 16 th Census in the 
country and the 8th since Independence. 
9. As regards the Census process, the Census Commissioner apprised the 
Committee that the main objective of the Census is to ensure that there is no omission 
or duplication. To achieve this goal, it is imperative to have a robust and updated frame 
of administrative units at all levels (State, District, Sub-District, Town/Ward/Village). The 
first step in this direction is updation of jurisdictional changes that have taken place 
between the freezing dates of previous and upcoming Census. All the State/UT 
Governments have already been requested not to make any jurisdiction changes from 
01.01.2026 to 31.03.2027. After ensuring the updation of all jurisdictional changes, the 
second step in this direction is rural-urban classification. 
10. About the ‘rural-urban’ classification, the Registrar General & Census 
Commissioner informed the Committee as under:- 
“Census of India follows a dichotomous approach wherein the entire geographical 
area of the country is classified as either urban or rural.”

11. As per the Census of India, urban areas comprise Statutory Towns, Census 
Towns, Outgrowths and Urban Agglomerations (a combination of Statutory Towns and 
Outgrowths). Concept and detailed approach of identification of these areas is 
mentioned in Census Circulars. Census’s urban definition considers several features 
such as demography, density and socio-economic character of the population, as given 
below: 
 
 Statutory Towns:  All administrative units that have been defined by statute as 
urban like Municipal Corporation, Municipality, Cantonment Board, Notified Town 
Area Committee, Town Panchayat, Nagar Palika, etc. irrespective of their 
demographic characteristics are known as Statutory Town. Further Statutory 
Towns with population of 1,00,000 and above are categorized as cities. 
 Census Towns : All villages that satisfy all of the following criteria: 
i. A minimum population of 5,000 persons; 
ii. 75% and above of the male main working population being engaged in non-
agricultural pursuits; and 
iii. A density of population of at least 400 persons per sq. km. (1,000 per sq. 
mile). 


 6
 
Outgrowths: In some cases railway colonies, university campuses, port areas, 
military camps etc., would have come up near a statutory town outside its statutory 
limits but within the revenue limits of a village or villages contiguous to the town. 
Each of these individual areas may not by itself satisfy the criteria to qualify as an 
independent urban unit but by virtue of its urban features in terms of infrastructure 
and amenities but it may qualify to be clubbed with the existing towns as their 
continuous urban spread. While determining the adjoining revenue village as 
outgrowth of a town, it is ensured that it possesses the urban features in terms of 
infrastructure and amenities such as pucca roads, electricity, tap water connection, 
drainage system for disposal of waste water, educational institutions, post offices, 
medical facilities, banks, etc. Identification of Out Growths is done by joint physical 
inspection by the officers from Directorate of Census Operations and concerned 
local authority. 
 
Urban Agglomerations : An Urban Agglomeration is a continuous urban spread 
constituting a town and its adjoining outgrowths (OGs), or two or more physically 
contiguous towns together with or without outgrowths of such towns. For 
identification of Urban Agglomerations following criteria is adopted: 
a. The core town or at least one of the constituent towns of an Urban 
Agglomeration should necessarily be a statutory town; and 
b. The total population of an Urban Agglomeration ( i.e. all the constituents put 
together) should not be less than 20,000 as per Census 2011.”

Census Towns 
 
13. Regarding the definition of ‘Census Towns’, the Registrar General and Census 
Commissioner submitted before the Committee that there was not any rigid definition of 
Census Towns in the Censuses of pre-independence era. It basically focused on higher 
proportion of population engaged in trade and industrial activities and a continuous 
group of houses. Further, provincial/State Census Superintendents exercised a great 
deal of discretion in identifying Census Towns in their Province/State. For the first time 
in Census 1961, rigid criteria were adopted for identification of Census Towns. Census 
Towns were identified on the basis of a number of empirical tests, along with an 
additional criterion that the place should, in the opinion of the Superintendent of the 
State, possess certain pronounced urban characteristics and amenities. However, from 
the 1981 Census onwards, this criterion was discontinued, and the identification of 
Census Towns was made purely on objective and empirical grounds. 
14. On being asked as to why it was decided to retain the 2011 definition of Census 
Towns or in fact, the 1981 definition of ‘urban’ in the ongoing Census, the ORGI replied 
as under: 
“The definition of a Census Town is kept the same as in Census 2011 to maintain 
uniformity, comparability, and reliability of data overtime.” 
 

 8
15. As per the Census Commissioner, the Census Towns are identified while 
preparing and updating the frame for Census i.e. before taking of Census operations 
and become the part of urban frame. As a matter of practice, all villages having a 
population count of 4000 and above in previous Census (expected to cross the 
population count of 5000 in next Census) and satisfying the other two criteria i.e., 
population density and economic activity as per data from previous Census are 
identified as CTs and are included in the urban frame for upcoming Census. It may be 
pertinent to mention that due to paucity of area figures of villages in absence of 
cadastral surveys in many North-Eastern States, it is difficult to identify Census Towns 
as population density cannot be worked out without area figures. 
16. The Registrar General & Census Commissioner explained the significance of 
Census Towns as under:- 
a. Census Town serve as an indicator of pre-urbanization, marking a transition 
phase from rural to urban characteristics. 
b. Census Towns (CTs) help the Census of India capture the real extent of 
urban growth in the country revealing the hidden dimension of India’s 
urbanization, beyond the officially recognized municipalities and corporations. 
c. Census Towns often serve as a reference for State/UT authorities when 
considering areas for notification as Statutory Towns. 
d. Various government agencies such as NSSO; Labour Bureau; Ministry of 
Environment, Forest & Climate Change take Census Towns into account for 
various surveys and policy making purpose. 
17. To provide the Committee a better understanding of the role of Census Towns 
(CTs), the Registrar General & Census Commissioner explained as given: 
“….as per census2011, which is the last census that we have, rural population 
was 83.3 crore, about 68.8 per cent, urban population was 37.7 crore about 31.2 
per cent, and population in census towns which we talked about is 5.4 crore and 
share of census towns in India’s urban population is about 14.4 per cent. So, this 
time, we expect this figure would have gone much higher.” 
 
18. On being asked as to whether the Indian Census provide an explicit definition of 
the term ‘rural’, the reply received from the Registrar General & Census Commissioner 
is as under: -

“Census’s urban definition considers a number of features such as demography, 
density and socio-economic character of the population. 
 
Statutory Town refers to any administrative unit legally defined as urban—such 
as a Municipal Corporation, Municipality, Cantonment Board, Notified Town Area

(ii) Statutory administrative definition of ‘urban’ 
 
 
21. Thus, in the preceding paragraphs, it is seen that Census Towns are those areas 
which are defined as urban by the Census of India but continue to be under rural 
governance till notified by State Government as Statutory Towns. Thus, for the 
administrative purposes, these are Statutory Towns which are administered by different 
types of Urban Local Bodies. Following the enactment of the Seventy-fourth 
Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992, all the State Governments were required to 
amend their existing Municipal legislations to sync them with Part IX-A of the 
Constitution. However, the criteria adopted by State for creation of municipality varies 
from State to State. Elaborating on this point, the representative of NIUA deposed 
before the Committee as under: 
 
“…. urban development is a State subject, so we have variation of criteria to 
identify statutory towns. For example, for municipal corporations, the population 
size varies from 50,000 in Himachal Pradesh to five lakh in Tripura or West 
Bengal. Density per sq. km. also varies from 500 to 3,000, non-farm employment 
from 50 per cent to 85 percent and revenue generated from Rs.2 crore to Rs.6 
crore. The municipal corporations also have varied definitions in addition to the 

 11
nagar panchayats. Again, following the 74 th Constitutional amendment in 1992, 
we have large urban areas which are basically the municipal corporations, small 
urban areas with which are the municipal councils and transitional urban areas 
which are the entry point of urban, which are the town panchayats or nagar 
panchayats.” 
 
22. The distinction between statistical urbanisation and administrative urbanisation 
has significant implication for planning, financing and delivery of urban services 
particularly in settlements where urban characteristics have emerged but municipal 
institutions have yet not been instituted. 
 
Criteria for defining ‘Municipality’ under various State Municipal Acts 
 
23. Article 243Q of the Indian Constitution dealing with the constitution of 
Municipalities, provides that: 
(1) There shall be constituted in every State,-- 
 
(a) a Nagar Panchayat (by whatever name called) for a transitional area, 
that is to say, an area in transition from a rural area to an urban area. 
 
(b) a Municipal Council for a smaller urban area; and 
 
(c) a Municipal Corporation for a larger urban area, 
 
in accordance with the provisions of this Part: 
 
Provided that a Municipality under this clause may not be constituted in such 
urban area or part thereof as the Governor may, having regard to the size of the 
area and the municipal services being provided or proposed to be provided by an 
industrial establishment in that area and such other factors as he may deem fit, 
by public notification, specify to be an industrial township. 
 
(2) In this article, 'a transitional area', 'a smaller urban area' or 'a larger urban 
area' means such area as the Governor may, having regard to the population of 
the area, the density of the population therein, the revenue generated for local 
administration, the percentage of employment in non-agricultural activities, the 
economic importance or such other factors as he may deem fit, specify by public 
notification for the purposes of this Part. 
24. Article 243Q, thus, provides a broad constitutional framework rather than a 
uniform criterion for declaring areas as transitional, small or large urban areas. This 

 12
discretion available to State governments under this constitutional provision has 
resulted in considerable variation in the criteria adopted by different States.

III. Criteria adopted/followed by different countries of the world for defining 
‘urban’ 
 
28. Talking about the criteria and approach adopted for defining ‘urban’ by various 
countries around the world, Director, National Institute of Urban Affairs (NIUA) briefed 
the Committee as given: 
“If we look at the international practices for defining ‘urban’, we had tried to look 
at the data provided by the United Nations World Urban Prospects of 2018, 
which is the latest data. As per that, what comes out through this analysis is that 
around 70 per cent of the countries use one criterion or two criteria to define 
‘urban’. In India, as I mentioned, we look at three criteria for determining census 
towns. There are countries which take into account four criteria, but that is only 
five per cent. Another five per cent looks at the entire urban population as urban. 
In 12 countries, or five per cent, there is no definition or clear definition for 
identifying urban. Three are alternative approaches which have come up. Before 
I go into that, there are also countries which look at one criterion which is 
basically administrative or population. Two criteria look at population and density. 
There are also countries including India which look at population or density and 
also economic criteria. When we look at alternative approaches, what we find is 
that international NGOs, researchers have proposed various approaches. One is 
the functional urban area by the European Union followed by the World Bank 
which has looked at population size, density and a travel time of 45 minutes to 
the main city. There are functional urban areas which are using the population 
density and also the catchment areas of mobile phone data. Researches, United 
Nations and the European Union’s Degree of Urbanisation look at population 
density, population size and also contiguity of urban settlements. Denis & Marius 
look at population and contiguity, whereas other researchers look at night light 
data.” 
 
29. Applying the above international criteria and approaches to the Indian context, 
Director, NIUA further explained as under: 
“When we look at India’s urban scenarios under changed criteria in 2011Census, 
and if we look at only the administration definition, what we find is that we were 
26 per cent urban in 2011. If we take into account Census Towns, Statutory 
Towns and urban outgrowths, we were 31 per cent urban. If we look at only the 
population size, we were 47 per cent urban. If we look at only the density criteria, 
we were around 74% per cent urban. If we look at only the economic activity, we 
were 35 per cent urban. If we look at alternative approaches, as per World Bank 
Agglomeration Index, we were 55 per cent urban and if we take into account the 
DEGURBA classification, we were 81 percent urban.”

367 CTs merged with old STs and 12 CTs of NCT Delhi will be treated as STs for

V. Issues with the existing definition of ‘urban’ 
 
36. Explaining the issues with the definition of ‘urban’, the representative of the 
Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs deposed before the Committee as under: 
“…the old binary of saying rural and urban is no longer adequate – that is the 
point we are making. It is because that was the approach that was possible 
perhaps in 60’s. It is no longer possible now because of the diversity and 
complexity of economic activity, particularly the services sector. What do we 
need to have to define a particular area as urban in terms of its ability to support 

 17
large populations, ability of that area to carry through mobility systems large 
populations and thereby enable them to be functionally efficient? 
 
Frankly, we do not have the full answer. That is why, we were giving the 
presentation of the complexity. I would like to state a small example. Supposing 
in the area of transportation alone, we were to say what would be an urban area 
and what will be a rural area. If I, as a citizen, were living in a rural area, possibly, 
my need to travel would be three times or four times a day, which will make me 
functionally efficient. But in a city, I might need to travel several times more than 
that to become functionally efficient. This means the ability of the city 
transportation system to enable me to transfer seamlessly and efficiently is much 
more than in the rural area. So, in a rural area perhaps a bus service of 30 
minutes’ frequency can be adequate. But in a city like Delhi, with a metro system 
running, we are trying to run on a five-minute or a six-minute frequency in the 
Delhi metro system with a 99.96 per cent efficiency of timeliness. Unless that 
happens, this urban area does not work.” 
37. Further, elaborating on the subject, the Ministry highlighted the following 
concerns regarding the existing Census definition of ‘urban’: 
(i) Outdated and Rigid Criteria in a Dynamic Context 
• Census definition (since 1971) uses fixed cutoffs — Population ≥ 5,000, Density 
≥ 400/sq.km, and ≥75% male main non-farm workers — which no longer reflect 
today’s demographic, economic, or spatial transformations 
• Urban characteristics have evolved, but the definition remains static. 
 
(ii) Uniform Thresholds Ignore Regional Diversity 
• The Census applies a uniform population threshold (Population ≥ 5,000, Density 
≥ 400/sq.km), ignoring state-specific variations (e.g., hilly vs. plain states) 
• Hilly states are under-represented—smaller settlements rarely meet the criteria 
 
(iii) Misrepresentation of Population Density 
• Current classification uses administrative (revenue) area, not abadi (built-up) 
area 
• This results in underestimation of density and misclassification of settlements 
 
(iv) Gender Bias in Economic Criteria 
• The 75% male main non-farm worker rule (since 1971) excludes female 
participation 
• In 1961, total workforce (male + female) was used — current definition is gender-
biased 

 18
 
(v) Outdated Ex-Ante Classification 
• Rural–urban classification is pre-decided (ex-ante), based on outdated data from 
the previous Census. 
• Leads to exclusion and inclusion errors — e.g., villages meeting urban criteria 
are missed, and some areas wrongly classified as urban. 
 
(vi) Overlooking Peri-Urban and Transitional Areas 
• The Census follows a binary rural–urban classification, leaving out many fast-
growing peri-urban areas that function as urban but remain classified as rural. 
 
• This binary framework fails to capture transitional spaces that are central to 
India’s ongoing urban expansion. 
38. The Ministry also identified keys issues with the Statutory Towns (STs) definition 
and summarise them before the Committee as under: 
(i) Wide Variation in Population Thresholds across States 
• Population thresholds for declaring Statutory Towns vary significantly and often 
lack coherence. 
• Transitional areas: 2,000 (Himachal Pradesh) → 30,000 (Tamil Nadu, West 
Bengal)  
• Smaller towns: 5,000 (Himachal Pradesh, Sikkim) → 1.5 lakh (Mizoram) 


 19
• Larger towns: 50,000 (Himachal Pradesh) → 5 lakh (Mizoram, Tripura, 
West Bengal) 
 
(ii) Violations of Declared Criteria 
• Many small and transitional towns do not meet population or economic 
benchmarks but are still declared Urban Local Bodies (ULBs). 
• This weakens the integrity and comparability of urban classifications. 
 
(iii) Executive Fiat and Discretionary Decisions 
• In several states without explicit Urban Acts, the declaration of urban areas rests 
on executive discretion 
• This leads to arbitrary and politically contested decisions on what becomes or 
unbecomes urban 
 
(iv) Implications for Governance and Comparability 
• Inconsistent criteria and discretionary practices have resulted in divergent 
interpretations of “urban” across states 
• This causes weak comparability, governance overlaps, and uneven policy 
implementation. 
39. Elaborating on how the existing definition of ‘urban’ is leading to ‘governance’ 
challenges, the issues submitted by the Ministry are as under: 
(i) Absence of Regional and Functional Planning- Blind Spot 
• Urban governance is confined to statutory limits, ignoring Census Towns, peri-
urban areas, and growth corridors where most expansion occurs, leading to 
fragmented and uncoordinated development 
(ii) Political and Local Resistance 
• Elected representatives and local people resist urban status to retain rural 
tenure, avoid higher taxes and user charges, and continue accessing rural 
subsidies and schemes 
(iii) Executive Discretion and Legal Inconsistencies 
• Urban notifications differ across states, often based on administrative discretion 
rather than uniform legal norms, undermining transparency and comparability 
(iv) Governance and Capacity Gaps 
• Many small or newly declared ULBs lack institutional capacity, manpower, and 
financial strength to deliver urban services effectively 

 20
(v) Data Silos and Coordination Challenges 
• Data sharing among agencies is limited. The lack of integrated, spatially updated 
databases constrains evidence-based planning and timely reclassification 
40. The Ministry also submitted the following programmatic issues in ‘urban’ 
definition before the Committee: 
(i) Inconsistent Classification and Funding Exclusion 
• Inconsistent recognition of urban areas across states distorts scheme eligibility, 
funding distribution, and programme design, leaving Census Towns, peri-urban, 
and transitional areas outside major urban programmes 
 
(ii) Binary Urban–Rural Approach 
• Schemes are designed strictly for either rural or urban jurisdictions, ignoring 
functionally urban settlements, resulting in service gaps and underinvestment in 
transition zones 
 
(iii) Lack of Programme Convergence 
• Central and state schemes often operate independently without coordination 
 
(iv) Weak Link Between Definition and Planning 
• Urban classification remains disconnected from development planning cycles, 
causing misalignment between demographic trends, infrastructure needs, and 
programme design 
 
(v) Data Gaps for Targeting and Monitoring 
• Limited access to granular, geo-coded, and disaggregated data hinders effective 
programme design 
41. The above programmatic related issues highlight the fact that classification of 
settlements has direct implications for access to infrastructure, centrally sponsored 
development schemes and other institutional support.

(c) Ex-ante Classification under the existing definition 
 
47. The Ministry in their brief submitted to the Committee has stated that Urban 
classification in India is determined ex-ante—that is, in advance—based on preceding 
Census data. According to the Ministry, this results in both inclusion and exclusion 
errors: many villages that now meet urban characteristics continue to be classified as 
‘rural’, while some areas remain ‘urban’ despite no longer meeting the prescribed 
criteria. 
 
48. In this context, the Committee asked the Registrar General and Census 
Commissioner about the rationale for ex-ante determination of ‘urban’. The Census 
Commissioner submitted as under: 
 
“The Census of India follows an ex-ante approach for rural-urban classification 
because an updated and finalized Census frame is required before 
commencement of Census operations to ensure complete coverage of the 
population without omission or duplication. 
 
The preparation of the Census frame involves (i) updation of all jurisdictional 
changes, (ii) rural-urban classification including identification of Census Towns, 
and (iii) identification of Urban Agglomerations and Outgrowths. These activities 
are undertaken prior to the conduct of Census so that enumeration blocks, 
charge boundaries, supervisory jurisdictions, maps, training arrangements and 
field deployment can be finalized in advance. 
 
This approach is essential for planning and conducting a nation-wide Census in a 
uniform and time-bound manner.” 
 
 
49. On being asked whether it is true that due to ex-ante classification of ‘urban’ 
many villages that now meet urban characteristics continue as ‘rural’ and whether the 
Registrar General and Census Commissioner not upgrade villages meeting urban 

 24
characteristics as ‘urban’, the reply given by Office of Registrar General and Census 
Commissioner is as under: 
 
“Under the existing methodology, some villages which may satisfy the prescribed 
urban criteria on the basis of data generated during the current Census cannot 
be classified as Census Towns for that very Census. Similarly, some Census 
Towns identified on the basis of data from the previous Census may 
subsequently cease to satisfy one or more criteria but continue to remain Census 
Towns until the next revision of the Census frame. 
 
This is an inherent consequence of the ex-ante approach followed for preparation 
of the Census frame. Further, until Census 2011, Census data was collected 
through paper schedules. Consequently, the processing, coding, and tabulation 
of worker-related information took lot of time and were time-consuming, resulting 
in a considerable delay in publication of worker related data. Since the rural and 
urban population figures were published much before the worker related data, 
there was no scope for changing the Census Towns related figures.  
 
The present methodology has been followed consistently to ensure stability of 
the Census frame, uniformity of classification and smooth conduct of Census 
operations across the country. Though, Census towns are identified based on 
Census 2011, it may, however, be noted that with the forthcoming digital Census 
and availability of data in digital form, at the point of release of data, if the data on 
criteria underpinning Census Towns is digitally available, the Census Towns may

(d) Variation in criteria followed by States for notifying ‘Statutory Towns’ 
51. Although the primary focus of this report is the Census definition of ‘urban’, 
however, the Committee during detailed examination found that it is the statutory urban 
areas which are administratively considered as ‘urban’. Thus, the inconsistencies in 
statutory administrative definition of ‘urban’ have also been examined. The Committee 
considered it necessary to examine the statutory framework because administrative 
recognition of urban areas determines government structures, institutional 
responsibilities and access to urban development programmes.  
52. As per the brief provided by the Ministry, the process of identifying Statutory 
Towns rests with the State Governments, leading to wide variation and inconsistency 
across the country. While some states specify eligibility criteria such as minimum 
population, population density, share of non-farm employment, or revenue generation, 
others lack such statutory provisions and provide broad discretion to the Governor or 
the State Government. This discretion flows from Article 243Q of the Constitution, which 
empowers the Governor to declare an area urban based on factors such as population, 
density, and non-agricultural employment. This has resulted in a discretionary and non-
standardised approach to recognising urban settlements. 
53. The States do not follow one criterion for declaring an area as ‘urban’, the criteria 
vary from State to State. On this, during oral deposition, the Ministry submitted as under: 
“In several States, without explicit Urban Acts, the declaration of urban areas 
rests on the executive discretion. This leads to arbitrary and politically-contested 
decisions on what becomes urban or what unbecomes urban. Then, comes the 
implications for governance and comparability. So, the inconsistent criteria and 
discretionary practices have resulted in divergent interpretations of ‘urban’ across 
the States. Now, this causes weak comparability, governance overlaps, and 
uneven policy implementation.” 

 26
54. Elaborating on the issue of significantly varying population thresholds set by 
different States for declaring Statutory Towns which often lacks coherence, the 
representative of the Ministry deposed before the Committee as given below: 
“Here, another thing is that many small and transitional towns do not meet the 
population or economic benchmarks but are still declared Urban Local Bodies. 
For example, there is a Statutory Town called Haldibari in West Bengal, which 
has a population of 14,404 but is not meeting the criteria of a Transitional Area. 
There are 19 such towns also in West Bengal. Chandernagore is another 
Municipal Corporation, with a population of 1.5 lakh but it does not meet the 
criteria of 5 lakh of a Municipal Corporation. So, this weakens the integrity and 
comparability of urban classifications.” 
55. Further, highlighting the executive discretion in declaring an area as urban, the 
Ministry submitted as under:  
“Urban notifications differ across the States, often based on administrative 
discretion rather than uniform legal norms and that undermines the transparency 
and comparability.” 
56. The Ministry has recommended for developing national guidelines that could help 
standardise the declaration of statutory towns and enhance comparability across states. 
The Committee thus enquired whether the Ministry has held any consultation with the 
States about developing national guidelines that could help establish criteria for 
declaration of statutory towns and enhance comparability across states. The Ministry 
replied submitted as follows: 
“As per 12 th Schedule of the Constitution of India, urban planning including town 
planning is a State subject. Further, Article 243Q of the Constitution provides the 
constitutional framework for classification of areas as transitional, smaller urban 
or larger urban areas and vests the authority for such determination with the 
Governor of the State, having regard to factors specified therein such as 
population, density, and non-agricultural employment etc.

Given the diversity of settlements patterns, administrative structures and 
statutory frameworks across States/UTs, the decision regarding declaration of 
statutory towns continues to rest with the respective State Governments.”

(e) Declassification from urban to rural 
 
58. Another issue examined by the Committee relates to declassification of urban 
areas into rural in some of the States. As per one study on the subject, in Tamil Nadu, in 
June 2004, 566 town panchayats (small urban local bodies) were reclassified as rural 
panchayats reportedly due to concerns relating to urban taxation. Declassification from 
urban to rural is possible in Kerela and in Tamil Nadu but not in all other states. When 
the Ministry were asked to give the factual position on this, the Ministry submitted as 
under: 
“As per the provisions of Article 243 W of the Constitution, in conjunction with the 
Seventh and Twelfth Schedules, matters relating to urban development fall within 
the purview of States/Urban Local Bodies. The process of notifying STs rests 
with the State Governments under their respective statutes in line with powers 
conferred under Article 243Q of the Constitution, which empowers the Governor 
to declare an area urban based on factors such as population, density, and non-
agricultural employment etc. Each State/UT enacted or may enact its local self-
government act and framed or may frame their respective rules in line with 
powers conferred under 74 thConstitutional Amendment Act (CAA), 1992, which 
varies across States/UTs.  
For example, in the case of Tamil Nadu, the classification of the Urban Local 
Bodies (ULBs) shall be reviewed every three years, based on the norms 
specified in sub-rule (1) Section 3 of Chapter-II under the Tamil Nadu Urban 
Local Bodies Rules, 2023 framed under Tamil Nadu Urban Local Bodies Act, 
1998, which was enacted in line with the 74 th CAA, 1992. Similarly, the Kerala 
Municipality Act, 1994 (KM Act) was enacted in line with the 74 th CAA, which has 
inclusion and exclusion provisions of municipals and municipal area in Section 4 
of Chapter-II. 
 
Further as per another study on the subject, in West Bengal and in Kerela, the 
two states with the highest number of new Census Towns, the provision available 
in the 74 th amendment to have town panchayat or nagar panchayat for 
transitional urban areas does not exist, which might explain the slow process of 
municipalisation. 
 

 28
The Kerala Municipality Act, 1994 (KM Act) has the provision of Nagar 
Panchayat or Town Panchayat for transitional urban area in Section 4 of 
Chapter-II, while West Bengal as per se has no provision of Nagar 
Panchayat/Town Panchayat for transitional urban area, but has classification of 
municipal areas (5 classification) based on population criteria ranging from ≤ 
25000 to > than 2 lakh according to Section 7 of the West Bengal Municipal Act, 
1993. Further, power to abolish or alter the limits of municipal area are given to 
State Government under Section 9 of the West Bengal Municipal Act, 1993.” 
 
 
(f) Fails to capture the Rural-urban continuum 
59. India’s urbanisation is a ‘rural-urban continuum’; however, existing Census 
definition divides it into a rigid ‘binary’. Because of this, the peri-urban areas in the 
periphery of cities neither belong to cities nor to villages and remain deprived of basic 
infrastructure. The Committee sought to know from the Registrar General and Census 
Commissioner about our policy to address this ‘governance vacuum’. The reply 
received is stated below: 
“This question does not pertain to this office. This office does not define ‘rural’ 
and ‘urban’ areas. For conducting Census, the directorate of Census operations 
obtains list of all administrative unitsfrom each State/UT authority for conducting 
Census.” 
60. When the same question was asked from the Ministry, the written submission 
made is as under: 
 
“As per the provisions of Article 243 W of the Constitution, in conjunction with the 
Seventh and Twelfth Schedules, matters relating to urban development fall within 
the purview of States/Urban Local Bodies. The Government of India supplements 
the efforts of States/UTs through schematic interventions, policy advisories, and 
by providing financial and technical support for planned urban development. The 
Urban and Regional Development Plans Formulation and Implementation 
(URDPFI) Guidelines, 2014 follow the Census classification framework while also 
emphasizing integrated planning for urban agglomerations, metropolitan regions, 
growth corridors and regional development areas. The Guidelines maybe 
adopted by States/UTs in accordance with their respective Town and Country 
Planning Acts and related statutory provisions. This Ministry is also promoting 
GIS-based planning for planning areas covering ULBs, outgrowths and village 
areas within planning boundaries. This approach facilitates geospatial data 
integration and evidence-based planning for the identification, monitoring and 
management of emerging urban growth areas.”

“As the Census exercise is already underway, it is not feasible to introduce a new

(g) Need for smooth Rural-Urban Transition 
 
67. It is seen that due to local resistance, the State Governments are at times 
unwilling to convert of CTs into STs. This local resistance is due to the fear of higher 
local taxation, absence of rural employment and poverty alleviation schemes such as G-
RAM-G, introduction of urban bylaws and planning devices, etc. Cases have been 
reported where the taxes for various services are collected by the ULB without actually 
making those services available to the people. 
 
68. To facilitate a smooth rural-to-urban transition, the Ministry has suggested - 
‘ensuring continuity of local leadership during the conversion of rural areas into urban, 
allowing existing representatives to serve until the end of their tenure. Introduce urban 
taxes, user charges, and bye-laws gradually, while maintaining access to rural schemes 
and benefits during the transition period to prevent disruptions in welfare delivery. 
Establish State- and Local-Level Transition Units to coordinate planning, finance, and 
governance, ensuring a seamless and inclusive shift to urban administration.’ 

 31
 
69. On this suggestion of the Ministry, the Committee enquired whether the Ministry 
has held any consultation with the States regarding the proposal of introducing a 
‘transition period’ and related issues, such as the duration of this transition period, the 
targets to be achieved during this period, nature of collaboration between local 
governing bodies, etc. The reply furnished by the Ministry is as under: 
“As per the 12 th Schedule of the Constitution of India, urban planning including 
town planning is a State subject. The Ministry has engaged with States/UTs 
through various consultations, workshops and technical discussions on issues 
relating to urbanization, planning of emerging settlements and management of 
peri-urban growth. The Urban and Regional Development Plans Formulation and 
Implementation (URDPFI) Guidelines, 2014 advocate a hierarchical planning 
framework comprising Regional Plans, Metropolitan Plans, District Plans and 
Development Plans to facilitate orderly development of areas experiencing rapid 
urbanization. 
The Ministry has not prescribed any uniform national ‘transition period’ for 
Census Towns or other transitional settlements. Given the diversity in 
demographic characteristics, growth patterns, governance structures and 
statutory frameworks across States/UTs, a standard transition timeline may not 
be appropriate. Instead, the Ministry encourages States/UTs to adopt a phased 
and context-specific approach to planning and infrastructure development 
through appropriate statutory planning instruments, including Regional Plans and 
Development Plans, while ensuring coordination among relevant local bodies 
and planning authorities.” 
70. The Committee further enquired whether the Ministry has conducted any detailed 
study on the ensuring a smooth rural-to-urban transition. The response received is as

“The Ministry and its technical organizations, including the Town and Country 
Planning Organisation (TCPO), have undertaken studies, and consultations from 
time to time on urbanization trends, spatial planning, urban-rural linkages and 
management of emerging settlements. The findings generally emphasize 
integrated planning, provision of infrastructure, strengthening of local institutions, 
adoption of GIS-based planning tools, and coordinated development of urban-
rural regions to facilitate smooth transition from rural to urban areas.” 
71. During the Sitting on the subject, the Committee were apprised that the Ministry 
are separately working with the Ministry of Rural Development to provide urban quality 
of life in non-urban areas located along the eight-nine broad corridors of urbanisation. 

 32
The Ministry was asked to elaborate on the proposal and the nature of collaboration 
envisaged between the two Ministries. The submission made by the Ministry is as under: 
“There are 4041 Statutory Towns (STs) and 3892 Census Towns (CTs) as per 
Census 2011. Census towns are those villages / Panchayats having a population 
of 5000 and above, at least 75% of male main working population are employed 
in non-agricultural activities and density of population is at least 400 persons per 
sq. km. However, Census Towns are urban in nature (high density of population). 
Estimated urban population in 2025 is 45-50 crore which is expected to increase 
to 85-87 crore by 2047.  This means an additional urban population of nearly 40 
crore people over next 20 years would lead to high density urban areas. This 
would guide the urban development in two ways - (i) Retrofitting of inner-city core 
areas / Brownfield development and (ii) Greenfield development – which would 
be happening in expanding peripheral areas of cities and rural areas. The 
Greenfield development is expected to cluster along the economic / transit 
corridors like high-speed rail networks, regional rapid transit systems (RRTS), 
and major expressways. This would bring urban amenities such as water supply, 
sanitation & sewerage management, buses for transport services etc. to non-

(h) Extension of Central Government Schemes to Census Towns and peri-
urban areas 
72. During examination of the subject, the Committee noted that the Census Towns

OBSERVATIONS/RECOMMENDATIONS 
 
1.  Review and Rationalization of the Definition of ‘Urban’ in India  
India is experiencing one of the fastest urban transitions globally. The 
urban population is projected to grow from 377 million (31%) in 2011 to 876 
million (53%) by 2050. This urban population is concentrated in merely about 3% 
of total land area of the country. Along with rapid urbanisation, there has also 
been a continuous rural urban migration which is also adding to the urban 
congestion. It is in this backdrop that the Committee have examined the definition 
of ‘urban’.  
The Committee, having heard the different stakeholder on the subject, 
acknowledge that the existing definition of ‘urban’ needs revisiting as suggested 
by the Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs to capture the evolving nature and 
actual scale of urbanization in India and to enable timely policy interventions. 
Interestingly, the definition of ‘urban’ in India operates through two distinct 
dimensions. One emanating from the Census which provides the demographic 
demarcation of urban areas and the other gives the statutory administrative 
demarcation of urban areas flowing from the State Municipal Acts or orders.It is 
not that the ‘Census Towns’ are invariably converted to ‘Statutory Towns’. 
Consequently, 14.4 percent of India’s urban population out of the total urban 
population of 37.7 crore in 2011 continue to remain under the administrative 
control of Rural Local bodies despite being classified as ‘urban’ population under 
the Census. 
The Registrar General and Census Commissioner of India deposed before the 
Committee that this oﬀice is at advance stage regarding preparation for Census 
2027. The freezing date for all administrative units i.e. 01.01.2026 has already 
been communicated to all States/UTs so that list of all administrative units can be 
finalized well before the start of first phase of Census 2027. As it may a ﬀect the 

 43
on-going Census preparatory work, it will be difficult to change the definition of 
Census town, at this moment. The Committee agree with the view and feel that at 
this juncture while the Census 2027 exercise has already begun, it would not be 
advisable to suggest changes to the criteria set for defining ‘Census Towns’ 
which pre-dates to 1981 and falls short of addressing the current dynamics of 
urbanization.  
Further, in the process of examination of the subject, the Committee also 
observed that the Census Towns recognized by the Census of India are neither 
governed by Urban Local Bodies nor automatically covered under the Schemes 
and Programmes of MoHUA. Nonetheless, the Census data forms the primary and 
authentic data which assists in gauging both actual urbanization and its trends in 
India.  
The Committee, thus, observe that the issue of defining urban areas cannot be 
treated merely as a statistical exercise. It must be examined from the perspective 
of social justice, gender equality, decentralisation and the constitutional rights of 
ordinary people.  
2. Making the definition of Census Towns ‘gender inclusive’ 
The existing criteria for identifying Census Towns, particularly the requirement 
that75 per cent of the male main working population should be engaged in non-
agricultural activities, require urgent reconsideration. According to the Census 
Commissioner of India, the proportion of female workers engaged in non-
agricultural activities is not used as a criterion for classifying 'census towns' 
because female workforce participation fluctuates significantly due to social 
factors, under-reporting, and the informal nature of the work. Consequently, it is 
not considered a reliable indicator for identifying urban characteristics. Instead, 
the share of male main workers engaged in non-agricultural activities is regarded 
as a more stable and clearer indicator. Moreover, the proportion of women in non-
agricultural work is much lower than that of men. If this were included as a 

 44
criterion, the number of census towns would decrease further due to the low 
number of women engaged in non-agricultural employment. 
The Committee are of the view that this criterion is based on a male-centric 
understanding of work and does not adequately reflect the growing participation 
of women in economic activities. It also fails to capture the contribution of 
women engaged in self-employment, informal work, home-based work, service-
sector activities and other forms of employment that are often undercounted. 
Female labour force participation has been steadily increasing. As per the data 
referred to in the Committee’s discussions, female Labour Force Participation 
Rate for persons aged 15 years and above stood at 34.8 per cent in August, 2026. 
In this context, continuing with a criterion based exclusively on male main 
workers would not reflect the changing socio-economic realities of India. 
The Ministry are therefore advised to commission a focused study to revise the 
gender-biased criterion and develop a gender-inclusive methodology for 
identifying urban areas. The revised methodology should recognise the entire 
workforce, including women workers, self-employed persons, informal workers, 
home-based workers, migrant workers and those engaged in emerging service 
and digital-sector activities. A gender audit of Census definitions and data 
collection procedures may also be undertaken to identify and address systematic 
undercounting of women’s work. 
3. Emphasis on conversion of eligible Census Towns into Statutory Towns  
As per the Census 2011 data, there were 2576 new Census Towns, thereby taking 
the total count of Census Towns to 3,892. Out of this, only 175 Census Towns 
have been notified into Statutory Towns, 367 Census Towns merged with existing 
Statutory Towns and 12 Census Towns of NCT Delhi will be treated as part of 
Statutory Towns in the ongoing census exercise for census purposes. Therefore, 
as of now, there are more than 3,000 Census Towns that continue to remain 

 45
under the administrative control of Rural Local Body (RLB) despite exhibiting 
urban characteristics as per the criteria prescribed by the RGI&CC. 
The Committee observe that Census Towns represent settlements which have 
undergone significant socio-economic transformation but continue to be 
governed through rural administrative structures unless notified as Statutory 
Towns by the respective State Governments. This difference between 
demographic recognition and administrative status deprives them of planned 
urban development, enhanced revenues and more particularly, timely 
intervention in terms of Central schemes and funds.  
The Committee note that in 2016, the then Secretary of the Ministry of Urban 
Development has requested the Chief Secretaries of all the States to take 
immediate and necessary action for conversion of 3,784 Census Towns into 
Statutory Urban Local Bodies to promote planned urban development, 
emphasizing that the opportunity of planned urban development, and had 
highlighted delayed action may result in unplanned construction and ad hoc 
infrastructure development.  
The Committee, therefore, are of the strong opinion that more and more Census 
Towns be recognized as ‘Statutory Towns’ so that urban amenities could be 
extended to these Towns. Conversion of Census Towns into Statutory Towns is 
also needed to check the rural-urban migration. The Committee, thus, 
recommend that after the release of Census 2027 data, the Ministry should again 
engage with States/UTs to examine the feasibility of converting eligible Census 
Towns into Statutory Urban Local Bodies so that planned urban development, 
infrastructure provisioning and effective delivery of services can be ensured. The 
thrust should be on developing new Towns and Model Township on the outskirts 
of city. The Committee further recommend that an updated database regarding 
the status of conversion of Census Towns into Statutory Towns may be 
maintained in coordination with States/UTs. 

 46
4. Consultation with States/UTs to rationalize criteria for ‘Statutory Towns’ 
A Statutory Town refers to any administrative unit legally defined as urban—such 
as a Municipal Corporation, Municipality, Cantonment Board, Notified Town Area 
Committee, Town Panchayat, or Nagar Palika—regardless of its demographic 
characteristics. Article 243Q of the Indian Constitution empowers the Governor to 
declare an area as ‘urban’ based on factors such as population, density, and non-
agricultural employment, etc. The discretionary power of the Governor and the 
absence of legislation determining the criteria for declaring ‘municipality’ in 
several States has resulted in a discretionary and non-standardised approach to 
recognising urban settlements. The States do not follow one criterion for 
declaring an area as ‘urban’; the criteria vary from State to State. 
The Committee observe that States follow different criteria for declaring Statutory 
Towns, while some States prescribed parameters relating to population density, 
economic activity or revenue generation, others provide greater discretion to 
administrative authorities. Such variations have resulted in inconsistent 
recognition of urban settlements across States.  
The Committee trust that where the Census definition is largely seen as an 
indicator of urbanisation, it is actually the statutory definition of ‘urban’ which 
counts for administrative and policy purposes. Therefore, it is the administrative 
definition of ‘urban’ that requires greater scrutiny, rationalization and review in 
order to gauge the actual urbanization in India, and for ensuring effective 
governance and policy implementation. 
The Committee therefore, are of the view that such wide variation in the 
parameters adopted by the States for determining a ‘Statutory Town’ affect the 
true depiction of urbanisation in India and may create challenges in planning and 
programme implementation. At the same time, a uniform national threshold may 
not adequately reflect the diverse settlement patterns and socio-economic 
realities of different States. The criteria should therefore be flexible, evidence-

 47
based and sensitive to regional conditions without compromising transparency 
and comparability. 
The Committee, thus recommend that the Ministry must initiate consultations 
with all the States and Union Territories to explore the feasibility of developing 
national guidelines that could help rationalise the criteria for notifying ‘Statutory 
Towns’ and enhance comparability across states, while giving due regard to the 
constitutional powers of States in matters relating to urban governance. 
5. Recognizing Census Towns as ‘transitional areas’ 
The Registrar General & Census Commissioner of India informed the Committee 
that Census Town serve as an indicator of pre-urbanization, marking a transition 
phase from rural to urban characteristics. Further, Census Towns (CTs) help the 
Census of India capture the real extent of urban growth in the country revealing 
the hidden dimension of India’s urbanization, beyond the officially recognized 
municipalities and corporations. 
 As regards consideration of Census Towns as peri-urban/transitional areas, the 
Ministry has informed that the present urbanisation pattern of India encompasses 
large metropolitan centres, medium and small towns, peri-urban settlements, and 
“rurban” areas. The governance of these settlements also varies, spanning both 
formal urban governance and rural governance systems. Census Towns can be 
considered as peri-urban/transitional areas.  
 
The Committee also agree and are of the view that the Census Towns should be 
treated as ‘transitional areas’ for administrative purposes. The Committee, 
therefore, recommend that the Ministry should after consulting all the 
stakeholders, take the initiative of declaring the Census Towns as ‘transitional 
areas’ so that they get the required policy focus and intervention.  
 
 

 48
6. Need for guidelines for smooth rural-urban transition 
The Committee note that there is considerable local resistance to the conversion 
of Rural Local Bodies into Urban Local Bodies, primarily due to apprehensions 
regarding higher local taxation, loss of access to rural employment and poverty 
alleviation schemes such as G-RAM-G, and the introduction of urban bye-laws, 
planning regulations and other statutory requirements. Consequently, State 
Governments may, at times, be reluctant to convert Census Towns into Statutory 
Towns. The Committee have also been apprised of instances where Urban Local 
Bodies (ULBs) have levied and collected taxes for various services without 
ensuring the actual provision of such services to the people. 
To address these concerns and facilitate a smooth and orderly rural-to-urban 
transition, the Committee agree with the suggestion of the Ministry regarding the 
continuity of local leadership during the process of conversion of rural areas into 
urban areas, including allowing the existing elected representatives to continue 
in office until the completion of their tenure. The Committee further recommend 
that urban taxes, user charges and bye-laws be introduced in a phased manner, 
while ensuring continued access to rural schemes and benefits during the 
transition period so as to avoid any disruption in the delivery of welfare services. 
The Committee also recommend the establishment of State- and Local-Level 
Transition Units to coordinate planning, financing and governance, to facilitate a 
seamless, inclusive and well-managed transition to urban administration. 
The Ministry has further submitted that given the diversity in demographic 
characteristics, growth patterns, governance structures and statutory 
frameworks across States/UTs, prescribing a uniform transition timeline may not 
be appropriate for Census Towns and other transitional settlements. The Ministry 
has accordingly encouraged States/UTs to adopt a phased and context-specific 
approach to planning and infrastructure development through appropriate 
statutory planning instruments, including Regional Plans and Development Plans, 

 49
while ensuring effective coordination among the concerned local bodies and 
planning authorities. 
While appreciating the suggestions made by the Ministry, the Committee are of 
the view that, in light of the tardy pace of conversion of Census Towns into 
Statutory Towns, with several such conversions remaining pending for decades, 
it would not be advisable to leave the ‘transition period’ undefined or entirely to 
the discretion of the States/UTs. The Committee, therefore, recommend that the 
Ministry, in consultation with the States and Union Territories, devise an 
overarching framework prescribing broad, step-by-step phases for the transition 
of a Rural Local Body into an administratively ‘urban’ area. Each phase should be 
clearly defined and measurable so that the progress of transition can be 
objectively assessed and quantified. The framework should also prescribe a 
maximum time period within which the transition is to be completed and it should 
also be ensured that this transition is accomplished before the next Census 
exercise. The Committee further emphasise that there should be active 
coordination between the State Government and the local self-governing bodies 
and constant monitoring by the Centre so that India’s urbanisation is achieved in 
the desired manner.  
7. Developing a time-bound national frameworkfor the planned development 
of Census Towns and peri-urban regions 
Another serious concern of the Committee is the exclusion of Census Towns and 
peri-urban settlements from the institutional and financial framework of urban 
governance. The Committee note that the Census Towns and peri-urban areas are 
also facing urban challenges such as traffic congestion, inadequate waste 
management and water shortages, much like any Statutory Town or metropolitan 
area. However, from an administrative perspective, only ‘Statutory Towns’ and 
their population are recognised as ‘urban’. Consequently, the coverage of various 
schemes of the Ministry relating to urban infrastructure and development is 
largely confined to Statutory Towns administered by Urban Local Bodies (ULBs). 

 50
Census Towns are, therefore, often deprived of the comprehensive urban 
planning, financial resources and technical capacities required to address the 
challenges associated with rapid urbanisation. The absence of timely and 
integrated planning may result in haphazard and unplanned growth, leading to 
housing congestion, inefficient use of resources, inadequate waste management, 
pollution, serious service gaps, inequality in access to basic facilities and other 
associated urban challenges. 
The Ministry, in this regard, informed the Committee that urban governance is 
generally confined to statutory municipal limits, thereby leaving Census Towns, 
peri-urban areas and growth corridors—where a substantial proportion of urban 
expansion takes place—outside a coordinated urban governance framework. The 
inconsistent recognition of urban areas across States also affects scheme 
eligibility, distribution of funds and programme design, resulting in Census 
Towns, peri-urban areas and transitional settlements being left outside several 
major urban development programmes. The Committee note that all schemes, 
missions and programmes of the Ministry are not automatically applicable to 
Census Towns, as the eligibility of an area for assistance under any particular 
scheme is determined by the objectives, scope and operational guidelines of that 
scheme. Since Census Towns continue to be administered by Panchayati Raj 
Institutions unless they are notified as Statutory Towns, their eligibility for 
various schemes is accordingly determined in accordance with the provisions of 
the respective schemes. 
The Committee observe that the Ministry has, however, extended the restructured 
PM SVANidhi Scheme to Census Towns. In Census Towns, eligible street vendors 
are identified and Letters of Recommendation (LoRs) are issued by the Block 
Development Officer (BDO), thereby facilitating the effective implementation of 
the Scheme. 
The Committee opine that the present approach risks creating a situation where 
the Government recognises urbanisation in official statistics but does not extend 

 51
corresponding governance structures, public services and financial support to 
the people living in these settlements. Such an approach will deepen the crisis of 
unplanned urbanisation and place an unfair burden on working-class and low-
income households. 
In this regard, the Committee are of the view that the approach adopted under the 
restructured PM SVANidhi Scheme provides a useful precedent for extending the 
benefits of urban development interventions to Census Towns. The Committee, 
therefore, recommend that the Ministry, in consultation with the States and UTs, 
develop a time-bound national framework for the planned development of Census 
Towns and peri-urban regionswherein rural-urban linkages are closely integrated 
so that these regions are developed as a ‘Visionary Towns’ with all basic urban 
infrastructure. Such a framework should provide for transitional administrative 
arrangements and dedicated financial assistance. Urban classification should not 
become a pretext for denying welfare benefits. Census Towns and peri-urban 
settlements must be made eligible for relevant urban development programmes, 
including housing, sanitation, drinking water, public transport, livelihood support 
and climate-resilient infrastructure. The Ministry must ensure that statistical 
recognition of urbanisation is accompanied by financial devolution and 
institutional responsibility. 
8. Need to device procedure for conducting inter-censal surveys 
The Committee note that the Census is a decennial exercise and that a period of 
ten years is sufficiently long for cities and settlements to undergo significant 
demographic, spatial and socio-economic transformation. The Ministry has, 
therefore, suggested before the Committee the introduction of periodic inter-
censal surveys to capture rapid changes in urbanisation and emerging settlement 
patterns, as well as to address data gaps in the targeting, implementation and 
monitoring of various schemes and programmes. The Committee further note 
that limited availability of granular, geo-coded and disaggregated data poses 
challenges for effective programme design and evidence-based urban planning. 

 52
The Ministry has accordingly emphasised that inter-censal surveys would provide 
timely and updated information, thereby enabling more responsive policy 
formulation, programme design and urban governance. 
Regarding the modalities for conducting such surveys, the Ministry stated that 
being a user of Census data, it does not undertake such surveys, which are 
generally conducted by the Office of the Registrar General and Census 
Commissioner of India (RGI), the Ministry of Statistics and Programme 
Implementation (MoSPI) or other data-generating Ministries/Departments. The 
Registrar General and Census Commissioner of India (RGI) has, however, 
informed the Committee that its primary mandate is to conduct the Census and 
discharge related statutory functions. It has further stated that the proposal for 
conducting periodic inter-censal surveys to capture rapid urban changes is a 
policy matter which may be examined separately by the Government, and that 
such surveys would be distinct from the Census in terms of their objectives, 
methodology, coverage and legal framework. 
The Ministry has further suggested that the feasibility and modalities of 
conducting inter-censal surveys may be deliberated upon by the concerned 
Ministries/Departments and organisations, including: 
(a) Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation (MoSPI); 
(b) Ministry of Rural Development (MoRD); 
(c) Office of the Registrar General and Census Commissioner of India; 
(d) Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO); 
(e) State Departments of Economics and Statistics (DES); and 
(f)State Urban Development Departments/Directorates of Municipal 
Administration. 
The Committee welcome the suggestion of the Ministry for undertaking periodic 
inter-censal surveys and recognise the importance of timely, reliable and 
disaggregated data for effective urban planning, policy formulation and 
implementation of development programmes. The Committee, therefore, 
recommend that the Ministry, in consultation with the Office of the Registrar 
General and Census Commissioner of India and other concerned 

 53
Ministries/Departments and organisations, examine the feasibility of instituting 
periodic inter-censal surveys for capturing changes in urbanisation, emerging 
settlement patterns and other relevant urban indicators. The Committee further 
recommend that, based on the outcome of such consultations, the Ministry take 
necessary steps for establishing an appropriate institutional mechanism and 
modalities for undertaking such surveys, so as to ensure that urban planning and 
policy interventions are informed by timely and updated data. 
9. Need for consultation and coordination between MoHUA and RGI over the 
definition of ‘Census Towns’ 
The Committee note that, in 2024, the Registrar General and Census 
Commissioner of India (RGI) held consultations with the Ministry of Housing and 
Urban Affairs (MoHUA) and the Ministry of Labour & Employment (MoL&E) 
regarding the criteria for defining ‘Census Towns’ and the broader definition of 
‘urban’ areas. MoHUA communicated its suggestions to the RGI; however, the 
RGI observed that the Ministry had not furnished specific objective criteria in this 
regard. Thereafter, MoHUA undertook a series of consultations with experts 
during 2024 and 2025 on the subject. The Committee note, however, that the RGI 
was not part of these consultations, nor were the outcomes thereof formally 
communicated to the RGI along with specific and objectively defined suggestions. 
The Committee are of the view that the issue of defining ‘Census Towns’ has 
wider implications and, therefore, warrants comprehensive consultation among 
all relevant stakeholders. The Committee also consider it important that the 
States/UTs, being directly concerned with the planning and administration of 
urban and transitional settlements, are suitably involved in the process. The 
Committee recognise that any immediate amendment to the existing criteria for 
defining ‘Census Towns’ may not be feasible. The Committee, therefore, 
recommend that the Ministry establish an appropriate institutional platform 
involving the RGI, States/UTs and other relevant stakeholders to facilitate 
structured and comprehensive deliberations on the subject. Such a platform 
should endeavour to evolve objective, transparent and mutually acceptable 

 54
criteria for the identification and classification of ‘Census Towns’, which may be 
considered for adoption in future Censuses.

Registrar General and Census Commissioner, Ministry of Home Affairs were called for a 
briefing on the subject ‘Census Criteria for defining Urban areas’. Welcoming the 
representatives of the Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs and Ministry of Home Affairs, the 
Chairperson highlighted the definitional challenge of various terms used in India’s Urban 
framework such as statutory towns, census towns, outgrowth areas etc. which are often used 
interchangeably but has specific meaning and administrative implication in developmental 
planning and funding. The Chairperson also sought to know the rationale for retaining the 
same criteria for defining ‘Urban areas’ as was followed for Census 2011 and wondered 
whether the said criteria needs to be revisited to align with contemporary urbanization trends 
& economic realities.

Committee on the subject through a power point presentation. The power point presentation 
highlighted the importance of definition the term ‘Urban’, the existing definition of urban 
areas, international practices for defining urban areas and alternative approaches, India’s 
urban scenarios under changed criteria, key issues related to existing urban definition and 
steps proposed to be taken for strengthening the existing urban definition leading to improved 
governance systems and programme implementation.

towns.
