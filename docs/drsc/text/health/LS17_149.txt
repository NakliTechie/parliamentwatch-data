PREFACE 
 
I, the Chairman of the Department -related Parliamentary Standing Committee on Health 
and Family Welfare, having been authorised by the Committee to present the Report on its behalf, 
present this One-Hundred Forty-Ninth Report on the subject “Implementation of Pradhan Mantri 
TB Mukt Bharat Abhiyan” (PMTBMBA) which was adopted by the Committee in its meeting held 
on the 31st August 2023. 
2. Even after advancements in medical research, diagnosis, and treatment of various 
infectious diseases, Tuberculosis (TB) is still a global epidemic. The disease accounts for one of 
the leading infectious killers after Covid-19. The cause of concern is despite the fact that there has 
been a decline in the occurrence of TB in some parts of the world, Western Europe in particular, 
India still contributes approximately one -fourth of the global TB cases. The primary objective 
behind identifying the subject, “Implementation of Pradhan Mantri TB Muk t Bharat Abhiyan 
(PMTBMBA)” by the Committee was, therefore, to examine the burden, prevalence  and impact 
of TB in India, the progress made in achieving the global and national targets to end TB, review 
of National policies to control TB, challenges being faced in tackling spread of TB, review and 
significance of PMTBMBA, and progress made in research related to TB in the country. 
3. The Committee held deliberations on the subject with the Ministry of Health and Family 
Welfare, the Ministry of Ayush, the Depar tment of Health Research, the Department of 
Biotechnology, the National Institute of TB and Respiratory Diseases (NITRD), the New Delhi,

the NGO - Collaboration to Eliminate Tuberculosis among Indians (CETI), Indore, Madhya 
Pradesh. The Committee conducted local visits on 9 th May, 2023 to AIIMS, New Delhi and 
NITRD, New Delhi, to review the progress made under PMTBMBA. The Committee also visited 
Tezpur, Guwahati, Kolkata and Hyderabad in January, 2023 to gather first -hand information on

CHAPTER I 
 
INTRODUCTION 
A.  Overview of Tuberculosis (TB) as a global health issue 
1.1 Tuberculosis (TB) is a communicable infectious disease. It is one of the 
leading causes of death worldwide, making it a critical global health concern for all 
age groups. In 1882, Robert Koch identified the tubercle bacillus, also called 
Mycobacterium tuberculosis, as the etiological agent of TB. It is a highly contagious 
airborne disease that spreads through droplet infection that commonly affects the 
lungs, causing pulmonary tuberculosis and other sites outside the lungs, constituting 
extra-pulmonary tuberculosis. The asymptomatic status of the dormant bacteria 
escalates to the transmissible, active form when the host’s immune system becomes 
debilitated. 
 
1.2 As per WHO Global TB Report 2022, around one -quarter of the world’s 
population (2 billion) is latently infected with TB, and it is the 13th leading cause of 
death and the second leading infectious killer after COVID-19. Until the 
COVID-19 pandemic, TB was the leading cause of death from a single infectious 
agent, ranking above HIV/AIDS. In 2021, an estimated 10.6 million people fell ill 
with tuberculosis worldwide. TB -infected individuals develop some signs and 
symptoms for clinical diagnosis. The clinical manifestation of active pulmonary TB 
may include pleuritic chest pain, low -grade fever, prolonged productive cough, 
fatigue, haemoptysis, loss of appetite, night sweats and weight loss. However, not 
all patients infected with TB  develop the disease. TB dis ease has no boundaries. 
Nevertheless, research over the years has provided valuable insights and treatment 
regimens to combat the disease and measures to eradicate TB worldwide. 
1.3 As per WHO's Global TB Report 2022, TB is a global epidemic strongly 
influenced by social and economic development and health-related risk factors such 
as undernourishment, diabetes, HIV infection, alcohol use disorders and smoking. 
The disease is particularly prevalent in low and middle -income countries, where 
poverty, malnutrition, and limited access to healthcare contribute to its spread. TB 
disproportionately affects vulnerable populations, such as people living with 
HIV/AIDS, those with weakened immune systems, malnourished individuals, those 
in crowded living conditions, and marginalised communities. The disease thrives in 
settings with limited access to healthcare, poor infection control, and inadequate 
resources for diagnosis and treatment. There is a pressing need to address these 
determinants to achieve glo bal targets for reductions in TB disease burden. In this 
regard, it is noted that the numbers of TB cases and TB deaths started to decline in 
Western Europe, North America and some other parts of the world around the turn 
of the 20th century; as incomes grew, levels of poverty fell, and housing and nutrition 
improved. The fastest declines in TB incidence and TB mortality in Western Europe 

2  
occurred in the 1950s and 1960s, in the context of progress towards universal health 
coverage (UHC), rapid social and economic development, and the availability of 
effective drug treatments. UHC is necessary to ensure that all people with disease 
or infection should have access to high -quality treatment. The number of people 
acquiring infection and developing illness (and, in turn, the number of deaths caused 
by TB) can be reduced through multi -sectoral action to address TB determinants 
such as poverty, undernourishment, HIV infection, smoking an d diabetes. As per 
WHO Report 2022, with a recommended standard anti -TB drugs regimen, almost 
85% of patients get cured; however, without treatment death rate from TB disease 
is about 50%. 
 
 
B. TB Burden and Prevalence in India 
 
TB Burden in India 
 
1.4 TB has existed in India for decades. Once considered a poor man’s disease, 
TB is also commonly seen in the affluent class. India continues to be the most 
significant contributor to global TB incidence. India contributes approximately 
one-fourth of the internation al cases, roughly 25 lakhs out of 1.05 crores globally. 
As reported by the WHO Global TB Report 2022, eight (8) out of thirty (30) 
countries with the highest TB burden countries account for more than 2/3 rd of the 
global TB burden and among those eight countries, India’s share is 28%.

F.  TB with Co-morbidities 
 
1.13 The health -related risk factors with TB infection are considered co - 
morbidities like HIV infection, substance abuse, Diabetes mellitus, malnutrition, 
etc., associated with a high risk of poor disease outcomes. These co-morbidities also 
account for missed or delayed TB detection, complicating TB disease. Therefore, 
timely detection and treatment of these co -morbidities is essential to evade their 
adverse impact on TB patients. 
1.14 As per the WHO TB Report 2022, diabetes is associated with a two -to- 
threefold risk of TB disease, & a higher risk of multidrug resistance (MDR -TB). 
Also, people living with HIV are at 18 times higher risk of developing TB than the 
rest of the population. People with TB, along with diabetes, are twice as likely to 
die during TB treatment and have a four -fold risk of TB relapse after treatment 
completion. 
1.15 From the data given by the Ministry about the trend of the number of TB 
patients with co-morbidities of HIV infection and Diabetes in India in the last five 
years, it is observed that TB with Diabetes (TB-DM) is more prevalent as compared 
to TB with HIV cases and have increased significantly over the period of five 
years.

G.  Paediatric TB cases 
 
1.16 As regards Paediatric TB patients, it was noted that TB is now the leading 
cause of death from infectious diseases for children of all ages globally. Children 
are more vulnerable to acquiring TB infection and developing the disease than 
adults, resulting in high morbidity and mortality. As per India TB Report 2023, 
diagnosing TB among children is comparatively more challenging, and diagnostic 
delays are frequent. Hence, many cases are missed, which leads to poor treatment 
outcomes. 
1.17 Infants and young children are at higher risk of developing life -threatening 
forms of TB disease (e.g., disseminated TB, TB meningitis) than older children and 
adults. Although pulmonary TB is the most common form in children, the 
proportion of Extra-pulmonary tuberculosis (EPTB) was between 28-32% consistently. 
Children, especially those under five years, are more vulnerable to TB because of their low 
immunity & under -nutrition and due to diagnostic cha llenges, they may develop severe 
illnesses. 
 
Paediatric TB notification 
1.18 As per the Collaborative Framework to Address the Burden of TB among 
Children & Adolescents, 2021 of the Ministry of Health & Family Welfare, India 
contributes 28% of the global burden  of TB in children up to 14 years of age. It is 
also reported that 6% of the cases reported to the National TB Elimination Program 
(NTEP) are from children up to 14 years of age. The Central TB division report on 
TB in Children, 2021 informs that children may develop disease rapidly with 
atypical symptoms and may also progress to severe illness. It is difficult to diagnose 
TB in children, firstly because the symptoms of TB among children are similar to 
other childhood illnesses and secondly because of difficulties in accessing 
pulmonary samples as children more often swallow sputum. The lower sensitivity 
of microbiological tests in children further adds to this difficulty. In addition to this 
burden, drug-resistant TB for the paediatric age group is another national as well as 
global challenge. The reason for the increase in several such cases is close contact 
with confirmed multidrug-resistant TB persons rather than inadequate TB treatment. 
1.19 Incidence of Paediatric  TB also indicates that there is a gap of more than 
50% in TB notifications in the paediatric age group. It is noted that to address the 
gaps in paediatric TB coverage, the NTEP has collaborated with Child Health and 
Adolescent Health programmes of the Min istry of Health and Family Welfare 
(MoHFW) that are Rashtriya Bal Swasthya Karyakram (RBSK) and Rashtriya 
Kishor Swasthya Karyakram (RKSK). 
1.20 In one of the studies on Global estimates of paediatric TB incidence in 
2013-19 published in the Lancet journal in 2021, it is reported that there are several 

11  
challenges that make it difficult to detect TB in the paediatric population due to 
which many children who develop TB do not receive a prompt and accurate 
diagnosis. It was also highlighted that India, the country  with the single largest TB 
epidemic globally, has a substantial gap in total TB  incidence as reported to WHO 
and estimates of TB incidence as per their study. 
1.21 It is also given to note that diagnostic tools for detecting TB are inadequate. 
Also, in the paediatric population, there is an increasing incidence of 
extra-pulmonary TB and meningeal TB, making the task of definite diagnosis more 
challenging. Further, there is limited data on drug- resistant TB cases in children. 
1.22 The Committee believes that malnourishment is commonly seen among 
the paediatric population, and it is one of the major reasons and risk factors 
for acquiring TB disease in children. Therefore, the Committee feels that 
efforts should be made to fill the gaps in nutrition for this vulnerable 
population. Community involvement in ensurin g the provision of proper 
nutrition among children should be considered in this regard. 
1.23 The Committee believes that creating awareness of the disease and its 
management among the young population would eventually decrease TB 
incidence. The Committee appreciates the integration of TB control activities 
with child health programs to increase awareness about TB among children 
and would like to impress upon the government to monitor these programs 
regularly to identify the implementation challenges and ta ke corrective 
measures immediately. For this purpose, a robust health delivery system  with 
adequate human resources should be made functional on mission mode, and 
proper incentives may be given to these frontline workers who are required to 
go into the fields to track and trace TB cases in remote areas. In addition, 
collaborations with relevant health sectors may be done to tackle the challenges 
faced in detecting, treating, and preventing paediatric TB. 
 
 
H.  Drug Resistant TB cases 
 
1.24 When the TB bacteria become resistant to drugs used to treat TB disease, the 
drugs are no longer effective in killing the bacteria, resulting in Drug -resistant TB. 
Drug-resistant TB has become a public threat. WHO has reported that three 
countries accounted for 42% of global  cases of drug -resistant TB in 2021: India 
(26%), the Russian Federation (8.5%) and Pakistan (7.9%), which suggests that 
India has the highest burden of drug-resistant TB as well. 
 
1.25 As per WHO’s Global TB Report 2022, resistance to Rifampicin, which is 
the most effective first-line drug, has become the greatest concern. Both MDR -TB 
and Rifampicin- resistant TB (RR -TB) require treatment with second -line drugs. 
The burden of drug-resistant TB (DR-TB) is estimated to have increased between 

12  
2020 and 2021, with 450000 new cases of Rifampicin -resistant TB. This increase 
is due to the overall increase in TB incidence between 2020 and 2021, which is 
estimated to have been caused by the impact  of the Covid -19 pandemic on TB 
detection. In 2021, the estimated proportion of people with TB who had MDR/RR- 
TB was 3.6% among new cases and 18% among those previously treated. 
1.26 In India, Drug -resistant TB cases have been detected for decades. The 
increasing burden of DR -TB cases has brought new challenges to TB control and 
treatment. As per the Global TB Report 2021, the estimated number of MDR and 
XDR-TB cases that have been put on treatment were 4 per 100,000 and 1 per 
100,000 population, respectively. During the pandemic, a significant reduction was 
observed in the total number of DR -TB patients. Similarly, higher reductions were 
also seen in the number of XDR-TB patients being started on treatment in 2020 and 
2021. Reversals in progress in the number o f people enrolled on MDR/XDR -TB 
treatment means that the gaps have widened for eliminating TB. As per the 
following data given by the Ministry, it is observed that a total number of 63801 
MDR/RR patients were diagnosed in 2022, out of which 90.5 % of cases have been 
started with appropriate treatment.

I. Impact of Covid-19 on Tuberculosis burden, diagnosis and treatment 
 
1.30 During the year 2020, to achieve the goal of ending TB by 2025, TB 
prevention & control activities were implemented in mission mode. However, the 
unprecedented COVID-19 pandemic substantially disrupted TB healthcare services 
and widely increased the vulnerability of acquiring TB infections. It reversed years 
of progress in the fight against TB by disrupting intensive TB case-finding measures 
and diagnostic services that further widened the gap of missing TB cases. As 
informed by the Ministry of Health & Family Welfare, the Government was on track 
to achieve the SDG targets related to TB till 2019. The TB case notification 
increased from 16.08 lakh s in 2015 to 24.04 lakhs in 2019. However, due to the 
COVID-19 pandemic, there was a 25% decline in TB case notifications as it went 
down from 24.04 lakhs in 2019 to 18.05 lakhs in 2020. 
 
Challenges in TB Service Delivery during the COVID-19 Pandemic 
1.31 The COVID -19 pandemic devastated health systems and public health 
programmes. According to the India TB Report 2021, during the initial months of 
2020, NTEP was uphill, notifying more than 4,11,000 patients. The national 
lockdown resulted in dropped notifi cations by 38%. During the COVID -19 
pandemic, TB notifications decreased due to pandemic -related restrictions on 
population mobility and its impact on the health -seeking behaviour of the 
community. Further, this pandemic also brought along stigma and discr iminatory 
behaviour against people perceived to have been affected with symptoms of any 
respiratory illness or in contact with the affected people. The  reduced access to TB 
services for diagnosis, involvement of TB program officers in COVID-19 activities, 
and difficulty in sample collection led to minimal focus on TB service provisions 
and implementation of activities as planned. Also, TB patients struggled to get 
medicines as they were stranded in different geographic locations due to the 
lockdown, which reduced adherence to TB management. 
1.32 TB and COVID-19 infections primarily affect the lungs. The prevalence of 

14  
TB among COVID-19 patients was found to be 0.37 -4.47 %. On the other hand, a 
history of active TB or latent TB is a significant risk factor for COVID-19 infection 
that not only increases its susceptibility but also leads to rapid and severe symptoms 
and disease progression with poor outcomes. 
1.33 The Committee noted that to mitigate the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic 
on the TB epidemic, the Government stepped up its efforts and implemented a Rapid 
Response Plan that augmented TB surveillance along with a screening of COVID 
cases through an integrated bi -directional screening strategy. The Ministry fur ther 
informed the Committee that the country’s response towards mitigating  the impact 
of COVID -19 on TB was far ahead of any other country globally, which was 
exclusively documented in the WHO’s Global TB Report 2021. The country’s TB 
case notification inc reased to 21.35 lakhs in 2021 (18% increase), while in 2022, 
the country notified 24.2 lakh cases and surpassed pre-COVID levels. The increase 
in the number of TB cases notified under NTEP has been due to the increased efforts 
taken by programmes like active case finding, service delivery through Ayushman 
Bharat Health & Wellness Centre, private sector engagement and targeted activities 
to mitigate the impact of COVID -19. This also indicates the resolute response 
systems the State/UTs built to handle future pandemics like COVID-19 effectively.

K. Financial impact on TB patients 
 
1.38 TB can have significant financial impacts on both individuals and healthcare 
systems. The financial impact of TB is particularly pronounced in low and  
middle-income countries, where the disease is more prevalent and health resources 
are limited. However, even in high- income countries, TB can still create significant 
financial burdens, especially in vulnerable populations and those with limited access 
to healthcare. 
 
1.39 The financial burden arises from various factors associated with TB in terms 
of medical expenses like the cost of diagnosis, treatment and medications, which is 
high in drug -resistant cases, particularly long -term health implications due to 
chronic health issues and relapses; loss of productivity and income due to illness; 
transportation costs particularly for patients living in remote areas; stigma leading 
to dis crimination and social isolation of patients which exacerbate the financial 
impact; missed opportunities for educational, professional and career advancement; 
TB patients requiring additional support from family members leading to increased 
financial pressure; the additional strain on government resources and healthcare 
systems; and expenses related to prevention and control efforts. 
1.40 Poverty is both a risk factor and a consequence of TB. The disease 
disproportionately affects households with low socio -economic status, causing a 
financial burden on them, resulting in a delay in seeking treatment, inadequate 
adherence to treatment and poor treatment outcomes. As per India TB Report 2023, 
in India, around 18% of the general population is experiencing such catastrophic 
healthcare expenditure in general. The End TB Strategy  of WHO and the National 
Strategic Plan (NSP) for TB in India have set a target to eliminate the catastrophic 
cost of TB care. 
1.41 End TB Strategy, as well as NSP, measures the out -of-pocket expenditure 
(OOPE) through the concept of “catastrophic costs”, which is different from 
“catastrophic expenditures”. Catastrophic expenditure focuses on direct medical 
costs only, whereas TB-related catastrophic costs also include indirect costs. As per 
India TB Report 2023, it is found in a Systematic Review (2020) that about 68% of 
Drug-resistant TB affected persons were experiencing catastrophic costs for TB 
care. 
1.42 The Committee noted that the financial burden and out -of-pocket 
expenditure (OOPE) on  TB treatment in India (which includes diagnosis cost, 
treatment cost, and indirect cost) are Rs. 10,263 in the Government sector, 
Rs. 16,849 in the Private sector and Rs. 11,061 in the NGO/Trust sector. 
1.43 The Committee was apprised that the cost for management of Drug sensitive 
TB under the Government sector (public sector plus collaborating private sector) 

17  
costs around Rs. 19000 for  adults and Rs.1300 for pediatric TB for six months of 
duration of treatment,  and average Drug - resistant TB treatment cost under the 
Government is around Rs. 38000 and in 10% of patients it costs around Rs.1.4 
lakhs. In the Private sector, as per the National TB Prevalence Survey (2021), the 
average treatment cost for TB patients in the private sector is around 
Rs.8595 (Rs.1000 - Rs.15000). 
1.44 PMTBMBA provides community support for TB patients and circumvents 
the financial impact of the disease on the patients and their families. To reduce the 
OOPE for TB cases, the measures taken under NTEP are as follows: 
1. Free diagnostic and treatment services provided to the patients in  all public 
health facilities and participating private health facilities like private 
hospitals, labs and chemists; 
2. Private sector engagement to increase access o f free drugs and diagnostics 
to the patients seeking care in the private sector; 
3. Decentralising TB screening services to the AB -HWC, closer to the 
community; 
4. Incentive of Rs 500 per month for the entire duration of treatment; 
5. Incentive of Rs 750 (one time)  to TB patients for travel support in tribal, 
hilly and rugged terrains; and 
6. Linkage with other social protection schemes at the State/District level. 
1.45 The Committee observes that the most commonly affected age group 
with TB disease is the one who is the earning member of the family. It is also 
observed that all the steps under NTEP are patient -centric and focus on their 
diagnosis and treatment. The Committee is of the view that taking care of TB 
patients in a family takes a toll on the physical, mental an d financial health of 
all family members as they have to accompany the patient to & fro clinics or 
hospitals for treatment purposes. In this scenario, both the patient and the 
family lose income/wages, which impacts their health as well as socio- economic 
status to a great extent. Therefore, the Committee recommends the 
Government that it is imperative to conduct a survey on the financial impact 
of TB in India which would give a realistic picture of socio -economic status as 
well as the financial burden of T B disease on a family. Such surveys can also 
be combined with other health surveys on a national level so as to assess the 
catastrophic costs related to TB disease. The Government may also explore the 
avenues to integrate such surveys with Ni-kshay portal. 
1.46 The Committee is of the opinion that a survey on financial impact of TB 
disease would help to evaluate the impact of existing measures taken under 

18  
NTEP to reduce catastrophic costs on TB patients & their families and would 
help the Government to review the policies strategically and to implement 
evidence-based interventions as per the district or state-specific requirements. 
Such survey would also address the socio-economic determinants of TB disease 
at an early stage and would aid in tracking the progress towards the goal of 
achieving zero catastrophic costs due to TB.

A.  Chronology of National TB control policies 
 
2.1 Following the success of domiciliary treatment for TB in the 1950s, the 
Government of India adopted the National TB Control Programme (NTP) in the year 
1962 to control and prevent tuberculosis in the country, which was primarily based 
on the use of Mass Miniature Radiography (MMR) for diagnosis and treatment of 
cases, in addition to BCG vaccination. This program focused on providing diagnosis 
and treatment services through government health facilities. In the 1980s and early 
1990s, it was realised that the programme suffered from general loopholes & 
challenges, including managerial inadequacies. 
 
2.2 Subsequently, in 1997, the Government of India adopted the Revised 
National TB Control Programme (RNTCP) to strengthen and expand the efforts of 
the NTP. The RNTCP was based primarily on sputum examination for diagnosis, 
and the Directly Observed Treatment Short -course (DOTS) strategy involved 
providing supervised treatment to TB patients. The programme was expanded to all 
the districts of the country & based on the lessons learnt from  the implementation 
of the programme over the previous 12 -year period; from 2006 onwards, RNTCP 
Phase II was developed with an aim to widen services both in terms of activities and 
access and to sustain the achievements in order to achieve the TB-related targets set 
by the Millennium Development Goals for 2015. The design of the RNTCP II 
remained almost the  same as that of RNTCP I, but additional requirements of 
quality-assured diagnosis and treatment were built in through schemes to increase 
the participation of private sector providers and the inclusion of DOTS+ for MDR 
TB and XDR TB. 
 
2.3 In the year 2015, it was realised that several missing cases of TB were not 
being diagnosed, and the emerge nce of Drug -Resistant TB posed new challenges. 
In order to deal with these challenges, in 2017, the National Strategic Plan for 
Tuberculosis Elimination 2017-2025 was developed for the control and elimination 
of TB in India by 2025, five years ahead of glo bal targets. The National Strategic 
Plan (NSP) sets out the strategic direction & key initiatives based upon 
Detect-Treat-Prevent-Build to achieve the goal of eliminating TB by 2025, which 
the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare will undertake from 2017 to 2025. The 
four pillars of the National Strategic Plan, namely Detect-Treat-Prevent-Build, can 
be understood as follows: 
 
Detect: Diagnose TB early and completely by identifying individuals 

20  
vulnerable to TB in the community; actively searching for TB periodically 
among the vulnerable; providing universal access to TB diagnosis and Drug 
Susceptibility Testing; deploying robust specimen collection and 
transportation mechanisms. 
 
Treat: Treat TB correctly and completely by following Drug Susceptibility 
Testing guided regimen for treatment; detecting and managing co - 
morbidities among TB  patients; promoting treatment support groups for 
social support during treatment; effective management of drug resistance  
through indoor management of resistant cases till first negative smear or 
culture report; and thorough judicious use of anti -TB drugs by peer/social 
audit. 
 
Prevent: Stop new TB infections through airborne infection control in 
health facilities, households & community and early detection and treatment 
of cases; prevent active breakdown of TB disease among the infected by 
detecting and managing co-morbidities; by managing risk factors including 
smoking and substance abuse; by preventing & managing under-nutrition; 
and by detecting & treating latent TB infection. 
 
Build: Reforms in the health system by capacity building of the health 
system; strengthening State and District TB Cell; and developing pathways 
for private sector participation; multi -sectoral engagement for TB 
elimination by the creation of TB elimination board at UT, district and 
panchayat/village levels; by engagement with various ministries to address 
TB elimination among tribals and mobile populations. 
 
This plan aimed to strengthe n diagnosis and treatment services, enhance 
surveillance, promote research and innovation, and address social determinants of 
TB. 
 
2.4 In January 2020, the programme was renamed as National Tuberculosis 
Elimination Programme (NTEP) under the umbrella of the National Health Mission 
(NHM) in line with the vision of the Government of India to achieve a rapid decline 
in the burden of TB, morbidity and mortality while working towards ending TB in 
India by 2025, wherein free diagnostic and  quality assured treatment are provided 
to TB patients of all forms (both Pulmonary and Extra Pulmonary TB). It is 
responsible for carrying out the Government of India’s five -year National Strategic 
Plans for TB elimination in a mission mode along with the States/UTs. Its multi - 
pronged approach aimed to detect all TB patients with an emphasis on reaching TB 
patients seeking care from private providers and undiagnosed TB in high -risk 
populations. Universal Drug Susceptibility Testing (UDST) is implemented under 

21  
the NTEP to ensure every diagnosed TB patient is tested to rule out drug resistance 
before or at the time of treatment initiation itself. 
 
2.5 With the goal of achieving  Sustainable Development Goals related to TB 
by 2025, the key activities implemented under the National TB Elimination 
Programme are: 
i. State and District Specific Strategic plan for targeted interventions 
in high-burden areas; 
ii. Provision of free drugs and diagnostics to TB patients, including for 
drug-resistant TB; 
iii. Active TB case-finding campaign in key vulnerable and co -morbid 
populations; 
iv. Integration with Ayushman Bharat  - Health & Wellness Centres to 
decentralise screening and treatment services closer to the 
community; 
v. Private sector engagement, including incentives for notification and 
management of TB cases; 
vi. Scale-up of molecular diagnostic laboratories to sub-district levels; 
vii. Ni-kshay Poshan Yojana for nutritional support to TB patients; 
viii. Intensified IEC campaigns to reduce stigma, raise community 
awareness and improve health-seeking behaviour; 
ix. Multi-sectoral response with involvement of line ministries; 
x. Scale-up TB preventive therapy to contacts of pulmonary TB; 
xi. Tracking of notified TB cases through a case-based web-based portal 
Ni- kshay. 
 
2.6 In spite of the above programs and strategies, the concern was that India 
remains among the list of high-burden countries as per WHO, which accounts for a 
quarter of the global burden of tuberculosis. The economic burden of TB in terms 
of lives, income and workdays is also substantial. Treatment of TB at times runs 
from six months to two years, and the patients are unable to go to work. TB usually 
affects the most economically productive age group of society, resulting in a 
significant loss of working days and pushing TB patients further into poverty. TB 
is also there in slightly economically well -off people, but even today, TB largely 
remains a disease of the socio -economically disadvantaged groups. The challenge 
of tuberculosis requires a multi-sectoral response to address the social determinants 
like undernutrition, alcoholism, living and working conditions, and an increase in 
access to diagnostic and treatment se rvices. In this regard, it became imperative to 
strengthen collaboration with the community and the existing institutions in the 
society, which can play a critical role in filling gaps and addressing social 
determinants, thereby contributing to achieving the national goal of TB Mukt Bharat 
by 2025, five years ahead of the global targets of 2030. 

22  
2.7 In apropos of the above and to galvanise efforts under the National TB 
Elimination Programme (NTEP) with the goal of 'Ending TB by 2025', the 
Government on 9 th September 2022 launched "Pradhan Mantri TB Mukt Bharat 
Abhiyan" (PMTBMBA) for community support to TB patients and to provide 
people with TB and their families increased nutritional, diagnostic, and vocati onal 
support, delivered within the community. It aims to bring together people from all 
backgrounds and escalate the progress toward TB elimination. 
 
 
B. Pradhan Mantri TB Mukt Bharat Abhiyan (PMTBMBA) and its  
significance in TB elimination 
 
2.8 PMTBMBA aims to provide additional patient support to improve treatment 
outcomes of TB patients through augmenting community involvement and 
leveraging Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) activities. It plays a critical role 
in filling gaps and addressing social determin ants related to management of TB 
disease by strengthening the collaborations with the community and existing 
institutions. This initiative significantly increased the active participation of society 
in the fight against tuberculosis as well as awareness among the public regarding 
tuberculosis, thereby leading to a reduction of stigma. The provision of additional 
socio-economic & nutritional support to the TB patient under PMTBMBA also 
reduced the out- of-pocket expenditure for the family of the TB patient  and better 
treatment outcomes. 
2.9 WHO Report titled ‘Implementing the End TB Strategy: The Essentials’, 
released in 2022, incorporates an integrated plan to end TB, encompassing three 
pillars. The first pillar is ‘Integrated patient-centred care and prevention’, which 
puts people living with TB at the heart of service delivery. The second pillar is ‘Bold 
policies and supportive systems’ that require intense participation across 
Government, communities and private stakeholders. The third is ‘ Intensified 
research and innovation ’, which is critical to breaking the trajectory of the TB 
epidemic and reaching global targets. 
 
2.10 The Committee notes that in India, TB elimination measures align with these 
three pillars of WHO’s integrated plan to end TB. An ambitious NSP 2017-2025 is 
in progress to ensure early detection and availability of free drugs at decentralised 
levels till the primary health centres are closer to the community. Efforts are being 
made to increase awareness among healthcare providers and the public, and highly 
sensitive molecular diagnostic tools are being made available to block levels to 
improve early diagnosis, testing and screening programs.  PMTBMBA aligns with 
the above strategy of WHO to end TB in terms of engaging the community wit h 
government efforts to address the social determinants of TB, ensuring universal 

23

emphasises improved access to quality care and treatment for TB patients,

CHAPTER 3 
 
AVAILABILITY AND AFFORDABILITY OF TB TREATMENT 
 
A.  Screening, Early Detection and Notification of TB cases 
3.1 The primary concern revolves around detecting and timely notification of 
TB cases. With the identification and subsequent reporting of a case, initiating 
treatment is possible. Early detection and notification are critical in India as it 
enables the identification and  isolation of contagious individuals, curtailing the 
further transmission of the bacteria in the community and facilitating the timely 
initiation of treatment. 
3.2 India also faces a significant challenge of drug-resistant TB. Early detection 
and initiation of suitable treatment involving proper anti-TB medications are crucial 
in limiting the proliferation of drug-resistant strains. Given that TB affects not just 
individuals but also has ripple effects on families and communities, its early 
detection helps protect vulnerable populations while mitigating the disease's social 
and economic impact by preventing complications due to delayed diagnosis and 
treatment of TB. 
3.3 Another challenge in early detection is the stigma associated with the 
disease, which  often dissu ades people from coming forward to report their 
condition. Such reluctance leads to further complexities as the disease continues to 
spread unabated. Community outreach strategy has been intended through 
PMTBMBA, where efforts are being made to improve scr eening, early detection 
and notification of TB cases. The Committee has been apprised of the following 
highlights of case-finding efforts: 
i. The Committee was given to note that during the year 2022, 24.2 lakh TB 
cases were notified across all states & UTs. The gaps in TB identification are 
primarily due to the non -reporting of the missing cases in the private sector 
and challenges in the health- seeking behaviour of the population. 
ii. As regards Presumptive TB examination for individuals who are suspected of 
having TB based on their clinical symptoms such as persistent cough, fever, 
weight loss, chest pain, and other relevant factors, even though a confirmed 
diagnosis has not been made, it is informed that in 2022, the Presumptive TB 
examination rates (PTBER) for the country increased to 1281 per lakh 
population from 763 (68% increase) in 2021. Further, as per India TB Report 
2023, the number of rapid molecular diagnosis tests (PTBER - NAAT 
including CBNAAT and Truenat) that were offered in 2022 as the first test of 
diagnosis for TB was 294 per lakh population. 
iii. Passive case finding: Apart from the presumptive cases that passively come to 

29  
the TB facilities for diagnosis, it is a provider -initiated activity with the 
primary objective of detecting TB cases early by case -finding in the hospital 
set up for patients who seek care for symptoms other than TB at OPDs 
including NCD clinics, Obs & Gyne, Pediatrics, ICTC / ART centres, Surgery, 
Neurology, Cancer clinics, etc. 
iv. Active case finding: A provider -initiated activity with the primary objective 
of detecting TB cases early by case finding in targeted groups in the 
community either on a campaign mode or as a routine activity on a periodic 
basis. Such vulnerable groups specifically need active attempts by the system 
to minimise avoidable delays in detection and prompt treatment initiation 
since they are often at higher risk of exposure, sub - optimal health-seeking 
behaviour, comorbidities, difficulty in access to healthcare services and poorer 
treatment outcomes from the disease. The peripheral health workers perform 
house-to-house screening, do vulnerability mapping, collect samples from 
presumptive cases, and transport these samples to the nearest laboratory for 
diagnosis. Mobile vans are also used in some States/UTs to take testing closer 
to the community. In 2022, across the country, a total of 22.1 crore individuals 
were screened for presumptive TB symptoms/signs as part of active case - 
finding efforts. Of the screened, 0.9% (19.5 lakhs) were examined and tested 
for TB diagnosis, of which 48,329 (2.5%) were diagnosed. The TB 
elimination programme has been able to identify 2.85 lakh additional TB cases 
through this exercise since its introduction in 2018. 
v. Another important indicator monitoring case -finding efforts through 
screening and early detection is the Annualised TB Case Notification Rate 
(ACNR). ACNR is defined as the number of TB cases notified per 100,000 
population on an annualised basis. ACNR trend from 2012 to 2022 as per India

2022, India notified 7.33 lakh TB cases from the private sector (the highest 
ever private sector notification), contributing to 30% of total notifications. 
3.4 The Committee obser ves that early detection is crucial, requiring 
screening of vulnerable populations to break the transmission chain. The 
Committee was apprised that lack of control of TB transmission, particularly 
in the poor & slum areas, is leading to the infection ratio , i.e., the number of 
individuals infected by a TB patient, greater than or equal to one and lack of 
testing with X -ray & CBNAAT are delaying the TB cases  detection. The 
Committee believes that the efforts to screen & detect TB at early stages should 
be made on a war footing. Keeping in view the large population of India and 
the target of achieving TB elimination by 2025, the Committee suggests that 
there is a need to significantly increase the rate of TB case finding by making 
efforts as follows: 

31  
i. Aggressive use of X-ray for faster & confirming TB diagnosis: - 
The National TB Prevalence Survey 2021 shows that out of 100 TB 
cases, clinical assessment picks up only 62 cases while X-ray picks up 95 
cases. Also, among those diagnosed during the survey, more than 50% 
did not have typical signs or symptoms suggestive of TB but had an 
abnormality in the chest X-ray, which led to their TB diagnosis. Hence, 
dependence on clinical assessment based on symptoms alone and the 
non- availability of X -ray diagnostic facilities led to about 33% of 
missed cases. Therefore, the Committee would like to suggest that the 
Government ensure that X - ray facilities are  available at all public 
healthcare facilities. Further, to speed up the early detection of TB 
cases, deploying Artificial intelligence -enabled portable and hand -held 
X-ray units for screening at district as well as block levels should be 
considered. Also,  the provision of hand -held X -ray machines at 
registered private centres at discounted rates may also be considered. 
 
ii. Scaling up molecular laboratories to block levels: - The 
Committee has been informed that in the year 2022, the percentage of 
PTBER-NAAT e xaminations where molecular diagnostic tests were 
conducted as the first test of diagnosis was 23% of the total PTBER tests 
conducted including microscopy. National TB Prevalence Survey shows 
that among one lakh people, microscopy picks up 152 TB patients while 
molecular tests pick up 285. Hence, considering the efficiency of 
molecular diagnostics is much higher than microscopy, further 
decentralisation of rapid molecular diagnostics, ensuring optimal 
utilisation of NAAT capacity to peripheral levels, at le ast the Block 
levels, will lead to a significant decrease in missed TB cases per lakh 
population and curb ongoing transmission of the disease. 
iii. Holistic implementation of intensified case finding in OPDs of all 
healthcare facilities: - This involves systema tic screening of all people 
seeking care in a health facility or a clinic for identification of people at 
risk for TB. Bi-directional screening of patients visiting the hospitals for 
diseases like diabetes must be brought into wide practice, which 
includes simultaneous diagnosis for TB along with other ailments. 
iv. Contact Tracing: - In the National TB Prevalence Survey 2021, 
it was observed that up to 64% of those with presumptive TB symptoms 
or signs in the general population did not seek care. The Committee is 
of the view that there is a requirement for systematic planning and 
implementation of active case -finding campaigns among vulnerable 
populations in community settings more aggr essively so as to minimise 
the delays in the detection and treatment of TB cases. The Government 

32  
may also consider incentivising anybody in the community for a case 
referred to and diagnosed as TB. 
v. Involvement of the private sector: - The National Strategic Plan 
envisioned obtaining 56% of the total notifications from the private 
sector by 2021, but the country has reached only 32%. Effective 
engagement of the private sector on a scale commensurate w ith their 
dominant presence in the Indian healthcare system is crucial to 
achieving the TB elimination target. The Committee is of the view that 
there is a need for systematic assessment and a proper strategy to 
promote engagement of the private sector. Va rious methods like 
expediting the payment of incentives and any treatment cost to the 
private sector, making the Ni -kshay portal more user-friendly for ease 
of reporting & capacity building programme in Information and 
communication Technology (ICT) for pr ivate providers. Apart from 
this, there should be a robust monitoring mechanism in place to ensure 
the accountability of private players to create a sustainable public - 
private partnership in TB elimination. 
vi. Increase in awareness program: - The Committee is of the view 
that the health-seeking behaviour of the population is also a challenge 
in the TB elimination drive that leads to missed cases. This can be 
overcome by aggressively targeted IEC campaigns in the community to 
raise awareness and improve health-seeking behaviour. 
 
vii. Region-specific detection pattern: - The causes for the spread of 
TB can vary across different regions due to a combination of social, 
economic, healthcare, and geographical factors. The detection and case- 
finding pattern, along with control measures, should be well strategised 
with an understanding of the differences. 
 
 
B. Diagnostic facilities for TB detection 
 
3.5 Ensuring that TB diagnosis is available, affordable, and accessible to all who 
need it is critical to ensure prompt and effective treatment, improve treatment 
outcomes, and reduce TB -related deaths. The availability of free -of-cost quality 
assured TB diagnostic services is essential for early detection, which is a 
cornerstone of tuberculosis control; it aids in identifying active cases of TB in their 
initial stages and reducing transmission, identifying drug -resistant TB early and 
preventing further spread of drug -resistant strains. It will also assist the health 
authorities to track the preva lence of the disease, evaluate the impact of 
interventions and make informed decisions for resource allocation. Therefore, the 

33  
availability and affordability of quality-assured TB diagnostic services are pivotal 
for the success of PMTBMBA. 
 
3.6 From the replies received from the Ministry, the following major initiatives 
are being taken by the Government to ensure the availability of TB diagnostic 
services at decentralised levels: 
i) Availability of TB diagnostic services at every level under NTEP. 
ii) Enhanced diagnostic techniques that emphasise microbiological 
confirmation of TB and use of chest X-rays to improve the efficiency 
of screening procedures. 
iii) To ensure comprehensive coverage, A structured laboratory network 
has been put in place, spanning National, State, District, sub-district, 
and peripheral levels. 
iv) Decentralisation of high-sensitivity molecular diagnostic tools such 
as NAAT to block levels, extending microscopy services to all 
Primary Health Centers (PHCs), and establishing X-ray facilities at 
sub-district levels have broadened access to testing and screening 
programs. 
v) Forward-looking methods such as point -of-care testing and mobile 
health technologies are adopted to facilitate early diagnosis  and 
treatment. 
vi) The participation of Ayushman Bharat Health and Wellness Centers 
(AB-HWCs) is leveraged for TB screening, especially within 
vulnerable populations. This includes the use of telemedicine and the 
decentralisation of services to the level of AB -HWCs, which are 
situated closer to the community. This approach simplifies access to 
care through a unified system. 
vii) Community Support: TB Vijayetas have been engaged to offer peer 
counselling and social support. The communit y-based approach 
enhances the overall impact of TB screening and treatment efforts.

C.  Affordable and quality TB treatment and availability of medications 
 
3.10 Affordability and quality of TB treatment and medicines are critical components 
of strategies to eliminate TB by 2025. Financial constraint is one of the challenges that 
hinder access to healthcare & completion of an entire course of treatment. 
 
3.11 The objectives of providing TB treatment services are to make the patient non- 
infectious rapidly, break the chain of transmission and decrease the probability of 
transmission of TB infection, decrease case fatality  and morbidity, and minimise and 
prevent the development of drug resistance. By making TB treatment affordable for 
patients, an increase in TB cases and the growth and spread of drug-resistant strains can 
be curtailed to a great extent, ultimately reducing the burden on limited resources and 
having positive economic & health implications. 

36  
3.12 From the replies of the Ministry, the Committee was informed that since 
2018, the programme has been able to sustain a treatment success rate above 80%. In 
2021, the success rate had reached 84%, and in 2022, it has increased to 85.5%. The 
Government has taken the following steps under the National TB Elimination Program 
to improve the treatment of TB: - 
 
i. TB Elimination Program has emphasised real-time Ni-kshay entries for 
data management and tracking each patient for treatment and post - 
treatment follow-up. 
 
ii. Identifying adverse events during treatment and providing appropriate 
management to the person affected with TB is essential. In this regard, 
the adverse event module has now been made available in Ni -kshay to 
manage events from reporting to declaring an outcome and ensuring 
optimal quality of care. 
 
iii. A treatment supporter (ASHA/any other community volunteer) is 
assigned to each patient to ensure adherence to treatment with a 
provision for incentives upon completion of treatment. Community 
support through TB Vijayetas for peer counselling and social support is 
also provided. 
 
iv. All notified persons affected with TB are eligible to receive an incentive 
of Rs 500/ - per month for the entire duration of treatment towards a 
nutritional support incentive of Rs 750/ - (one time) to TB patients for 
travel support in tribal, hilly and rugged terrains, linking with other 
social protection schemes at the State/District level, patient home visit 
as per convenience of patient and counselling of patient and family 
members. 
 
v. The Family Care Model, a community -based approach, is leveraged to 
provide home-based care through a family caregiver to improve 
treatment access and adherence. This model involves training and 
involving a family member or caregiver to play a central role in 
providing care, treatment support, and monitoring to the affected 
individual within their home. 
 
vi. The government provides access to fre e and quality anti -TB drugs for 
all patients seeking care from the public and private sectors. A package 
of TB services has also been covered under Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya 
Yojana for in-patient care. 
 
vii. TB Preventive Treatment (TPT) has been expanded from People living 

37  
with HIV/AIDS (PLHIV) and less than 5 -year-old household contacts 
of TB patients to all age group household contacts of pulmonary TB 
patients and other high -risk groups since August 2021. By the end of 
2022, 722 (94%) districts of India have expanded TPT as per the national 
guidelines. The total number of household contacts of pulmonary TB 
and PLHIV initiated on TPT increased from 56,907 in 2017 to 13,92,106 
in 2022. 
 
viii. A fixed -dose combinations (FDCs) multi -drug regimen as per the 
weight band to persons affected with drug-susceptible and drug-resistant 
TB under direct observation of a trained treatment supporter. A shorter 
oral Bedaquiline -containing MDR/RR -TB (Multi -Drug Resistant/ 
Rifampicin-Resistant TB) regimen has been rolled out pan -country in 
April 2022. 
 
3.13 The Committee appreciates the efforts made by the Government in 
improving TB treatment and making the treatment and the medicines more 
affordable and accessible. However, the Committee is of the view that there is a 
wide scope for improvement to realise the target by 2025 and makes the following 
suggestions based on its observations: 
 
i. The Committee noted that even some of the tertiary hospitals in 
major cities of the country lack dedicated TB wards and treatment facilities 
with adequate capacity, whereas some hospitals have TB treatment 
facilities but have an acute shortage of skilled manpower. The Committee 
suggests that a network of dedicated TB clinics and centres equipped with 
necessary diagnostic and treatment facilities supported with adequate 
manpower is required to be expanded to all healthcare facilities from 
tertiary and primary health centres, covering remote locations. Further, 
the Committee suggests that resources available under the Ayushman 
Bharat-Health and Wellness Centres Scheme should be leveraged 
effectively to enhance the reach and efficacy of TB treatment services, 
especially in underserved areas. 
 
ii. Apart from improving early TB diagnosis and availability of  TB 
treatment, there is a significant need to improve the acceptability of TB 
treatment. Therefore, the Government should invest more in nationwide 
public awareness campaigns in collaboration with private healthcare 
providers, NGOs and other stakeholders t o educate the public about 
available treatment options, the importance of completing the full 
treatment course, and dispel myths and misconceptions. The counsellors 
and psychologists should be roped in the PMTBMBA program in a big way 

38  
to provide adequate social and emotional support to all TB patients. 
 
iii. In order to make the TB elimination program successful, there is a 
need to establish a continuous robust data collection and monito ring 
system, which would help in identifying areas of improvement and 
allocation of resources effectively on a regular basis. This would also ensure 
that quality standards and treatment protocols are followed by all 
providers. 
 
iv. To improve adherence to TB treatment, the Committee suggests 
that a daily fixed -dose regimen under a directly observed treatment short 
course should be rolled out throughout the country on mission mode. 
Further, much -needed support, including more fund allocation, is to be 
given to support research and trials to expedite the introduction of newer 
anti-TB drugs providing less toxic and short-duration treatment. 
 
v. In view of higher prices of anti -TB drugs, the Committee suggests 
that the Government may collaborate with pharmaceutical co mpanies to 
negotiate prices for  TB drugs through bulk purchasing or licensing 
agreements. It has come to the notice of the Committee that some of the 
drugs which are used for treating Drug-Resistant TB are still not available 
to private practitioners. In view thereof, it is also required to establish an 
arrangement with fixed accountability to maintain a regular supply of 
quality TB medications to all parts of the country. Moreover, the 
government may encourage the domestic production of generic TB drugs 
with infrastructural support, streamlined regulatory processes, and tax 
incentives to reduce dependency on imported medicines. 
 
vi. The Health Sector of the country has benefitted substantially from 
Corporate Social Responsibility, but the same is lacking in or ganisation 
and direction to enhance the benefit of a specific program. The formulation 
of policies and systematic efforts are required to be made to leverage 
Corporate Social Responsibility activities with targeted utilisation 
enhancing affordability and accessibility of TB treatment and drugs. 
 
vii. The effective utilisation of telemedicine and e -health platforms is 
required to be promoted by developing user -friendly and easy -to-operate 
applications to connect patients with experienced doctors for remote 
consultations and diagnosis. This can reduce the need for physical visits to 
diagnostic centres. Moreover, such applications can also be used to provide 
information about TB and offer medication reminders. 

39  
viii. The Committee was informed that it was noticed during the 
National TB Prevalence Survey, 2021 that a substantial portion of 
people with TB do not seek care and remain undetected to the system. 
The community avoids the treatment for various reasons, includi ng the 
stigma associated with the disease, which hinders the successful 
implementation of the TB elimination program. Therefore, TB 
Prevention Treatment is a great initiative to remove the stigma and 
make PMTBMBA more effective, especially on the preventiv e side. TB 
Prevention Treatment covering all age groups is required to be executed 
extensively across the country by reaching out to communities and 
asking people, even at the infection level, to come and start treatment. 
 
 
D.  Adequacy of healthcare infrastructure and manpower to support PMTBMBA 
 
3.14 Public and primary healthcare in India is critical to control the menace of 
TB. Universal access to healthcare and improving primary health facilities can 
substantially reduce the prevalence of TB. The Govern ment has undertaken a 
multitude of initiatives to strengthen the existing health infrastructure, but keeping 
in mind the large population and high TB burden in India, the revamp of the 
infrastructure of existing CHCs, PHCs and SCs, and the construction of new health 
centres in unserved regions of the country may be fast-tracked on mission mode to 
overcome the overall shortage of health infrastructure and to achieve the Universal 
Health Coverage at the earliest. 
 
3.15 The National Health Policy 2017 envisages “the attainment of the highest 
possible level of health and well -being for all at all ages, through a preventive and 
promotive healthcare orientation in all developmental policies, and universal access 
to good quality healthcare services without anyone having to face financial hardship 
as a consequence. This would be achieved through increasing access, improving 
quality, and lowering the cost of healthcare delivery.” Accordingly, the Policy 
recommended increasing the Government’s health expenditure from 1.2 per cent to 
2.5 per cent of GDP by 2025. Also, the Fifteenth Finance Commission, in its report 
had recommended that the public health expenditure of Unions and States should 
be increased progressively to reach 2 .5 per cent of GDP by 2025. The Committee 
noted that in keeping with this objective, Central and State Governments’ budgeted 
expenditure on the health sector reached 2.1 per cent of GDP in FY 2022 -23 (BE) 
and 2.2 per cent in FY 2021-22 (RE), against 1.6 per cent in FY 2020-21. 
 
3.16 The Economic Survey 2022 -23 highlighted that the share of government 
health expenditure in total health expenditure has increased from 28.6 per cent in 
FY 2013-14 to 40.6 per cent in FY 2018-19. The Survey also shows the hike in the 

40  
share of spending on health in the total expenditure on social services, which has 
increased from 21 per cent in FY 2018-19 to 26 per cent in FY 2022-23 (BE). 
3.17 The Committee believes that the Government's health expenditure 
needs to be increased on the lines of the recommendations of the Fifteenth 
Finance Commission. Apart from increased public health expenditure, the 
efficient utilisation of available funds shou ld also be emphasised to improve 
the quality and access of healthcare infrastructure. The active collaboration 
with private stakeholders may also help in strengthening the infrastructure 
significantly. The Committee further suggests that the Government sho uld 
also consider the possibility of utilising private and public pharmacies as 
potential DOTS centres to improve the accessibility of TB drugs. 
3.18 During the meeting held with stakeholders, the Committee was apprised of 
the problems faced at health facilitie s for TB diagnosis. It was submitted that the 
diagnosis of TB sometimes gets delayed in various peripheral centres as well as in 
tertiary institutes because of a lack of availability of necessary reagents and kits, as 
there are delays in procurement at various levels. The Committee is of the view that 
such administrative delays in the procurement of requisite resources would weaken 
the momentum of TB care services and would lead to the active spread of TB 
disease. Therefore, the Committee suggests that there should be an 
uninterrupted supply of CBNAAT & Truenat cartridges and other functional 
diagnostic equipments at every healthcare centre. For this purpose, the 
Government may take steps to streamline the procurement procedures and to 
make a robust mechanism for reporting and monitoring of the availability of 
cartridges stock and other equipments. Further, the Government should 
engage with manufacturers and suppliers to ensure a regular supply of quality 
kits and eq uipments. Local manufacturing may be promoted with necessary 
tax rebates and subsidies. 
3.19 Apart from health infrastructure, the availability of health care providers and 
technical workforce is critical for the optimal utilisation  of available health 
infrastructure and to achieve Universal Health Coverage in real terms. From the 
replies of the Ministry on a query regarding details of the availability of technical 
staff and health care workforce under the National TB elimination pro gram at 
District, sub-district and peripheral levels, the Committee is informed that there is 
a shortage of manpower and vacant posts at all levels involved in the TB elimination 
program like State level programme staff and District level programme staff. The 
program is affected at the grassroots level by deficiencies in human resources due 
to vacancies at district  and state levels. Further, the shortage of staff involved in 
the program overburdens the existing staff, which affects the efficient 
implementation of the TB programme. 

41  
3.20 The Committee was further informed that the State TB Officer (STO) and 
District TB Officer (DTO), who are implementers of the TB elimination program at 
the state and district level, are medical practitioners and lack program management 
and administration expertise. 
3.21 The Committee believes that STOs and DTOs, who are the backbone of 
implementing the TB program at the grassroots level efficiently, may be 
provided intensive executi ve training to perform administrative and 
managerial duties efficiently. The Committee was also given to note that PDSA 
or Plan-Do-Study-Act, which is a four -stage problem-solving model used for 
improving a process or carrying out change, may be encouraged by the 
Government to be implemented by DTOs in their strategies and 
implementation of TB program. Apart from the s kill training programs, they 
are also required to be sensitised towards the program’s objectives and to 
encourage significant involvement of non -government resources like NGOs in 
the fight against TB, which will also fill the gap due to lack of manpower. 
3.22 Keeping in view the fact that health is a State subject, the Committee 
feels that the Union Government needs to impress on the State Governments 
to put a greater level of priority on TB and to come up with a policy to address 
shortfalls in the human resources for the State Health Sector. The role of allied 
staff is critical for activities like sputum collection and follow-up in treatment, 
and hence, their shortage should also be addressed. The Committee emphasises 
that all vacant posts may be filled at the earliest. 
3.23 The Government should also consider training programs and bridge 
courses for ASHA workers, treatment supporters, counsellors, caregivers 
under the Family Care Model, other allied health workers and comm unity 
volunteers about the TB disease to increase management awareness about the 
disease and its progression and treatment options. 
3.24 The Committee was given a note that the salary structures of human 
resources involved  in the TB elimination program are not on par with other 
contractual healthcare staff appointed as part of the National Health Mission 
(NHM). This leads to lower motivation of staff and internal friction amongst the 
staff. The Committee suggests that the ra tionalisation of the salary of  the 
healthcare workforce under the TB program at par with other healthcare staff 
appointed under NHM may be done at the earliest, and all contractual NTEP 
manpower may be regularised. The Government also needs to take positiv e 
measures such as special allowances, availability of accommodation, etc., to 
incentivise professional clinical and non - clinical staff to get posted to rural/ 
hilly areas. 
3.25 The Committee has also been apprised during its interaction with

stakeholders that many complications during and following the treatment of TB 
require thoracic surgical interventions, the facilities for which are grossly 
insufficient in the country. In view thereof,  the Committee feels that the 
Government must take urgent steps for the recruitment of specialists to

E.  Major Challenges in treatment and tackling of TB 
 
3.26 During the Committee’s deliberations on the subject and interactions with 
various stakeholders, it is noted that despite the government’s accomplishments in 
controlling TB,  certain major challenges continue to impact the TB control 
programs at various levels. The following challenges have been highlighted: 
 
i) Latent TB infection: The primary challenges of the program include 
latent TB infection in the community, which affects around 31% of 
India's population as per the National TB Prevalence Survey 2021. These 
latent infections can become active diseas es based on immunity, 
nutrition, and lifestyle. Identifying these cases necessitates reaching a 
significant portion of the population. It is a big challenge for the 
programme to find out those who do not have symptoms but can still 
unknowingly spread the disease. 
 
ii) Shortage of staff and functional diagnostics: Maintaining a 
comprehensive treatment network poses a second challenge. Patients 
move through primary healthcare centres  where microscopy tests may 
be performed. The patient is sent to the block level for molecular 
diagnostics, and after that, the patient is connected to treatment 
supervisors or ASHA workers, which may run over several months. 
Shortages in staff and functio nal diagnostics at state levels hinder 
progress. Vacancies in state health systems, with a scarcity of laboratory 
technicians and supervisors ranging from 30% to 80%, also add 
complexity to the efficient functioning of the program. 
 
iii) Vulnerability mapping: Vulnerability mapping aids active case 
finding, especially in slums and high -risk areas, but screening over a 
crore of people annually is challenging. 
 
iv) Private sector and missing cases: The private sector contributes to 
missed cases, as people with TB symp toms often seek private care, 
delaying accurate diagnosis. Active engagement with the private sector, 
including incentives, aims to address this. 

43  
v) Poor health -conscious behaviour of people: Patients' poo r health - 
seeking behaviour, either due to a lack of awareness or due to prevailing 
misconceptions in the community about TB, is another hurdle. Self - 
treatment and neglect lead to around 14% of TB cases becoming resistant 
due to incomplete or improper treatment. The reluctance of the 
community to follow respiratory hygiene and cough etiquette is also a 
challenge on account of people’s behaviour. 
vi) Treatment defaulters: Another significant challenge revolves around 
treatment defaulters. The treatment for TB, including sensitive cases, 
spans two phases: an intensive two-month phase followed by a 
four-month continuous phase. During the initial two months, patients are 
administered different drugs that significantly redu ce the infection. 
Subsequently, they must continue for four months. However, many TB 
patients, particularly migrant labourers or those with transient work, 
begin treatment during the first two months but later abandon it. This 
results in defaults or patients becoming untraceable, posing a substantial 
hurdle for the TB program's efforts to ensure treatment adherence. 
 
vii) Social stigma: The stigma associated with the disease is a significant 
barrier to its management. The negative perceptions and misconceptions 
surrounding TB often lead to discrimination, isolation, and reluctance to 
seek treatment. Stigma also discourages individuals from getting tested 
or adhering to treatment, ultimately impeding efforts to control the 
spread of TB disease. The Committee feels  that stigma reduction is 
among the most important aspects that can provide an enabling 
environment for people with TB to seek care and complete their 
treatment. Addressing this stigma through targeted advocacy, 
communication and social mobilisation activi ties; structured 
sensitisation programmes specifically for rural areas; publicising 
success stories of TB survivors and community engagement are 
crucial for effective TB control programs. The Committee 
recommends that the Government should consider screeni ng and 
active case finding should be called Lung Health Screen and not TB 
screening to reduce stigma. 
 
viii) Social determinants of TB:  Apart from health -related risk factors, 
social and economic factors like access to clean fuels, income equality, 
food security, social protection, and living conditions strongly influence 
the TB epidemic. These factors are notably pronounced in lower - and 
middle-income countries. In India, approximately 22% live below the 
international poverty line (2011 data), 15% are ma lnourished (2019 
data), 68% have access to clean cooking fuels (2020 data), and 35% of 

44  
urban dwellers reside in slums (2018 data). 
 
Role of nutrition in TB treatment and management 
 
3.27 The Committee notes that undernutrition and TB have a bi -directional 
relationship. Undernutrition is an established risk factor for the progression of latent 
TB infection to active TB. At the population level, undernutrition contributes to an 
estimated 55% of annual TB incidence in India. TB leads to weight loss, muscle 
wasting and worsening of nutritional status. The vicious cycle between 
undernutrition and TB persists and significantly increases the burden of both 
diseases in India. Undernutrition is a se rious co-morbidity in patients with active 
TB in India and increases the risk of severe disease, death, drug toxicity, drug mal- 
absorption and relapse after cure. In the absence of nutritional support, 
undernourished patients with TB get entangled in a vicious cycle of worsening 
disease, which can be detrimental and even fatal. 
 
3.28 As reported in India TB Report 2023, evidence has shown that for each unit 
reduction in BMI, the risk of TB increases by about 14%. Similarly, compared to 
individuals with normal body weight, the risk of relapse is four times higher for each 
unit reduction in BMI. In addition, for children, and pregnant & lactating women, 
the physiological requirement for nutrition is  high, further aggravating the risk of 
TB disease if the demand for increased nutrition is unmet.  Adequate nutrition also 
helps in treatment adherence and an overall reduction in mortality. Hence, assessment 
and management of nutritional status are critical for TB treatment and care. 
 
3.29 The Committee has been apprised of the following key interventions by the 
Government to address the nutritional requirements of TB patients: 
 
i. With the support of various partners, the Central TB Division has 
developed a mobile-based application, the Nutrition-TB App (N-TB 
app), that explains nutritional assessment, counselling and support 
and provides guidance on appropriate supplementation for 
undernourished adult patients with tuberculosis. 
 
ii. Under the Ni -kshay Poshan Yojana, an incentive of Rs 500 per 
month for the entire duration of treatment towards nutritional 
support. 
 
iii. Under PMTBMBA, anybody in the community (Ni-kshay Mitra) can 
adopt a consented TB patient and provide additional nutritional 
support and other social support, including vocational support to the 
patients. 

45  
iv. Family Care Model - community-based approach for home -based 
care through a family caregiver to provide nutritional counselling 
and support leading to improved adherence and treatment success. 
 
3.30 It is important to note that addressing undernutrition and providing 
nutritional support to households with TB patients align with Sustainable 
Development Goals (SDGs 1, 2 and 3). Recently, the results of the Reducing 
Activation of Tuberculosis by Improvement of Nutritional Status (RATIONS) trial 
published in the Lancet Journal, 2023 have shown that nutritional support is an 
integral component of patient -centred TB care, improves treatment outcomes and 
has a potential impact on reducing all forms of TB incidence by 40% and infectious 
TB by nearly 50%. 
 
3.31 In view of the above, the Committee emphasise  the implementation of 
guidelines of the National TB elimination program for nutritional care and 
support of patients with TB in India. Nutritional assessment should be made 
mandatory for TB patients and their family members. 
 
3.32 The Committee suggests that a Community -based model of nutritional 
care and the utilisation of Corporate Social Responsibility under PMTBMBA 
should be strategically promoted. The Government needs to explore strategies 
to expand the role of nutritional counselling and dietary diversity and provide 
an adequate diet based on calorie, protein and micronutrient requirements 
tailored to TB patients' ideal body weight. Nutritional counselling may also 
play a significant role in raising awareness among community su pporters and 
volunteers, ensuring that patients receive optimal nutritional support, 
specifically in terms of protein and micronutrient requirements, balanced diet, 
clarification of misconceptions related to supplements/tonics and promoting 
food hygiene and healthy cooking practices. 
 
3.33 The Committee further suggests that on a large scale, the Government 
must take action -oriented steps to provide nutritional support to 
undernourished people who are at a much higher risk of developing active TB. 
For this pur pose, the Government must diversify the diet provided under 
various social protection schemes like the Food Security Scheme, Mid -day 
Meal, etc., to ensure adequate calorie, protein and micronutrient requirements 
are fulfilled in the meals. An integrated pr ogramme with food kit distribution 
or through the public distribution systems may be considered to tackle 
malnutrition problems and reduce the risk of developing TB disease. Also, 
post-treatment follow-up of patients, including their nutritional status, should 
be ensured after completion of treatment.

RESEARCH, WAY FORWARD AND BEST PRACTICES 
 
A.  Ongoing research initiatives and studies related to TB in India 
 
4.1 TB research is one of the strategic areas under the National Strategic Plan to 
End TB in India. TB research and development are critical for meeting the global 
TB targets in the Sustainable Development Goals. 
 
4.2 The Committee was apprised that to end TB epidemic in India, the 
Department of Health Research (DHR) and the Indian Council for Medical Research 
(ICMR) are leading TB research in India to develop affordable diagnostics, shorter 
drug regimens for treating TB infection & disease, new TB vaccines, geospatial 
analytics for vulnerable hotspots, and innovative strategies to address broader 
determinants of TB infection and disease. They have also established the India TB 
Research Consortium targeted towards innovative, breakthrough research in newer 
point-of-care diagnostics, n ewer drug molecules & regimens, vaccines and 
implementation research. The Program also encourages operational research to 
study challenges and benefits in implementing recent policy decisions to strengthen 
healthcare service delivery. In addition, the Comm ittee was also apprised of the 
following developments being undertaken by the Government in the field of TB 
research: - 
 
i) The priority areas for research have been clearly defined under the 
programme to encourage Operational and Clinical research. The programme 
also has expert technical guidance and support mechanisms to garner 
research in TB among budding medical graduates and biology, 
biotechnology and social sciences students. It was further informed that 
interim results of a pragmatic study on newer  drug regimens conducted by 
the National Institute for Research in Tuberculosis (NIRT -ICMR) in 
collaboration with the Central TB Division and the WHO country office for 
India in 2021 are promising and will be introduced under the programme 
once administrative approval is received. 
 
ii) The Central TB division has been involved in developing artificial 
intelligence solutions for automated reading of Chest X -rays and cough 
sounds for TB detection and interpretation of Line probe assay  (LPA). The 
AI models have be en trained and validated internally. Validation of the 
cough sounds-based AI solution and LPA solution is currently underway 
with ICMR. 

47  
iii) The molecular testing machine 'Truenat' has been developed 
indigenously and is also pre -qualification certified by WHO. This machine 
provides the result in 45 minutes and detects Rifampicin resistance in 
another 45 minutes. It was also used to detect Covid infection during the 
COVID-19 pandemic. 
 
iv) Regarding the problem wi th the skin TB test or PPD test, it was 
informed that in case a person has been given the BCG vaccine during 
infancy, the PPD test gives a positive result, and it was difficult to 
differentiate whether the positive result is due to BCG vaccine or TB 
infection. Recently, ICMR has developed a new skin TB test (Cy - Tb), 
which gives a true positive result even on BCG immunisation during infancy 
and is also quite cheap. The Committee has also been enlightened that to 
make the diagnosis of TB infection more accessible and affordable, a 
validation study for the Cy -Tb test is ongoing for the age group under 18 
years. 
 
v) ICMR initiated a study o n TB vaccines in July 2019. For that 
purpose, ICMR enrolled 12722 participants who were put into three arms, 
namely, Immuvac (MIP) arm, VPM1002 (rBCG) arm and Placebo arm. It is 
a phase III trial. The interim analysis done in 2021 has established the safety 
of the vaccines. However, details on the immunogenicity and efficacy of the 
vaccines will only be available after the three -year follow-up period, i.e., 
after Dec 2023. 
 
vi) BCG is an antigen vaccine, significantly decreasing the chances of 
TB occurring  in children due to antibodies developed by the vaccine. 
However, with increasing age, until they reach adulthood, the level of 
antibodies falls, increasing the susceptibility to  TB infection. The Central 
TB division is collaborating with ICMR to study the  effectiveness of a 
second dose of BCG vaccine among adults, and the process is underway. 
 
4.3 The Committee is of the view that research and innovation in TB 
elimination programs are core components that would re-define the diagnostic 
and treatment measures t o transform the TB response efforts and develop 
novel approaches that not only benefit India in reducing TB burden but can 
lead the way for other countries to follow. Keeping in view the challenges being 
faced in TB control, the Committee would like to suggest developing a 
diagnostic method that is very simple to use and less time -consuming that 
frontline workers can use to get results within minutes at minimal costs. Such 
diagnostic methods would add new dimensions to the diagnostic services, which 
are affordable and easily accessible to patients. 

48  
4.4 Another priority for research in TB is the development and validation 
of new drugs that are critical for strengthening the treatment regimens in 
terms of sh ort duration and less toxicity to TB patients.  It is also noted that 
evidence on certain drug regimens for Latent TB infection, drug-susceptible & 
drug-resistant TB infections is clear; however, these regimens are still not 
scaled up. The Committee would request the government to expedite the 
process and bring these drug regimens to treat TB cases. 
 
4.5 TB vaccine research is underway. The Committee recommends the 
Government prioritise the clinical trials of all TB vaccine candidates to assess 
their efficacy i n various community settings and for different target groups. 
For this purpose, the government should mobilise the funds to facilitate these 
clinical trials so that these vaccine platforms can be scaled up rapidly. 
 
4.6 As regards the BCG vaccine, it is noted that it does not provide adequate 
protection to adolescents and adults who are at high risk of developing TB 
disease. In view thereof, the Committee suggests expediting the research 
programme to study the effectiveness of re- vaccination of BCG and, based on 
the result, to consider introducing a booster dose of recombinant BCG vaccine 
in the universal immunisation programme. This could be an effective and 
cheaper option to control TB. 
 
4.7 The Committee suggests the Government procure the indigenously 
developed Truenat diagnostic machine in bulk and supply the same at the 
minimum block levels across the country as quickly as possible, later on, which 
may be extended to primary health centres also. This would aid in the earliest 
realisation of upfront molecular testing across the country, which curbs the 
missing TB cases and simplifies the detection of drug- resistant TB cases. 
 
4.8 The Committee believes that newly developed skin tests may be utilised 
extensively for TB preventive treatment and conducted for close contact with 
TB patients & risk groups like diabetes, alcoholics, etc. The validation of this 
test for children less than 18 years old may be expedited so that the TB 
preventive treatment may be given at the infection level itself.

C.  Bio-medical waste management in TB Health Centres 
 
4.11 On a query raised on Biomedical Waste Management (BWM) of TB 
healthcare centres, the Ministry, in its replies, informed the Committee that BWM 
is included as an integral part of the Standard operating procedures across all TB 

50  
diagnostic facilities. Any waste generated inside the laboratory is considered 
infectious and is disinfected, segregated in colour -coded bags & containers and 
disposed of, adhering to the BWM guidelines from the Government of India (2016) 
and the Central Pollution Control Board. The designated Kayakalp authorities 
undertake routine monitoring at the facility level. 
 
4.12 Management of biomedical waste from TB diagnostic facilities is integrated 
into the General Health System. The facility/ hospital/ institution handles it as 
routine activity through  the BWM or Hospital Infection Control Committee. The 
NTEP supervisory staff conducts monitoring of BWM at all levels of TB Health 
Centres during their regular supervisory visits. 
 
4.13 In view of the health hazards of biomedical waste in TB healthcare 
centres, the Committee is of the view that de -contamination of biomedical 
waste is critical in preventing the transmission of TB disease. As in act ive TB 
cases, sputum collection is done to diagnose TB disease; it is important to 
handle and dispose of infected sputum as soon as possible. However, the 
current practices of disposing of infected biomedical waste have limitations. 
The Committee is aware that even after biomedical waste treatment of TB 
bacteria, it should not go live in the environment as it can pose health hazards 
due to its drug -resistant nature. In view of this, the Committee recommends 
that the treatment and proper disposal of biomedic al waste is of immense 
importance and is crucial in controlling the increasing burden of drug-resistant 
TB cases. The Committee suggests that the Government should monitor 
compliance towards existing BWM practices at TB health centres and ensure 
that there  are requisite equipment, kits and human resources involved in 
biomedical waste treatment at all TB healthcare centres. 
 
4.14 Further, the Committee believes that research should be conducted to 
develop safer and better disinfection methods to manage BWM that can ensure 
the safety of people and the environment. 
 
D.  Best practices for TB control in various states of India 
 
4.15 The Committee has noted some of the best practices and innovative 
approaches to control TB from different States/UTs as mentioned in India TB 
Report 2023, which may be considered for rolling out across the nation: 
 
i. Nalumanipookal (Flowers that bloom at 4 o'clock) is an innovation 
conceptualised in Kerala, combining community participation and peer 
education to fight against TB stigma. Neighbourhood groups (NHGs) of 
women were formed to target TB- free neighbourhoods. Handbooks with 

51  
information regarding TB in the local language were given to 12,536 
NHGs, which were to be discussed by  the NHG leader with the group of 
10-15 women assembled in the weekly meeting. This has catered for 
making TB a household discussion topic and helped screen family 
members through the NHG members. 
 
ii. Nupaal sample collection unit is a microenterprise by a group of women 
spearheading a cost -effective sputum hub and spoke mechanism in the 
Idukki district of Kerala. When the direction for upfront molecular testing 
came from the state TB cell, the challenge was to find a cost-effective and 
efficient way of arranging sputum transportation across the Idukki district 
from the periphery to the nearest NAAT site, where 90% of the area is 
covered by forests and mountainous terrains with the predominantly rural 
population. A group of 9 motivated women agreed to be sputum 
transporters trained and provided with customised bags to hold the tubes 
containing the sample from the periphery to the molecular testing sites. 
This has ensured giving upfront molecular testing to all vulnerable groups 
at a very economical cost of transportation. 
 
iii. Employer-led model for TB and Diabetes care in Brihanmumbai Electric 
Supply and Transport Undertaking (BEST), Mumbai, is an exemplary 
model that can be  emulated in other organisations. BEST intensified TB 
and diabetes screening activities through programmatic collaborations 
with the Mumbai District TB Control Society of Municipal Corporation 
of Greater Mumbai. 
 
iv. Timely transportation of sputum samples and TB drugs is a challenge in  
the mountainous terrain of Himachal Pradesh. In the Mandi district, an 
MoU was executed with a private service provider company for the use of 
a Drone for the transportation of sputum sample and TB drugs between 
the district centre at Mandi  and the block headquarter at Janjehli. The 
usage of drones reduced both transportation time and operational costs to 
one-fourth. 
 
v. The Uttar Pradesh government decided to observe the 15 th day of every 
month as Ni -kshay Diwas across all health establishment s, including 
private practitioners. Under the initiative, the following activities inter alia 
were envisaged: attempted to refer 10%  of total OPD cases for 
presumptive TB testing, enrolment on the Ni -kshay platform, private 
practitioners encouraged to notify TB cases and retrieval of lost to follow- 
up patients with involvement of Gram Pradhans and ASHA workers.  
This resulted in the examination of 31,030 presumptive cases, diagnosis 
of 1312 TB cases and retrieval of 1142 lost to follow-up patients across 

52  
the state in two rounds of Ni-kshay Diwas during December 2022 & 
January 2023. 
 
vi. In the state of Tamil Nadu, the FAST (Find, Assess, Support and Treat) 
centre is being organised as a single window syste m for notification of 
patients diagnosed with  TB, linkage for public health actions and 
treatment adherence support to ensure standards of TB care as well as 
enhance the participation of all private hospitals and professional 
organisations in TB control ac tivities. It is seen that notifications from 
FAST centres have increased by 47% as compared to an increase of 19% 
in non-FAST centres during the period from 2019 to 2022. 
 
4.16 The Committee is of the view that the above-mentioned practices, which 
have been designed in response to specific problems in various geographies to 
address health system - related challenges, may be emulated in other parts of

4.18 India has successfully eradicated Smallpox and Polio from the country. As 
regards the Smallpox eradication program, it is noted that in the 1970s, as a part of 
the Smallpox eradication exercise, supervi sors were trained to go into fields to 
identify the circumstances & problems of Smallpox outbreaks, to identify every 
infected case & to initiate containment measures. Such intensive surveillance 
measures assisted the program in containing the disease and making the country free

from Smallpox. Similarly, for Polio eradication, Rapid Response Teams were

circulation of the polio virus was maintained. Also, environmental surveillance to 
detect Polio virus transmission was also being conducted. Such intensive measures 
helped the country to eradicate Polio.

achieve a complete detection of TB cases. All individuals suspected of having 
TB, whether symptomatic  or asymptomatic, should be brought to clinics or 
diagnostic laboratories for testing, ensuring thorough screening for TB 
positivity, particularly in high - risk areas like slum areas and densely

4.21 The Committee also recommends that all clini cal data on the Ni -kshay

From the data provided by the Ministry, it is observed that over the last five

infections. The Committee believes that as the health of the young population

worked out to address their socio -economic conditions, healthy lifestyle and 
preventive strategies to have a holistic approach to the TB elimination drive. 
(Para 1.12)

Screening, Early Detection and Notification of TB cases 
 
The Committee observes that early detection is crucial, requiring 
screening of vulnerable populations to break the transmission chain. The 
Committee was apprised that lack of control of TB transmission, particularly 

60  
in the poor & slum areas, is leading to the infection ratio, i.e., the number of 
individuals infected by a TB patient, greater than or equal to one and lack of 
testing with X -ray & CBNAAT are delaying the TB cases  detection. The 
Committee believes that the efforts to screen & detect TB at early stages should 
be made on a war footing. Keeping in view the large population of India and 
the target of achi eving TB elimination by 2025, the Committee suggests that 
there is a need to significantly increase the rate of TB case finding by making 
efforts as follows: 
i. Aggressive use of X -ray for faster & confirming TB diagnosis: - The 
National TB Prevalence Survey 2021 shows that out of 100 TB cases, 
clinical assessment picks up only 62 cases while X-ray picks up 95 cases. 
Also, among those diagnosed during the survey, more than 50% did not 
have typical signs or symptoms suggestive of TB but had an abnormality 
in the chest X-ray, which led to their TB diagnosis. Hence, dependence 
on clinical assessment based on symptoms alone and the non - 
availability of X -ray diagnostic facilities led to about 33% of missed 
cases. Therefore, the Committee would like  to suggest that the 
Government ensure that X - ray facilities are available at all public 
healthcare facilities. Further, to speed up the early detection of TB 
cases, deploying Artificial intelligence -enabled portable and hand -held 
X-ray units for screenin g at district as well as block levels should be 
considered. Also, the provision of hand -held X -ray machines at 
registered private centres at discounted rates may also be considered. 
ii. Scaling up molecular laboratories to block levels: - The Committee has 
been informed that in the year 2022, the percentage of PTBER -NAAT 
examinations where molecular diagnostic tests were conducted as the 
first test of diagnosis was 23% of the total PTBER tests conducted 
including microscopy. National TB Prevalence Survey  shows that 
among one lakh people, microscopy picks up 152 TB patients while 
molecular tests pick up 285. Hence, considering the efficiency of 
molecular diagnostics is much higher than microscopy, further 
decentralisation of rapid molecular diagnostics, en suring optimal 
utilisation of NAAT capacity to peripheral levels, at least the Block 
levels, will lead to a significant decrease in missed TB cases per lakh 
population and curb ongoing transmission of the disease. 
iii. Holistic implementation of intensified case finding in OPDs of all 
healthcare facilities: - This involves systematic screening of all people 
seeking care in a health facility or a clinic for identification of people at 
risk for TB. Bi-directional screening of patients visiting the hospitals for 
diseases like diabetes must be brought into wide practice, which 
includes simultaneous diagnosis for TB along with other ailments. 

61  
iv. Contact Tracing: - In the National TB Prevalence Survey 2021, it was 
observed that up to 64% of those with presumptive TB symptoms or 
signs in the general population did not seek care. The Committee is of 
the view that there is a requirement for systematic  planning and 
implementation of active case -finding campaigns among vulnerable 
populations in community settings more aggressively so as to minimise 
the delays in the detection and treatment of TB cases. The Government 
may also consider incentivising anybo dy in the community for a case 
referred to and diagnosed as TB. 
 
 
v. Involvement of the private sector: - The National Strategic Plan 
envisioned obtaining 56% of the total notifications from the private 
sector by 2021, but the country has reached only 32%. Ef fective 
engagement of the private sector on a scale commensurate with their 
dominant presence in the Indian healthcare system is crucial to 
achieving the TB elimination target. The Committee is of the view that 
there is a need for systematic assessment and  a proper strategy to 
promote engagement of the private sector. Various methods like 
expediting the payment of incentives and any treatment cost to the 
private sector, making the Ni -kshay portal more user-friendly for ease 
of reporting & capacity building programme in Information and 
communication Technology (ICT) for private providers. Apart from 
this, there should be a robust monitoring mechanism in place to ensure 
the accountability of private players to create a sustainable public - 
private partnership in TB elimination. 
vi.  Increase in awareness program: - The Commitee is of the view that the 
health- seeking behaviour of the population is also a challenge in the TB 
elimination drive that leads to missed cases. This can be overcome by 
aggressively targeted IEC campaigns in the community to raise 
awareness and improve health-seeking behaviour. 
vii. Region-specific detection pattern: - The causes for the spread of TB can 
vary across different regions due to a combination of social, economic, 
healthcare, and geographical factors. The detection and case -finding 
pattern, along with control measures, should be well strategised with an 
understanding of the differences. 
(Para 3.4)

often lead to discrimination, isolation, and reluctance to seek treatment. Stigma also

impeding efforts to control the spread of TB disease. The Committee feels that

Committee also recommends that an integrated approach through w orkplace 
policies similar to the employer -led model of BEST, Mumbai, must be 
encouraged in all public and private organisations. This approach was also 
adopted during the COVID-19 pandemic in several organisations nationwide. 
It will lead to earlier diagnosis and curb the transmission of TB. 
 
(Para 4.16) 
The Committee believes that the Government may envisage 
comprehensive utilisation of new technologies like drones to aid the 
transportation of samples and drugs between the primary health centres and 
district hospitals in rugged and hilly terrains. The Committee further suggests 
that the concept of FAST centres as a single window system, as undertaken in

clinics/nursing homes without diagnostic capacity may be linked to the nearest

(Para 4.17) 
 
 
Way Forward 
 
The Committee is of the view that on the lines of the above -mentioned 
successful programmes, the TB elimination programme can be strengthened 
regarding surveillance of TB cases. As the Ni -kshay portal has the potential for 
continuous surveillance and monitoring of  each TB patient from detection to 
completion of treatment, it can be use d to contact and track TB patients for early 
detection and treatment follow -up. In view thereof, the Committee suggests 
making the Ni-kshay portal more user-friendly and a one-stop platform for TB 
data collection. Other technological interventions like mob ile applications for 
continuous monitoring of TB patients, district-wise vulnerability mapping, and 
active case finding may also be developed by leveraging digital tools to 
strengthen the surveillance of TB cases. 
(Para 4.19) 
 
The Committee emphasises that the Government should make efforts to 
bring inter - sectoral convergence for strengthening the community effort in 
PMTBMBA on similar lines. As part of endeavours to improve detection, 
integrating an active case-finding drive for TB with the existing house-to-house 
survey conducted by ASHA workers twice a year for the immunisation 
programme can be considered. Conducting household visits is essential to 
achieve a complete detection of TB cases. All individuals suspected of having 
TB, whether symptomatic or a symptomatic, should be brought to clinics or 
diagnostic laboratories for testing, ensuring thorough screening for TB 
positivity, particularly in high - risk areas like slum areas and densely 
populated areas. 
(Para 4.20) 
 
The Committee also recommends that all clinical data on the Ni -kshay 
portal be recorded in standard form and connected through telemedicine so 
that each patient can be viewed and managed by any health service provider 
in the country. Further, the  Committee proposes regular use of telemedici ne 
and meeting apps like Zoom or Google Meet for real -time monitoring and 
follow-up of patients after treatment. 
(Para 4.21) 
 
During interaction with the stakeholders, the Committee noted that malnutrition is 
significantly related to TB and is one of the main problems for TB infection. India

that addressing malnutrition  should be of immense importance to win the 
battle against TB. Therefore, the Committee  suggests that food and nutrition - 
related programmes should be strengthened and integrated with the 
PMTBMBA programme through the public distrib ution system and another 
system, as medicines alone might be insufficient without proper nutrition, and 
both must be integrated. 
(Para 4.22) 
 
The Committee suggests that across the country, there are about 5,000 to 
6,000 public representatives. If they collaborate with officers and NGOs, each 
representative could support around a hundred cases, which is feasible. Public 
representatives like MPs and MLAs can be encouraged to utilise their grants or 
funds for PMTBMBA in their constituency or state to provi de medicines and 
nutritious food. Alongside the existing utilisation of CSR funds, this could 
significantly expand support under PMTBMBA to cover all TB patients 
nationwide. Further, the Committee believes that another area requiring the 
attention of the Government is efficient resource management and its effective 
channelisation, which can motivate representatives at all levels to assist TB 
patients. 
(Para 4.23) 
 
The effective engagement of State Governments is critical for 
successfully implementing centrally sponsored programmes. The Committee 
believes that State Governments should pull up their socks to participate in 
PMTBMBA on mission mode to achieve the target of ending TB by 2025. 
 
(Para 4.24) 
 
The Committee believes that the success of the programme is dependent 
upon patients completing their treatment in its entirety, which sometimes does 
not happen.  Apart from steps like impressing the patient to complete 
treatment, the patient must also  be assured of not losing their employment 
during the treatment period. The Committee suggests the Government address 
the problems related to loss of wage/employment. The Government may also 
consider the training of patients  who completed their treatment to develop 
vocational skills through CSR funds. 
(Para 4.25) 
 
The Committee recommends a special emphasis on PMTBMBA under 
Ayushman Bharat PMJAY through dedicated deployment of staff and

allocation of budget for PMTBMBA under Ayushman Bharat. Ni-kshay portal 
may also be integrated with Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission. This 
integration would create a synergetic approach towards addressi ng the 
healthcare needs of the population, especially in the context of TB elimination. 
By leveraging the resources and benefits of both  initiatives, a more 
comprehensive and efficient response to TB would be achieved, enhancing the

6. Lastly, Dr. Salil Bhargava and Cmde Raman Arora (retd.)  made a presentation which inter 
alia highlighted the following points:-

(i) Implementation of e -PDSA (Electronic Plan Do Study Act) in districts is improving TB 
notification; 
(ii) The role of District TB officers and the need to build their capacity in the areas of 
leadership and quality improvement is pivotal for TB management in the country;   
(iii) There is need of awareness programs in respect of improvement in etiquettes and behavior  
of people in public place like spitting, chewing pan, gutkha, etc; 
(iv) National TB Prevalence Survey shows that out of 100 TB cases, symptoms based diagnosis 
picks up only 62 cases while x-ray based diagnosis picks up 95 cases which depict that more 
x-ray machines are needed; 
(v) Among one lakh TB patients, microscopy picks up 152 TB patients while molecular tests 
(like CBNAAT) picks up 285. Hence the program must pivot from microscopy diagnosis to 
molecular diagnosis; 
(vi) As per Prevalence Survey, active case findings get about 318 cases per lakh while passive 
reporting by notification is around 218 per lakh. Hospital inpatients need to be screened for 
TB;  
(vii) Symptoms of TB like fever, cough, weight -loss are ignored by 70% of  patients. Ignoring 
symptoms leads to ongoing transmission and delay in diagnosis . Every touch point in health 
care need to ask 5 symptom questions; and  
(viii) Risk of TB is highest in first two years after exposure and then declines. In view thereof, 
persons in contact of TB patients like family members, workplace colleagues, etc. must get 
TB prevention therapy to curb the transmission of the disease.
