INTRODUCTION
I, the Chairman, Department-related Parliamentary Standing Committee on Transport,
Tourism and Culture, having been authorised by the Committee to present on its behalf, do hereby
present this One Hundred and Seventieth Report on “Modernization of Major Ports”.
2. Despite a long coastline and long history of maritime activities in the country from centuries
back, the traffic handled by a single international port is more than the traffic handled by all the
Indian Ports put together. As such to take account of what ails the Indian Shipping Sector, the
Department-related Parliamentary Standing Committee on Transport, Tourism and Culture decided
to take up the examination of the functioning of major ports . The Committee reviewed the
upgradation of Indian Ports and the projects under National Maritime Development Programme
which cover the entire gamut of activities proposed for the port modernization target.
3. On 9
th February, 2010, the Committee heard the Secretary, Ministry of Shipping to get an
overview of the subject. The Committee conducted study visits in two phases and visited Kolkata,
Chennai and Kochi in the first phase and covered Haldia - Kolkata, Chennai, Ennore, Tuticorin and
Cochin Ports. During the second phase Paradip, Mumbai, Jawaharlal Nehru, New Mangalore,
Mormugao, Kandla and Visakhapatnam Ports were covered. The Committee heard the views of all
the 12 Port Managements. The Committee also interacted with the stake holders and port
employees unions of various ports during the visits. Thereafter the Committee sought clarifications
from the Ministry of Shipping and heard the views of the Secretary of Ministries of Shipping, Road
Transport and Highways, and the Ministry of Railways on the 20
th December, 2010 on various
aspects of modernization of ports.
4. The. Committee wishes to express its thanks to the Secretary and other officers of the
Ministry of Shipping, Ministry of Road Transport and Highways, Ministry of Railways, Officials
and Employees of the Major Ports for providing necessary inputs and clarifications during
deliberations on the subject.  The Committee also acknowledges the contribution of the unions/
Associations who submitted their valuable suggestions on the subject.
5. The Committee considered and adopted the Report at its meeting held on the 27
th May, 2011.

REPORT
Introduction
There are 12 Major Ports (6 in the east coast and 6 on the west coast) and 187 Minor
Ports in India. The Major Ports are under the control of the Central Government and the minor
ports are under the control of State Governments. Ports provide an interface between the ocean
transport and land-based transport. About 95% by volume and 7% by value of the country’s
external merchandise trade is carried out through maritime transport. The country’s long coastline
of around 7517 km. spread on the western and eastern shelves of the mainland and also along the
islands is a natural resource capable of being harnessed for the country’s trade and tourism
development. The Indian shipping industry not only transports national and international cargoes but
also provides various other facilities such as shipbuilding, ship repairing, lighthouse facilities, freight
forwarding, etc.
2. The main functions of the Major Parts are cargo handling: loading the cargo vessels for
export and unloading the cargo brought by import. Major Ports, however, have to undertake several
ancillary functions; dredging, maintenance of navigational channels, pilotage, berthing, buoying,
bunkering, dry docking including chipping and painting, maintenance of lighthouses and so on. The
multifarious activities make the Port a huge and complex organization. The Indian Major Ports
maintain hospitals, schools/colleges, and guest houses and as such, the number of work force
appears to be on high side when compared with the International Ports.
3. In India, the Major Ports are placed under the Union List of the Constitution, and are
administered under the Indian Ports Act, 1908 and the Major Port Trust Act, 1963 by the
Government of India. Under the Major Port Trust Act, each Port is governed by a Board of
Trustees constituted by the Government of India giving dominance to public enterprises and
government departments. The powers of the trustees are limited and they are bound by directions
on policy matters and orders from the Government of India.
4. Minor Ports are placed in the Concurrent list of the Constitution and are administered under
the Indian Ports Act, 1908. The Act defines the jurisdiction of Central and State Governments over
the Ports. It lays down rules for safety of shipping and conservation of Ports. It regulates matters
pertaining to the administration of Port dues, pilotage fees and other charges.
5. At the State level, the department in-charge of ports or the State Maritime Board is
responsible for formulation of water front development policies and plans, regulating and overseeing
the management of state ports, attracting private investment in the development of state ports,
enforcing environmental protection standards etc.
6. Despite such a long coastline and long history of maritime activities in the country from
centuries back, it is a fact that none of our Major Ports is able to develop into a hub such as
Singapore in the East, Dubai & Middle East on the West and Colombo in the South. The traffic
handled by single international Port like Shanghai Port is more than the traffic handled by all the
Indian Ports put together.
7. The Annual Report of the Ministry of Shipping for the year 2010-11 shows the details of
the port sector achievements in terms of traffic, capacity and performance.

11. After the introduction of new economic reforms in the year 1991, there was drastic change
in the policy of the Government with regard to the functioning of the Major Ports. The
Government felt that in the fiercely competitive world and the emerging challenges a structural
change would be required for improvement of functioning of the Ports.
12. Government appointed a Committee headed by S. Sundar on Port Restructuring in India.
This Report throws light on the main factors that have led to inefficiencies in the Indian ports.
The Report states:
“Most Major Ports were originally designed to handle specific categories of cargo which
have declined in time while other types of cargoes gained importance. The ports have not
been able to adjust to the categories of cargo which grew the most. There are thus several
berths for traditional cargo which are under-utilised, and only a few for new cargo, which
are over-utilised. Equipment utilisation is very poor both because equipment is obsolete and
poorly maintained. Over staffing remains rampant and productivity indicators in respect of
cargo and equipment handling continue to be poor. Documentary procedures relating to
cargo handling such as customs clearance requirements are unduly Complicated and time
consuming. Electronic document processing is still to be introduced in all the ports. Port
access facilities and arrangements for moving inbound and outbound cargo are inadequate
and unsatisfactory. Absence of inter-port and intra-port competition which have been
conducive to substantial productivity increases in other countries is absent in India due to
poor inland connectivity and a policy regime that protected domestic ports against
competitive pressures”.
13. As such, to take account of what ails the Indian Ports, the Department-related Parliamentary
Standing Committee on Transport, Tourism and Culture decided to examine the functioning of
Major Ports. On the 9
th February, 2009, the Committee heard the Secretary, Ministry of Shipping
to get an overview of the efforts being made for the development and modernization of Shipping
and Port Sector in the country.
14. The Committee made study visits to some of the Major Ports in two phases: it visited
Kolkata, Chennai, Ennore and Cochin Ports in the first phase; and Paradip, Mumbai, Jawaharlal
Nehru and Visakhapatnam Ports in the second phase. The Committee heard the views of all the
12 Port Managements. The Committee also interacted with the Port users, stake holders and Port
employee’s unions of various ports during the visits. Thereafter the Committee sought clarifications
from the Ministry of Shipping and heard the views of the Secretaries, Ministries of Shipping, Road
Transport and Highways, and Railways on the 20
th December, 2010 on various aspects of
modernization of ports.
15. In the meetings held by the Committee with the management of various ports, the officials
gave the details of the extent of mechanization and equipment available in the Ports. They also
submitted the details of ongoing and future plans for capacity additions. The Port-wise detail of
infrastructural facilities and future programmes are given in the succeeding paragraphs.
Mumbai Port
16. Mumbai Port is a fully integrated multipurpose port handling all types of cargo like break
bulk, dry bulk, liquid bulk cargo and containers. The port has extensive wet and dry dock facilities
to meet the normal needs of ship wing of the port. The Port provides services/facilities from
pilotage to berthing, storage to delivery of cargo and ancillary services of running CFS, Railway
and also maintenance of crafts, equipment and buildings. The Committee was informed that the
port has 56 berths out of which 45 berths are handling general cargo, five berths for container,
four berths for petroleum oil lubricants (POL) and two for liquid chemical.

5
17. There are three enclosed wet docks namely, Prince’s, Victoria and Indira Docks, having a
total area of 46.30 hectares and quay-age of 7,776 meters inside the wet basin and 853 meters
along the harbour wall.
18. There are two dry docks namely Merewether Dry Dock of 152.40 meters and Huges Dry
Dock of 304.04 meters in length respectively. Merewether Dry Dock is being de-commissioned.
The docks are equipped with electric wharfside crane, floating cranes and mobile cargo handling
equipments.
19. General cargo traffic is mainly handled in Indira Dock constructed in 1914, which has
deeper draft of 9.1 metres. It also has a lock entrance system with 4 berths for handling petroleum
oil lubricants (POL) at Jawahar Dweep, an island off Mumbai Port.
20. The Port recorded highest ever cargo throughput of  57.04 million tonnes surpassing
previous best achieved in 2006-07. However, due to global recession the Traffic had declined by
over 9% during 2008-09 to 51.88 MT.
21. The Committee was informed that Mumbai Port has undertaken various capacity additions
project to overcome the capacity constraints. The main projects are as follows:-
1. Construction of two offshore berths for containers with estimated cost of Rs.l460 crore;
2. Construction of second chemical berth with estimated cost of Rs. 116 crore;
3. Development of 18-21 ID Harbour wall berth with estimated cost of Rs. 468 crore;
4. Captive coal, berth by Tata Power Consultancy with estimated cost of Rs.58 crore;
5. Construction of 5
th Oil Berth with estimated cost of Rs.662 crore.
22. The Committee was also informed by stakeholders that the ageing labour population and old
cranes are partly responsible for the lower productivity levels at the Mumbai Port. A daily loss of
two hours between the third shift and the day shift affects productivity due to the break in
operations. Ideally the port should work 24 hours a day.
23. The Committee was informed that some equipments require replacement and the ban on
purchase of new vehicles need to be rifted as the existing ones are very old. The Ministry of Shipping,
however, clarified that the Port is empowered to replace old machineries with its own resources.

27. The JN Port handled 4.061 million TEUs of container traffic during the financial year 2009-10

11 metres below Chart Datum. However, with the help of tidal window, vessels having draught

stations in operation and NOC to 23 more have been awarded.
30. The Port has undertaken the following projects under modernization plans which are under

(i) Acquisition of 1 new Rail Mounted Quay Crane.
(ii) Acquisition of 1 Rail Mounted Gantry Crane (RMGC).
(iii) Acquisition of 3 Nos. New Super Post Panamax RMQCs as replacement of three old

(iv) Acquisition of 3 Nos. New Super Post Panamax RMQC and Shifting of three old

harbour channel which gradually reduces to 12.8m. Expected quantity of capital dredging is

Kandla Port
32. Kandla Port is on the western cost of India and is holding a leading position as regards the
tonnage handled among the twelve Major Ports. As a post-independence development and loss of
Karachi Port to Pakistan, the Government of India decided to develop Kandla as a Major Port in
order to cater the vast hinterland spread over the North Western and Central States. The Kandla
Port has witnessed a steady and sustainable growth since then in the manifold activities of the
EXIM trade of India.
33. As on date, Kandla Port has 12 dry cargo berths and six oil jetties at Kandla and other
facilities at Vadinar Terminal Cargo berths 1 to 10 are handling dry cargo, berths 11 and 12 are
meant for container traffic. This terminal has been set up on BOT basis. Out of the 6 oil jetties
at Kandla, four are owned by the Port Trust and the rest are operated by private agencies.
34. The Port proposes to acquire two 50 tonnes bollard pull tugs from Public Sector
Undertaking Shipyards i.e. M/s HSL or CSL and hire four numbers of 50 tonnes bollard pull tugs
as part of the modernization programme.
35. V essel Traffic Management System (VTMS) in the Gulf of Kutch is under implementation
and is likely to be completed by March, 2011.
New Mangalore Port
36. The New Mangalore Port Trust was constituted w.e.f. 1
st April 1980. The Port is located
at Panambur, Mangalore in the western coast of India. The Port is a all weather modern port and
is also an artificial lagoon port. The major commodities handled by the Port are crude and POL
products, LPG, coal, limestone, granite stones, timber logs, finished fertilizers, iron ore fines/lumps,
edible oil, liquid ammonia, phosphoric acid, chemicals, containerized cargoes, etc.
37. Cruise vessels are calling at the Port regularly since Mangalore has attractive tourist spots
like Jain temples, historic monuments, farm houses, cashew factories, enchanting beach resorts,
old temples, etc. The Committee was informed that to provide more facilities for handling the
increased number of cruise vessels, a modern cruise lounge at a cost of Rs.2.87 crores has been
constructed and put into operation in 2008.
38. At present, the Port has 14 berths having the draft depth ranging from 7 meters to 14
meters. Some of the berths are able to accommodate vessels having Dead Weight Tonnage (DWT)
of 85000 million tonnes. The total capacity of the Port as on 31.3.2010 is 44.20 million tonnes
out of which 14.70 million tonnes for general cargo, 7.50 million tonnes for Iron Ore and 22.00
million tonnes for Crude & POL products. The capacity utilization was 80% during 2009-10.
39. The Port handles both import and export of cargo. The major cargo imported is crude oil
which measures to about 60% of the total cargo handled.
40. The Committee was informed that the following projects are under various stages as part
of modernization process at the New Mangalore Port:
(i) Coal handling facility for UPCL at a cost of Rs.230 crores on BOT basis;
(ii) Mechanisation of Iron Ore handling facility at a cost of Rs.296 crores on BOT
basis;

Paradip Port
47. Paradip Part is situated at the Eastern coast of India in the State of Orissa. The port is

wide approach channel. The dredging activities are under progress to make the length of the
entrance channel to 2000  meters and its width to 240 meters. The approach channels are to be
increased to 10000 meters long and 300 meters wide after the completion of dredging. The port
has 14 berths as on date. The port has handled 57.01 million tonnes of cargo during 2009-10
period which is an all time high rate of its traffic handlings during the past years. This period has

iron ore and 26% comprised of thermo coal. The liquid cargo has shown a tremendous growth

Cochin Port
61. The modern Port of Cochin was developed during the period 1920-1940. By 1930-31, the
Port was formally opened for vessels up to 30 feet draught. Cochin was given the status of Major
Port in 1936. The Administration of the Port got vested in a Board of Trustees on 29
th February,
1964 under the Major Port Trusts Act, 1963. Port of Cochin is located at Kochi in Kerala on the
south west India. The Cochin Port is a natural and all weather port.
62. The Committee was informed that its strategic location on the south-west coast of India
is having a commanding position being at the cross roads of the east-west ocean trade. The Port
is a natural gateway to the vast industrial and agricultural produce markets of the south-west India.
The hinterland of the port includes the whole of Kerala and parts of Tamil Nadu and Karnataka.
A study carried out on the traffic flow in the  hinterland of the Port indicates that about 97% of
the total volume of traffic is accounted for by Kerala. The hinterland of Cochin Port has further
spread over to different areas with the  growth or containerization in the country and establishment
of Inland Container Depots at different load centers in India. The Cochin Port with its proximity
to the international sea route between Europe and the far East and Australia can attract a large
number of container lines offering immense sailing opportunities.
63. The Cochin Port has deep draft alongside berths with modern cargo handling equipments
and modern ship to snore cranes provided for container handling. The approach channel up to the
Cochin Gut, is about 1000  meters long with a designed width of 200 meters and maintained
dredged depth of 13.8 meters. From the Gut, the channel divides into Mattancherry channel and
Ernakulam channel, leading to west and east of Willingdon Island respectively and berthing facilities
for ships have been provided in the form of wharves, berths, jetties and stream moorings alongside
these channels.
64. Cochin Port is India’s first e-port having fast documentation and efficient administration of
services bracked by a fully computerized network and single window facility for faster
documentation with modern communication facilities. The Port is having adequate warehousing
capacity and is linked to the Inland Container Depots of South India by road and rail. The Port
is a congestion-free one and has the personalized service to its users. The Port also has the
distinction of zero incidence of pilferage.

record traffic of 174.29 lakh tonnes during 2009-10  as against 154.94 lakh tonnes in the previous
year registering an increase of 12.49%. Container traffic has touched an all time record of 2,89,817
TEUs which is an increase of 11.13% over the previous year’s 2,60,784 TEUs. 1270 vessels
cleared during 2009-10  as against 1085 in the previous year, showing an increase of 17.05%.

highest being 43 in the year 2007-08. As regards the productivity parameters, the Port registered

Chennai Port
102. Chennai Port established 125 years ago, has old infrastructure which is inadequate meet the
demands of the modern day transport sector. Normally, reserve capacity of 30% is required at each
Port to take care of the maintenance works at the berths, approaches, equipments, etc. Hence,
there is need to create additional capacity as the Port is handling volumes wel1 above its rated
capacity of 50 million tonnes. This shortfall is now being addressed by upgrading the existing
berths (modernization of Jawahar Dock and other berths in the inner harbour in stages) and also
creating new berths such as the Second Container Terminal at Dr. Ambedkar Dock, Mega Container
Terminal at North of Bharathi Dock etc.
103. Due to historical reasons, the draft at the inner harbour berths of Chennai Ports varies
between 7.91 meters and 11 meters which are not adequate to handle the larger vessels prevalent
in the international trade. There is also need to handle higher parcel sizes and modern cargoes such
as containerized cargoes and project cargoes in line with the directions of the Ministry to provide
draft of atleast 14 meters in all channels, basins and alongside berths. The NMDP scheme of
Deepening of channels, berths and basins was formulated and works are being taken up in stages.
The work has been completed in Jawahar Dock and other berths will be taken up in a phased
manner. Capital dredging will follow the berth modernization.
104. As in the case of infrastructure upgradation, there is a need to increase loading and
discharge rates of vessels at berths so that Ports are able to compete at the international level. As
part of this process, Chennai Port Trust is replacing old cranes with modern high capacity cranes
in a phased manner. In addition, a semi-mechanized closed coal conveyor system has been installed
for improving productivity levels in coal handling (from Jawahar Dock East berths to the coal plots
at the Southern end of the Port) and also reduce dust pollution levels.
105. A Multi-pronged strategy is being adopted for better utilization of the available land area
especially as Chennai Port Trust suffers physical constraints in expansion due to the Port being
surrounded by Chennai city on 3 sides. The problem is being addressed by:-
a. Dismantling old sheds/warehouses which have outlived their useful lives to create open
space.
b. Creating additional area by reclaiming land from the sea as in the case of area of 7.85
Hectares east of East Quay.

109. The Committee notes that the traffic handled by the Major Ports has increased from
313.53 million tonnes in 2002-03 to 560.96 million tonnes during 2009-10. The aggregate
capacity of the Major Ports has also increased from 362.75 million tonnes per annum to
628.03 million tonnes during that period. The Committee is, however, constrained to note
that despite the increase in capacity and traffic handled by the Major Ports, there has been
negligible growth in efficiency parameter at Major Ports. For example, average turn around
time has gone up from 4.24 tonnes in 2001-02 to 4.5 tonnes in 2009-10 and also the average
pre-berthing detention time has increased from 11.04 hours in 2000-01 to 12.19 hours in

compared to some of the Ports in nearby countries. Even today the majority of Indian
exports/imports are carried through transshipment centers at Ports in Colombo, Singapore
and Dubai. The average dwell time at Indian Major Ports is 1.88 days for import and 3.78
days for export of containers in comparison to the dwell time at Singapore Port is 0.6 days
for import or export of containers.

DREDGING
122. Most Indian Ports operate at drafts less than 10 meters (33 ft). This directly undermines
their capability to service large international ship. Thus lower draft restricts cargo movement to a
large extent capacity. In fact, internationally the average draft is 14.5 meters but even in the biggest
container port of India, Jawaharlal Nehru Port (JNPT) average draft is 12.5 meters. Colombo Port,
for example has (18.5 meters) draft, which can easily handle new-generation ships carrying over
6,000 containers. The result is that large container ships, which are bound for India, are forced
to be routed via Ports where they can be broken down into smaller cargos, for onward
transportation. Thus transportation cost and travel time increases. Deepening projects critical for
Indian Ports for their competitiveness, has been largely neglected.
123. The Ministry informed that with the increase in size of the cargo vessels in the shipping
trade, almost all the Ports are not capable of receiving bigger vessels with the drafts available at
present. Deepening of channel and berth basins, therefore, becomes necessary. Dredging
Corporation of India (DCI) is catering to maintenance/capital dredging requirements to the extent
of 65% for maintenance dredging.
124. The Committee was also informed that most of our Major Ports are incurring expenditure
annually for both capital and maintenance dredging in order to achieve adequate draft. Some Ports
have also received gross budgetary support (GBS) from the Government for undertaking dredging
activities. In the recent past, this includes Paradip Port and currently projects funded by
Government support are being undertaken at Cochin and Tuticorin Ports as wall.

216. The Committee was informed that all the Major Ports are manned by CISF. All the Ports

Autonomy in Major Ports
289. The Committee was informed that the Ports enjoy adequate autonomy in as much as the
delegated financial powers to the Ports are exercised by Board/HoD. The extent of autonomy to
the Port is reviewed from time-to-time depending upon their experience and needs.
290. The Port Trusts are autonomous bodies functioning under the Major Port Trusts Act, 1963.
The powers that are delegated to Ports for undertaking various works, taking loan, sanction of
projects etc. are reviewed from time to time. As far as private ports are concerned, they are under
the administrative control of respective Maritime States and not covered under the MPT Act, 1963.
These private Ports or non-Major Ports are getting more cargo share of the total cargo handled
in India mainly because they are new, have large investment and infrastructure equipped with latest
technology. The Ministry of Shipping is in the process of giving more autonomy to the Major Ports
by way of corporatization. Already Ennore Port Ltd., which is a major port, has been corporatized,
Ministry of Shipping is in the process of corporatizing more Ports in the line of EPL pursuant to
a meeting taken by Hon’ble Prime. Minister on 3
rd February, 2010. After corporatization, the Major
Ports would be in a position to have more financial power as per the delegation of powers
applicable to the PSUs. At present EPL, which also enjoy mini-ratna status can take investment
decision upto Rs.500 crores. Further, the Ministry informed that the delegation of financial power
is an ongoing process and orders are issued from time to time.
291. The Committee was informed that the existing administrative, financial and other powers
with the Major Ports are not proving to be bottleneck to the developmental activities of the Ports.
The Major Ports have been given administrative and financial powers for entering into contracts

expenditure to capital. As far as developmental work is concerned, the Port Trust Board has been
delegated power of Rs.50 crores for new projects and Rs.100 crores for replacement as per Annual

292. The enactment of the Major Port Trusts Act in 1963 marked a new milestone in the
evolution of the port laws in India in relation to Act of 1908. The Major Port Trusts Act, 1963

51
for the first time laid out the institutional framework for creation of a separate port authorities for
each major port and defined the powers and functions of such a port authority in respect of all
aspects of port functioning. Until enactment of Major Port Trusts Act, 1963, the major and minor
Ports in India were directly under the administrative control of the Central or State Government
and were managed through a separate ministry or a departmental organization. It was also informed
that the Major Ports are functioning under the Major Port Trusts Act, 1963 and the powers that
are delegated to Ports are reviewed from time to time. However, the Committee has been informed
by all the Ports. about the delay in decision making by the government on most of their
administrative and modernization programmes as a result by which the major works are get delayed
at various levels of implementation. It is true that the delay is causing very dearer to the Major
Ports whereby the adjoining private Ports are benefiting out of the situation.
293. A major amendment to Major Port Trust Act of 1963 was notified in 1997, following the
announcement of Guidelines on Participation of Private Sector in Indian Port Sector in 1996. The
amendment brought into being Tariff Authority for Major Ports (TAMP), to independently regulate
the fixation of various port related tariffs, to be charged by Major Ports. The TAMP sought to
ensure a level playing field between the upcoming private port developers entering the Indian port
sector and the existing Major Ports.
294. The passage of the Act also paved the way for decentralization and rationalization of port
administration. Despite its salutary effects, the MPT Act, 1963 has lately been criticized for its
limiting provisions in the post-liberalisation phase in the development of Indian port sector.

company enhanced financial and operational autonomy and empowers it to invest up to Rs.1000
crore of 15% of their net worth on a single project without seeking Government approval. In a
year, these companies can spend up to 30% of their net worth not exceeding Rs.1000 crore. They
will also have the freedom to enter joint ventures from alliances and float subsidiaries abroad.
296. Further, Navratna status is conferred by Department of Public Enterprises. To be qualified
as a Navratna, the company must obtain a score of 60 (out of 100). The score is based on six
parameters which include net profit to net worth, total manpower cost to total cost of production
or cost of services, PBDIT to turnover, EPS (Earning Per Share) and inter-sectoral performance.
Additionally, a company must first be a Miniratna and have four independent directors on its board
before it can be made a Navratna. This classification is applicable only to PSUs and cannot be
applied to port Trust.

Tariff Authority for Major Por ts (TAMP)
303. As private sector was allowed entry into the Major Ports to provide services often in
competition with the Port Trust themselves, there was a demand from the private sector for an
independent regulator to set port tariffs in order to ensure that there was no unfair competition
between themselves and the Port Trust. Tariff Authority for Major Ports (TAMP) was set up in
March, 1997 through an amendment of the Major Port Trust Act, 1963 to fix and revise port
tariffs. All powers for fixing tariffs in major port lies with TAMP, but it has no jurisdiction over
minor Ports or private Ports. TAMP is only an authority for fixing tariffs and has no other
regulatory functions or powers. All the conservancy powers in Ports and all other regulatory
function in regard to safety etc. are vested in the Port Trust themselves. Despite considerable
degree of operational powers the port trusts formed under the Major Port Trust Act lack
teeth and suffers in executing power and authority.
304. The Committee observes that TAMP has also not been specifically mandated to
improve ef ficiencies or lay down quality of services standards in port operations. However,
the Major Port Trust Act provides that while fixing tariffs the conditions under which the
service is to be rendered can be prescribed. Using this as a mechanism, TAMP is now
seeking to relate tariff to efficiencies. But each port has its own tariff schedule and scales;
the accounting procedures are also different. The Committee, therefore, feels that it would
be necessary for TAMP in the first instance to establish some common accounting
procedures amongst Ports and move towards fixing uniform principles for fixation of tariffs;
this would go a long way towards promoting inter-port competition.
305. However, the Committee is also of the view that with private investment coming in
shipping industry, in view of competition in the port sector Government should consider
setting up a tribunal to hear complaints against port tariffs.
306. The Committee is of the view that to strengthen Major Ports, a policy whereby the
two Acts, namely Indian Ports Act, 1908 and Major Ports Act, 1963, can be amalgamated

53
into a single Act should be conceived. The consolidated Act should oversee operation of both
major and minor ports in India as both the Acts have become old and outdated. Since the
inception of these two Acts, the Indian shipping sector has undergone tremendous
transformation in its working structure, with significant private participation now being
witnessed in the sector.
307. With the coming of minor ports in the country, the pace of development has
slackened for the Major Ports. Since minor ports are governed by the Indian Ports Act, 1908
and are outside the purview of the Tariff Authority of Major Ports (TAMP), a single
comprehensive Act will provide a level playing field to the Major Ports in terms of
infrastructure development, better railway networking etc. Currently, the tariff structure
also differs to a great extent for major and minor ports of the country, which needs to be
bridged.
308. To develop Indian ports to global norms there is an urgent need for the Government
to change its role from a regulator to being a facilitator. There is an urgent requirement
for aggressive second generation regulatory reforms, backed by the requisite legal and fiscal
changes to give Indian Ports a competitive edge in the global market.
309. A port promotion council could be formulated to take initiative on the developmental
activities as well as the proper marketing of the services available with the Port. Since the
performances of the Ports are little bit negative during the last years, it would be more
desirable if proper monitoring on the performance be made regularly with immediate
measures to rectify the faults and drawbacks.
Income Tax
310. The Major Ports are constituted and duly notified under the Major Port Trusts Act, 1963
and are autonomous bodies administered by statutory Board of Trustees constituted by the Central
Government. All Major Ports were allowed exemption under Section 10(20) of the Income Tax Act,
1961 as they were recognized as ‘local authority’. However, with the insertion of an explanation
of sub section 20 of Section 10 of Income Tax Act, 1961 with effect from 1.4.2002 the tax
authorities have excluded Major Ports from the ambit of prevailing benefits of exemption in
taxation.
311. It is pertinent to mention here that the Major Ports invest their revenues for maintenance
and development of infrastructure facilities which are utilized on a common user basis by trade and
industry and hence they provide essential services for the growth of trade and commerce in the
country. The investment on maintenance and development of infrastructure facilities ultimately
benefits the general public in way of supplementing to growth of trade and commerce in the
country. Exemption from income-tax would enable Ports to charge less thereby bringing down the
transaction cost for trade. There, they should be covered by sub clause (iii) of the explanation.
Department of Revenue has been accordingly, requested in December, 2009 to include sub
Clause (v) in the explanation to Section 10(20) which may read as “Board of Major Ports as
referred to under Section 3 of Major Port Trust Act, 1963”.
312. The above inclusion in the Explanation will restore the exemption status of Major Port
Trusts from taxation under Section 10(20) of the Income Tax Act which existed prior to 1.4.2002.
313. The Committee is of the view that Public Sector entities which are not purely
commercial units have to meet social obligations. To help in further welfare objective of the
Government must be viewed differently and be kept out of the purview of the income tax.
The Committee therefore recommends that the Ministry of Shipping may take necessary
action to include the sub-clause (v) in the explanation to Section 10(20) of the Income Tax

54
Act in order to restore the income tax exemption to the Major Ports as requested by the
Ministry.
Public Private Partnership
314. W ith the opening up of the Indian economy, the Government has allowed private sector
participation in Major Ports to infuse funds, induct latest state-of-the-art technology, improve
management practices and above all for addition of the capacity. FDI upto 100% under automatic
route is permitted for construction and maintenance of Ports and harbour. To encourage private

Request for Qualification (RFQ) and Request for Proposal (RFP) have been finalized. A Model
Concession Agreement (MCA) has been finalized to bring uniformity in the Concession Agreements.
TAMP fix the charges to be paid by the port users by the private operators.

direction of Planning Commission to go for PPP mode for the commercially viable projects to

funds through budgetary supports. The Major Ports are not restricted to replace the cargo handling
equipments and mechanization of the berths and terminals that are being run by the Ports. There

Cabotage Issue
338. India follows a Cabotage policy by exercising powers under Section 406 and 407 of the
MS Act, 1958 dealing with licensing of ships. Section 406 governs the provision of granting license
to two categories of ships
(i) Indian ships within the meaning of section 21 of the Act
(ii) Ships chartered by an Indian entity
339. Such licenses include the license for the whole or any part of the coasting trade or India.
Section 407 deals with licensing of foreign flag ships for the remaining category for coasting trade
which are essentially foreign flag ships not chartered by any Indian entity.

59
340. For the successful commencement of operation of India’s first Transshipment Terminal
there is need for immediate resolution of the Cabotage issue, which has serious implications. To
be developed and recognized as a transshipment hub port in the region, the ICTT need to mainly
attract the mainline vessels, which are at present transshipping the Indian bound/origin containers,
mainly at Colombo. The ICTT is thereby expected to facilitate price competitiveness of the Indian
produce and reduce the dependence on foreign ports for transshipment, in the strategic interest of
the Indian Exim trade.
341. The Committee was informed that the Cabotage Law (Section 406 and 407 of Merchant
Shipping Act, 1958) acts as a serious deterrent in the diversion of India bound transshipment traffic
from Colombo and in facilitating the development of ICTT as a transshipment hub port. The
existing Cabotage Law mandates that movement of containerized cargo between Indian Coastal
Ports is permissible only on Indian flag vessels. This effectively means that a foreign shipping line,
irrespective of whether it operates a ‘feeder’ , non-feeder or mainline vessel, would not be able
to carry a container either to or from ICTT to any other port in India.
342. M/s. India Gateway Terminal Private Limited (IGTPL), Cochin, the BOT operator of ICTT
had sought the intervention of Cochin Port Trust to request the Government for considering the
relaxation in Cabotage Law. They have inter alia stated that they have taken up the matter of
exemption for containerized trade at ICTT from the applicability of Cabotage with the Director
General of Shipping and also with the Ministry of Commerce and Industry, etc. They have further
requested for a waiver of the Cabotage Law and for the correct interpretation by the authorities
regarding its applicability to containerized EXIM cargo proposed to be transshipped from
Vallarpadam (ICTT).
343. M/s IGTPL have mentioned that the sizeable investments made by them as well as by the
Government of India in providing rail-road connectivity and for the deepening of navigational
channel, would be jeopardized in case no waiver is granted to ICTT. The Cochin Port Trust has
already made a representation to the Ministry of Commerce in June, 2009 seeking their intervention.
344. The Government is empowered to grant relaxation regarding Licensing of ships for coasting
trade in terms of Section 407, sub section (3) of the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958. The relevant
provision is quoted below:
“The Central Government may, by general or special order, direct that the provisions of sub-
section (1) shall not apply in respect of any part of the coasting trade of India or shall
apply subject to such conditions and restrictions as may be specified in the order.”
345. Some of the important aspects in this regard, to be taken cognizance of, are as given below:
• The present annual volume handled at Colombo port is around 3.80 million TEUs, of
which roughly 75% (2.85 million TEUs) is total transshipment. Of this, about 70%
(1.99 million TEUs) per annum is transshipment from Indian Ports, and out of which,
60% (1.19 million TEUs) is to and from South Indian Ports; which could be the
potential business for ICTT Vallarpadam.
• On commissioning of Vallarpadam, many advantages will accrue to Indian exporters, in
terms of reduction in feeder service costs and faster shipping service if the cargo is
routed through Cochin instead of Colombo.
• This advantage, however, can be optimized if the present system of transportation of
containers by the Main line operators through Colombo port where they are re-routing
their India bound transshipment containers through foreign flag vessels of their own
or of the lines in  their global consortia, is permitted at ICTT, Vallarpadam too.

60
• As per the existing regulations, the present capacity of the Indian flag Ships engaged
in coastal transportation is highly inadequate even to handle present total traffic of 1.19
million TEUs bound for South India. Moreover, some of these ships are also engaged
in transportation of India based coastal containers on fixed routes and would not be
available.
• In effect, the Cabotage regulations will act as the limiting factor for attracting the
traffic from the port of Colombo as most of the main line carriers would like to
continue there at, availing present advantage of sharing slots on heir ships amongst the
members of consortia operating worldwide.
• While it is essential to protect the interest of the Indian flag vessles in coastal trade,
protection of the interests of the Indian port at Vallarpadam being developed as major
transshipment hub, and also the Indian EXIM trade at large cannot be undermined.
346. In view of the critical implication of this regulation in the successful
implementation of the ICTT project and in the larger interest of economic self-reliance
of the Indian EXIM trade, it is imperative that the Cabotage Law is relaxed to enable
transshipment of containers through foreign flag vessels from ICTT, Cochin. The
Committee, therefore, recommends that the Government should immediately undertake a
review of the Cabotage law and take appropriate decision in consultation with all the
stakeholders involved.

OBSERV ATIONS/CONCLUSIONS/RECOMMENDATIONS — AT A GLANCE
The Committee notes that the traffic handled by the Major Ports has increased from
313.53 million tonnes in 2002-03 to 560.96 million tonnes during 2009-10. The aggregate
capacity of the Major Ports has also increased from 362.75 million tonnes per annum to
628.03 million tonnes during that period. The Committee is, however, constrained to note
that despite the increase in capacity and traffic handled by the Major Ports, there has been
negligible growth in efficiency parameters at Major Ports. For example, average turn around
time has gone up from 4.24 tonnes in 2001-02 to 4.5 tonnes in 2009-10 and also the average
pre-berthing detention time has increased from 11.04 hours in 2000-01 to 12.19 hours in
2010-11. Though the average output per ship per berth day has gone up substantially from
6961 tonnes in 2000-01 to 12133 tonnes in 2010-11 (up to December, 2010) which, however,
is not a matter of great solace as all our Major Ports are far behind as efficient as
compared to some of the Ports in nearby countries. Even today the majority of Indian
exports/imports are carried through transshipment centers at Ports in Colombo, Singapore
and Dubai. The average dwell time at Indian Major Ports is 1.88 days for import and 3.78
days for export of containers in comparison to the dwell time at Singapore Port is 0.6 days
for import or export of containers. (Para : 109)
While going into the reasons for low efficiency of the Major Ports, the Committee
finds that inadequate Port capacity, old and out-dated cargo handling equipments/
machineries, lower draft, non-availability of berth, poor road and rail connectivity with
Ports, inadequate navigation aids facilities, narrow entrance channels, low labour
productivity, shortage of storage spaces, regulatory restrictions, low IT applications and
delay in obtaining sanction/clearance from the Ministry are some of the major factors that
are affecting the efficiency of the Major Ports, adversely. (Para : 110)
The Committee found and the stakeholders also complained that cargo handling
equipment/machinery at the Ports were very old, installed decades ago and the right type
of equipment required by the trade were either not available or insufficient. The Committee
also found that modern container handling equipment like Quay Gantry Cranes (QGC) were
available in some Ports only Chennai, Cochin, Mumbai, Vishakhapatnam and JNPT like.
Similarly, Rubber Tyred Gantry Crane, Rail Mounted Gantry Crane and other similar
equipments are not in adequate numbers at most of the Major Ports. (Para : 111)
The Committee finds that proposals of Major Ports for replacement of old cargo
handling equipments/machineries with modern ones are in the process stuck at one level or
the other. The Committee came to know that Major Ports are authorized to undertake a
work worth only Rs.50 crore for new projects and Rs.100 crore for replacement of outdated
equipments. A general feeling amongst the port management, Port trustee and port users
was that lack of adequate financial powers with the Ports in undertaking developmental
projects was the major factor for delays. The Committee, therefore, recommends that all
the proposals for modernization/expansion submitted by the Major Ports may be processed
at the fast track and step be taken to ensure that the projects were cleared/completed in
time, particularly there for the replacement of old cargo handling equipment and other
machinery. The Committee further recommends that Government should enhance the
present limit (Rs.50 crore) for new projects and (Rs.100 crores) for replacement of
equipments available to the Major Ports so that Ports get more autonomy. (Para : 112)

The Committee observes that TAMP has also not been specifically mandated to

making units. This has taken away the job of thousands of workers working in the Ports.
(Para : 323)
The Committee recommends that government should examine the experience and
performance of PPP abroad particularly in countries which have been the mother of the PPP
contracts. It would not be in the interest of the nation to allow foreign MNCs to take over
the port related activities. Moreover, the Ports have strategic Naval defence systems within
its periphery, which will be open to monopoly of Multi National Companies who may control
Ports and Indian defence secret will be exposed to Foreign Countries. (Para : 324)
Major and Minor Ports
The Committee observes that Major Ports are facing a stiff competition from the minor
ports. The Committee does not agree to the view of the Ministry that the presence of private
ports creates a healthy competition amongst the ports. The Committee is of the view that the
competitiveness of the Major Ports is missing. The Board and Managements of these ports
including operations are regulated by the Government as a result thereof the Major Ports
largely remain confined to their respective hinterland. Major Ports are also serving the cause
of welfare of the society. On the other hand the private ports are enjoying full autonomy in
their functioning and are working purely with the commercial motives. (Para : 332)
The Committee is constrained to note that Government failed to protect the interests
of the Visakhapatnam Port, a PSU unit that could not control the situation which led to
setting up of Gangavaram Port. The Gangavaram Port was initially proposed by the
Visakhapatnam Port Trust to serve as a satellite port of the Visakhapatnam Port Trust to
meet the requirement of SAIL steel plant with a view to reduce the air pollution at the
Visakhapatnam area. However in the year 2000 the State Government had formulated a
steering committee for Gangavaram Port Development with State Government as the nodal
agency. The project was ultimately taken away from the Visakhapatnam Port Trust and
handed over to the private operator. (Para : 333)
The Committee is of the view that Vishakhapatnam Port has to face a stiff competition
from Gangavaram Port. The Visakhapatnam Port for years remained number one among the
Major Ports in cargo traffic. However, during the past two-three years it has slipped to second
position behind Kandla mainly due to the fact that a major portion of its cargoes consisting
of coking coal, thermal coal and others have been diverted to the Gangavaram Port. The

77
Committee, therefore, recommends that the Vishakhapatnam Port should be given all
financial assistance and autonomy to ensure that the upgradation projects including deepening
of draft, deepening of Ports, strengthening of berths in inner harbour, development of truck
parking terminal and mechanical handling facilities and inner harbour are completed in a
time-bound manner to compete with the Gangavaram Port. The Committee has observed that
the loss of Gangavaram Port from the hands of Visakhapatnam Port is a classical example
how dear the delay in timely decision making could be. (Para : 334)
The Committee also recommends that the Government should come out with
appropriate guidelines to ensure that no port is coming up in the vicinity of the Major
Ports. Even if some port has to be developed near to the Major Ports, the Major Ports
should be given the option to develop it rather than allowing other agencies encroaching
into those areas which may ultimately affect the functional viability of the Major Ports.
(Para : 335)
A blanket ban on privatization in the maritime sector is not possible. The
Committee, however, recommends that in the broad interest of the nation, the
Government’s endeavor should be to take steps for expansion and improvement of the
Public Sector and to ensure that tax payers money invested in the PSU do not go waste.
(Para : 336)
The Committee also recommends that the Government should ensure that the
management of private ports fulfills their obligations/promises towards the society. There
are reports that the people of Gangavaram and Dibbapalem villages were protesting for not
providing employment to the fisherman by the management of Gangavaram Port.
(Para : 337)
Cabotage Issue
In view of the critical implication of this regulation in the successful implementation
of the ICTT project and in the larger interest of economic self-reliance of the Indian EXIM
trade, it is imperative that the Cabotage Law is relaxed to enable transshipment of containers
through foreign flag vessels from ICTT, Cochin. The Committee, therefore, recommends that
the Government should immediately undertake a review of the Cabotage law and take
appropriate decision in consultation with all the stakeholders involved. (Para : 346)
Tourism
The Committee recommends Tuticorin Port authorities to earmark a few waters front
for this purpose. The Committee has observed that tourism can supplement to the revenue
earnings of the Ports. Separate cruise terminals at suitable places need to be maintained
for the benefit of the visitors. Care should be taken for the easy access of the terminals
to the main tourist places in the area. Also proper security clearance facilities should be
created at the respective places in consultation with security agencies. (Para : 348)
Inland Water Transport
The Committee is of the view that linking of water ways as in the case of National
Waterways 1&2 with the Kolkata Port system, National Waterway-3 with the Cochin Port and
the proposed plan for linking of National Waterway-5 with Paradip Port all will supplement to
the inland movement of cargo in a more economical way. The Committee therefore
recommends that more such options may be worked out in other parts of the country so that
it may have a positive impact on the Port activities in the adjoining areas. (Para : 351)
