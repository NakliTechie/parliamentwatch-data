REASONS FOR VARIATIONS / UNDER UTILIZATIONS DURING, 2022-23, 
2023-24 AND CURRENT FINANCIAL YEAR 2024-25: 
5. For 2022-23, the initial budgetary provision was ₹ 3009.05 crore, which was 
revised to ₹ 3363.07 crore at the RE stage. The Ministry incurred an expenditure of ₹ 
3284.12 crore, which is approximately 97.65% of the revised allocation. 
6. For 2023-24, the budget was set at ₹ 3399.65 crore, later increased by ₹ 60.35 
crore to ₹ 3460 crore at the RE stage. The Ministry's expenditure for the year was ₹ 
3406.90 crore, representing 98.46% of the revised estimate. 
7. For 2024-25, the budgetary provision is ₹ 3260.93 crore, including ₹ 190.01 
crore under the Capital section. Although the Ministry proposed an RE allocation of ₹ 
3756.55 crore, reflecting an increase of ₹ 495.62 crore, the RE allocation remained the 
same as the BE allocation. The Committee acknowledges that the Ministry has 
undertaken additional activities such as the Kumbh at Prayag and the Republic Day 
tableau, which were not included in the RE stage. However, it is expected that the 

7 
 
allocated budget for 2024-25 will be utilized optimally as per the Ministry's planned 
activities. 
8. The Committee observes that there is a significant reduction in the 
Ministry's total budget from ₹ 3,406.90 crore (actual) in 2023-24 to ₹ 3,260.93 
crore (RE) in 2024-25 and an increase to ₹ 3,360.96 crore for 2025-26. The 
Committee further observes the reduction in the Ministry’s total budget from ₹ 
3,406.90 crore (actual) in 2023-24 to ₹ 3,260.93 crore (RE) in 2024-25 can be 
attributed primarily to the one-time expenditures related to the Azadi Ka Amrit 
Mahotsav (AKAM). The AKAM celebrations saw a substantial increase in 
expenditure, with a revision of the Budget Estimate (BE) from ₹ 185 crore to ₹ 
335 crore during 2023-24, resulting in an expenditure of ₹ 334.39 crore, which 
was more than 80% over and above the original BE allocation. 
9. The Committee acknowledges that the reduction in budget during the 
2024-25 fiscal year is a reflection of the conclusion of AKAM-related activities, 
which were exceptional in nature and not a recurring part of the Ministry's 
regular expenditure plans. However, the reduction in the budget allocation for 
2024-25 compared to 2023-24 highlights the need to ensure that the Ministry's 
cultural preservation and development initiatives are adequately funded, 
especially in light of long-term goals for heritage conservation.  
10. The Committee further notes that the budget formulation process for 
2024-25 did not adequately account for key national cultural commitments, 
including the Kumbh at Prayag and the Republic Day tableau, leading to an 
additional request for funds mid-year. The omission of such significant 
expenditures at the BE stage does seem to indicate deficiencies in financial 
forecasting and contingency planning. The Committee recommends the 
implementation of a comprehensive Multi-Year Budgetary Framework (MYBF) 
for the Ministry of Culture that extends beyond the annual budget cycle. This 
framework should incorporate: 

8 
 
a) A five-year rolling budget projection with annual revisions to accommodate 
changing priorities while maintaining long-term vision. 
b) Categorization of expenditures into core recurring activities, planned 
development initiatives, and special events/commemorations with separate 
budgetary provisions for each category. 
c) Establishment of a Budget Stabilization Reserve of approximately 5% of the 
total annual allocation to account for unforeseen cultural activities or 
emergencies, preventing mid-year fiscal disruptions. 
This approach would mitigate the abrupt budgetary fluctuations witnessed 
between 2023-24 and 2024-25, and provide greater stability for cultural 
institutions dependent on Government funding. 
11. The Committee further notes that although overall budget utilisation 
remains high, expenditure patterns across various cultural schemes have been 
inconsistent. Programmes such as museum development, cultural mapping, and 
global cultural engagement have experienced fluctuating levels of fund 
disbursement, with some initiatives facing under-utilisation in certain years and 
excessive allocations in other.  The Committee strongly recommends the 
development of a Cultural Calendar-Based Budgeting system that: 
a) Maintains a comprehensive five-year cultural events calendar that identifies 
recurring festivals, commemorations, and international engagements with 
historical budget requirements. 
b) Integrates cyclical cultural events (such as Kumbh Mela, which occurs every 
12 years) into long-term financial planning through a dedicated cultural events 
fund. 
c) Implements quarterly budget review mechanisms to assess expenditure 
against planned activities and make necessary adjustments before the Revised 
Estimates stage. 

9 
 
d) Develops an AI-assisted forecasting model that analyzes historical expenditure 
patterns and predicts resource requirements for upcoming cultural activities 
based on past event costs, inflation factors, and attendance projections. 
This systematic approach would ensure that cultural events of national 
importance receive appropriate financial provisions at the Budget Estimate stage 
rather than relying on mid-year adjustments 
 
12. The Committee observes a pattern wherein the Ministry relies heavily on 
mid-year budgetary adjustments, seeking substantial increases at the RE stage 
instead of ensuring that BE allocations accurately reflect financial needs. In the 
opinion of the Committee, this approach introduces uncertainty into long-term 
financial planning and may disrupt the timely execution of projects. A more 
accurate and predictive budget estimation process is required to minimise 
dependency on RE-stage adjustments. The Committee recommends that the 
Ministry strengthen its engagement with the Ministry of Finance to establish a 
more flexible, needs-based budget allocation framework, one that will ensure 
funding provisions are more effectively met with practical expenditure 
requirements. Also, the Ministry should establish a Corporate Social 
Responsibility (CSR) engagement cell within the Ministry to facilitate 
partnerships between businesses and cultural projects, capitalizing on the 100% 
tax exemption available under Section 80G(ii) of the Income Tax Act

14. The Committee recommends a more robust allocation for the conservation, 
restoration, and maintenance of protected monuments, especially the World 
Heritage Sites. Considering the increasing pressure on ASI’s existing resources, 
the allocation need to be increased to ensure timely conservation and meet global 
standards for preservation. It is recommended to create a dedicated Heritage 
Conservation Fund with a corpus of ₹ 500 crore over five years, with 
contributions from tourism revenue, CSR initiatives, and matching government 
grants through the National Culture Fund framework. This fund would 
specifically address emergency conservation needs and climate vulnerability 
mitigation measures at protected monuments.  
14.1 The Archaeological Survey of India (ASI) manages 3,698 centrally 
protected monuments under the Ancient Monuments and Archaeological Sites 
and Remains (AMASR) Act, 1958, encompassing temples, forts, tombs, and 
prehistoric sites. Despite recent efforts, including a 70% budget increase to ₹ 
443.53 crore in 2023–24 and the delisting of 18 untraceable monuments like 
Delhi’s Barakhamba Cemetery and Haryana’s Kos Minar No.13, challenges such 
as encroachment, climate threats, and resource gaps persist. To address these 
issues, parliamentary committees recommends a multi-pronged strategy. First, a 
GIS-based digital inventory with real-time monitoring and biennial audits 
should be prioritized to enhance accountability and prevent monuments from 
disappearing due to urbanization. Strengthening legal frameworks, including 

12 
 
stricter penalties under the AMASR Act and fast-track courts for heritage 
violations, would streamline enforcement against encroachments. 
14.2 Climate resilience measures, such as expanding Automated Weather 
Stations (AWS) and pollution monitoring labs to high-risk sites like Konark Sun 
Temple, must be scaled to mitigate environmental damage. Concurrently, 
integrating AI-driven predictive maintenance, 3D mapping, and drone 
surveillance—as seen in Tamil Nadu’s excavation projects and Mehrauli’s recent 
Supreme Court-mandated survey—can revolutionize preservation through early 
damage detection. Community engagement is equally critical; initiatives like a 
tax-incentivized “Adopt a Monument” program and training locals as heritage 
stewards would foster grassroots ownership. 
14.3 Finally, delisting protocols require reform. An independent panel should 
reassess criteria to ensure delisting occurs only after rigorous documentation and 
public consultation, paired with a relic repatriation policy for displaced artifacts. 
By combining technology, legal rigor, climate adaptation, and community 
participation, India can balance heritage conservation with developmental 
pressures, safeguarding its cultural legacy for future generations. These steps 
would align with ASI’s mandate while addressing systemic gaps in monument 
management. 
15. The Committee recommends addressing delays in ongoing Museum 
Projects like the Dholavira Museum and the Leh Museum as they have faced 
significant delays due to issues such as land allotments, extending timelines by 8–
12 months. The committee further recommends prioritizing the resolution of 
these delays, ensuring that land allotments are expedited and necessary 
clearances are streamlined. A dedicated task force or coordination mechanism 
should be set up to oversee and speed up the approval processes for critical 
infrastructure projects. 

13 
 
16. The Committee recommends comprehensive digitalization of heritage 
assets to enhance both preservation efforts and public engagement: 
a) Develop a phased implementation of "Digital Twin" technology for all 
World Heritage Sites and at least 100 nationally significant monuments by 
2028. This would involve creating precise digital replicas to monitor 
structural changes, simulate conservation interventions, and enhance 
visitor experience through augmented reality applications. 
b) Establish a centralized Heritage Monitoring System using remote sensing 
and IoT devices to provide real-time data on environmental conditions, 
structural stability, and visitor impact at critical monuments. This would 
enable predictive maintenance and early intervention, potentially reducing 
long-term conservation costs by 30-35%. 
c) Launch a Metaverse Heritage Initiative that creates immersive digital 
experiences of inaccessible or vulnerable monument areas, allowing for 
virtual tourism while reducing physical impact on fragile structures. This 
initiative should prioritize monuments facing access restrictions due to 
conservation concerns. 
d) Implement block chain technology for monument documentation and 
conservation history, creating an immutable record of interventions and 
condition assessments that ensures accountability and preservation of 
institutional knowledge across administrative transitions. 
16.1 Recommendation: Institutional Reforms for Museum Project 
Implementation: The Committee recommends fundamental reforms to the 
project implementation mechanisms: 
a) Implement a modular development approach for museum projects, enabling 
phased completion and partial operationalization while resolving issues with 
remaining components. This would allow for incremental public access and 

14 
 
revenue generation rather than complete closure during extended development 
periods. 
b) Develop a standardized pre-implementation framework for museum projects 
that mandates resolution of land acquisition, environmental clearances, and 
conceptual design before budget allocation and project announcement, 
minimizing mid-project disruptions. 
c) Create specialized project management units for each major museum 
initiative with representation from relevant stakeholders, including state 
governments, ASI, content specialists, and community representatives to ensure 
coordinated action and accountability. 
 
16.2 To address content finalization delays as observed in the Digital Museum 
project at Kanakakunnu Palace, the Committee recommends: 
 
a) Establish a permanent Museum Content Advisory Board comprising 
historians, cultural anthropologists, museologists, and digital content specialists 
to expedite content development processes across all museum projects. 
b) Implement concurrent content development alongside infrastructure creation 
rather than sequential processes, with rolling approvals for thematic sections to 
prevent complete project stagnation during content refinement. 
c) Develop a shared digital content repository for Ministry museums that enables 
efficient resource sharing and adaptation of existing content for new museums, 
reducing redundant research and development costs. 
d) Integrate emerging technologies such as AI-assisted curation, interactive 
installations, and personalized visitor journeys through mobile applications to 
enhance the museum experience and attract younger audiences. 
e) Implement a "Living Museum" approach that encourages regular content 
updates and community contributions, moving away from static permanent 

15 
 
exhibitions toward more dynamic and participatory models of heritage 
presentation. 
16.3 Recommendation: Heritage-Led Regional Development Strategy: The 
Committee recommends leveraging archaeological sites for sustainable local 
development: 
a) Implement a Heritage-Led Regeneration approach, integrating archaeological 
sites into broader regional development plans. This would involve creating 
archaeological tourism circuits with appropriate infrastructure, interpretation 
facilities, and community engagement. 
b) Establish Archaeological Heritage Zones around significant excavation sites 
with special planning regulations, economic incentives for heritage-sensitive 
development, and community stewardship programs.

ENHANCING EFFORTS FOR CULTURAL REPATRIATION 
29.1 The Archaeological Survey of India, operating under the Ministry of 
Culture, is spearheading this significant cultural recovery effort, which includes 
the Kohinoor and beyond it to encompass thousands of artifacts removed during 
British colonial rule. 
29.2 To strengthen India’s campaign for cultural repatriation, the government 
should consider establishing a specialized “Heritage Recovery Task Force” 
comprised of diplomats, legal experts, archaeologists, and art historians. This 
multidisciplinary team would be dedicated exclusively to identifying, 
documenting, and pursuing claims for stolen Indian artifacts worldwide. The 
task force could develop comprehensive dossiers on priority items, including 
detailed provenance research, legal arguments for restitution, and cultural 
significance documentation to bolster India’s claims. 
29.3 India should leverage its growing economic and diplomatic influence to 
negotiate cultural property agreements with more countries, similar to the recent 
pact with the United States. These agreements should establish clear mechanisms 
for identifying looted artifacts, resolving disputes, and facilitating returns. 
Special attention should be paid to former colonial powers like the United 
Kingdom, where many significant Indian treasures remain. These agreements 

24 
 
could be tied to broader diplomatic and trade relationships, creating incentives 
for cooperation on repatriation issues. 
29.4 To strengthen legal arguments for repatriation, India should invest in 
cutting-edge technological tools for provenance research and authentication. 
Advanced imaging techniques, DNA analysis of organic materials, and AI-
powered databases could help establish the Indian origin of disputed artifacts 
and track the chain of ownership. This scientific evidence could prove invaluable 
in making compelling cases for return, particularly for items with unclear 
provenance records. 
29.5 Additionally, India should explore innovative public-private partnerships 
to support repatriation efforts. The government could establish a “Heritage 
Repatriation Fund” that accepts contributions from corporations, wealthy 
individuals, and the Indian diaspora to support legal challenges, negotiate 
purchases when necessary, and fund transportation and conservation of 
returned artifacts. This approach would expand the resources available for 
recovery efforts while engaging broader segments of society in the mission.

10.  The meeting resumed at 02: 40 P.M. to hear the views of the Secretary, Ministry of 
Culture on Demands for Grants (2025 -26) of the Ministry . At the outset, the Chairman opened 
the meeting by welcoming the Members of the Committee, officers and representatives from the 
Ministry of Culture.  
11.  Shri Arunish Chawla, Secretary, Ministry of Culture started his presentation by 
presenting a comprehensive overview of the Ministry’s financial allocations and key policy 
initiatives. He stated that the Ministry has been allocated Rs. 3,360 crore s for the financial year 
2025-26, reflecting a marginal increase over the previous year. The Ministry has consistently 
maintained a high expenditure rate, with 98.47% of the allocated budget utilised in the preceding 
financial year and 89% already expended  in the current fiscal cycle. He outlined the 
implementation of seven Central Sector Schemes, which include Kala Sanskriti Vikas Yojana, 
Centenaries and Anniversaries, Development of Museums, Development of Libraries and 
Archives, Gyan Bharatam Mission, Gl obal Engagement Scheme, and the National Mission on 
Cultural Mapping. The Secretary also highlighted major accomplishments, such as the successful 
organisation of the World Heritage Committee Meeting in July 2024, the repatriation of 655 
antiquities, and t he recognition of Garba of Gujarat as part of UNESCO’s Intangible Cultural 
Heritage.  
12.   The Secretary emphasised the Ministry’s ambitious vision under Rachanatmak Bharat: 
namely, the National Mission on Creative Economy, which seeks to integrate India’s cultural and 
artistic heritage with modern economic frameworks while initiatives like the Cultural Satellite 
Account, aimed at quantifying the economic contribution of cultural activities, as well as the 
integration of major monuments with tourism circui ts through modern technological solutions, 
including Artificial Intelligence and Augmented Reality. He reassured the Committee that steps 
were being taken to expedite recruitment processes for vacant positions in ASI, museums, and 
national academies.  

41 
 
 
13.  Thereafter, the Ministry elaborated on its financial strategies, explaining categorization of 
the budget under three verticals: Establishment Expenditure, covering administrative costs and 
institutional overheads; Central Sector Schemes, dedicated to the  implementation of key cultural 
programmes; and Autonomous Bodies and Grantee Institutions, which provide financial support 
to cultural academies, museums, and research institutions.  
14. The Ministry highlighted its efforts to optimize fund utilization an d streamline 
administrative processes. It emphasized the budget for cultural infrastructure and digital 
modernization, with greater investments allocated to digital repositories, archival preservation, 
and museum enhancements. It was informed that internat ional collaborations and public -private 
partnerships were actively being pursued to reduce reliance on domestic financial allocations and 
secure expertise for large -scale cultural preservation projects. The Ministry asserted that its  
approach to prudent f iscal management, strategic planning, and innovative funding mechanisms 
would significantly bolster its ability to preserve the country’s rich cultural legacy and enhance 
global cultural influence. 
15.   The Committee raised the following issues during the course of the Meeting:  
 Members queried on the reasons behind certain funds remaining underutilized  despite an 
increase in budgetary allocations and sought clarity on measures to ensure timely 
expenditure. 
 Concerns were raised regarding vacancies in key i nstitutions such as the ASI, museums, 
and national academies, and their impact on research and preservation efforts. 
 Members raised q ueries regarding the decreasing number of archaeological excavations 
compared to previous years, particularly in historical ly significant sites such as 
Balirajgarh and Harappan settlements. 
 Members sought clarification on whether tribal dances, oral traditions, and other 
indigenous cultural elements were being adequately documented and supported. 
 Members highlighted the need f or financial assistance, pension schemes, and capacity -
building programmes to safeguard the livelihoods of folk artists and craftsmen. 
 Concerns were expressed regarding the perceived inactivity of Zonal Cultural Centres 
and their engagement with local communities. 
 Members sought to know the conditions of artefacts stored in museum basements and the 
Ministry’s plan for their proper preservation and exhibition. 
 Members welcomed efforts to reclaim antiquities from the United States  and urged 
similar bilateral agreements with the United Kingdom and other nations to recover 
significant cultural artefacts.
