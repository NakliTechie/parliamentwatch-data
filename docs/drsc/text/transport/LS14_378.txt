INTRODUCTION 
A well-structured road network is crucial for economic growth and social 
connectivity. The Ministry of Road Transport and Highways (MoRTH) plays a 
pivotal role in developing and maintaining this infrastructure. 
2. The Ministry of Road Transport and Highways (MoRTH) is responsible for 
the construction and maintenance of National Highways, the administration of key 
legislative frameworks, including the National Highways Act, 1956, and the Motor 
Vehicles Act, 1988, as well as the formulation of broad policies on road transport, 
automotive standards, and cross-border vehicular movement. The Ministry plays a 
pivotal role in ensuring an efficient, sustainable, and well-regulated road transport 
system, underpinning national economic growth and connectivity. 
3. Associated Offices under the Ministry are: 
⮚ National Highways Authority of India (NHAI) 
⮚ National Highways & Infrastructure Development Corporation Ltd. 
(NHIDCL) 
⮚ Indian Roads Congress (IRC) 
 
GOALS AND OBJECTIVES 
4. The Output-Outcome Monitoring Framework (OOMF) is a performance 
measurement tool to assess the effectiveness of various schemes and projects. 
Outputs refer to the immediate, measurable deliverables of a project, such as the 
number of highways constructed or the number of safety audits conducted. 
Outcomes, on the other hand, represent the long-term impact and effectiveness of 
these outputs, such as improved mobility, reduced accidents, or increased private 
sector investment. The OOMF ensures transparency, accountability, and efficiency 
 

2 
 
in infrastructure development and policy implementation. The OOMF of the 
Ministry sets out the following objectives- 
⮚ National Highway Development (allocated budget ₹2,76,475. 09 crore)-  
To achieve this objective, with an allocated budget of ₹2,76,475.09 crore, it 
sets forth a target of 10,000 km of new highways to be constructed in the 
financial year 2025-26 including the development of 1,100 km of highways in 
the North-Eastern States and 750 km in tribal areas. Also, a high-speed corridor 
network covering 5,800 km is also planned to be operationalised. 
⮚ Private Investment and Public-Private Partnerships (PPP)-  
The Ministry aims to secure ₹35,000 crore in private investment for highway 
projects during the FY 2025-26 year. Furthermore, 30% of the total awarded 
highway projects is being targeted to be executed under the Public-Private 
Partnership (PPP) model. 
⮚ Asset Monetisation and Resource Mobilisation- 
The Government has set a target of ₹30,000 crore to be raised from the 
monetisation of developed highway stretches, utilising existing infrastructure to 
generate revenue. 10% of total budgetary resources are expected to be sourced 
from asset monetisation efforts. 
⮚ Road Safety and Accident Reduction- 
The framework outlines the removal of 1,000 identified black spots on 
national highways. Furthermore, 40,000 km of highways will undergo 
comprehensive road safety audits. 
⮚ Vehicle Scrapping, Inspection, and Certification (allocated budget of 
₹595.02 crore)- 
The plan includes the establishment of 130 Automated Testing Stations 
(ATSs) to improve vehicle inspection standards across various States and Union 
Territories. Additionally, 15 Registered Vehicle Scrapping Facilities (RVSFs) 
are planned to be developed to phase out obsolete and polluting vehicles, 
promoting cleaner and more fuel-efficient transport systems. 

3 
 
⮚ Training and Capacity Building- 
The Government plans to set up four Institutes of Driving Training and 
Research (IDTRs) and seven Regional Driving Training Centres (RDTCs). 
Moreover, 25 small driving training centres will be established to improve 
access to professional driver training. Also, five training programmes for road 
safety auditors will be conducted to develop expertise in accident prevention 
and highway safety measures. 
⮚ Emergency Care and Cashless Treatment for Road Accident Victims-  
Under this objective, it aims for the implementation of cashless treatment 
facilities for road accident victims across all 36 States and Union Territories.

allocation and utilization. While this percentage represents a substantial 
commitment to road infrastructure, the slight decrease from previous year's 
allocation requires implementing a more strategic planning framework. Given 
that global passenger and freight travel is expected to increase significantly, 
India must position itself for this future demand through meticulous planning 
and resource optimization. The Ministry should transition from traditional 
planning approaches to data-driven, simulation-based infrastructure 
planning. Traffic modeling and virtual testing of infrastructure modifications 
can significantly enhance the effectiveness of expenditure by identifying 
optimization opportunities before physical implementation begins. This 
approach allows planners to analyze underlying problems, test different 
scenarios, and create more efficient transportation strategies that improve 
connectivity while maximizing return on investment1. Additionally, the 
Ministry should develop a comprehensive spending plan that incorporates not 
just immediate construction needs but also accounts for future urban 
population growth. 
13.1. The spending plan should incorporate explicit provisions for remote 
areas, industrial corridors, and tourist destinations with clear timelines and 
key performance indicators. Each proposed project should undergo rigorous 
cost-benefit analysis that factors in both direct economic benefits (reduced 
travel time, decreased vehicle operating costs) and indirect benefits (regional 
economic development, improved access to healthcare and education). 
Incorporating these elements will transform the spending plan from a mere 
budget document into a strategic roadmap for national connectivity and 
economic empowerment. 
14. The Committee's concern regarding the shortfall in NHAI funding 
(allocation of Rs.1,70,266 crore against the projected Rs.1,87,803 crore) 
requires a more comprehensive solution than simply seeking additional funds. 
This 9.34% reduction (Rs.17,537 crore) threatens critical highway expansion 
projects and risks significant delays in land acquisition processes. However, 
evidence suggests that funding shortfalls are often not the only reason for 
languishing highway projects; delays in obtaining appropriate approvals and 
clearances from various government agencies equally contribute to project 
deferrals and cost overruns. 

11 
 
14.1 The Ministry should implement a dual-track approach to address this 
challenge. First, it should conduct a thorough portfolio analysis of all NHAI 
projects to identify those at critical stages where funding delays would cause 
disproportionate impact, prioritizing these for immediate resource allocation. 
Second, the Ministry should establish a centralized clearance facilitation unit 
that works across government departments to expedite approvals for highway 
projects, particularly those affecting critical economic corridors. This 
approach addresses both the financial constraint and the procedural barriers 
that frequently derail infrastructure timelines. 
14.2 Furthermore, the Ministry should explore alternative procurement and 
financing models. The current challenges preventing wider adoption of 
Public-Private Partnerships (PPPs) need systematic analysis and resolution, 
particularly in contracting and monitoring processes, the Ministry can 
introduce innovations that allow private capital to supplement government 
funding without compromising project viability or public interest. This 
approach would help bridge the current funding gap while establishing 
sustainable models for future infrastructure development. 
15. The allocation shortfall for North-Eastern States (Rs.19,499.09 crore 
against Rs.21,449 crore) requires contextualization within broader 
development initiatives for the region. The Cabinet has already approved 
continuation of schemes under the Ministry of Development of North Eastern 
Region. These include the North East Special Infrastructure Scheme 
(NESIDS) and various other targeted funding initiatives. 
 
15.1 The Ministry should develop an integrated approach that coordinates 
road infrastructure development with these existing schemes to maximize 
impact. Rather than viewing the Rs.1,950 crore shortfall in isolation, the 
Ministry should collaborate with MDoNER to ensure complementary 
development that addresses gap-filling needs of the eight North Eastern 
States. This coordination should focus on three critical areas: strategic border 
connectivity, access to social infrastructure (hospitals, educational 
institutions), and linkages that enhance economic opportunities. 
 

12 
 
15.2 Additionally, the Ministry should prioritize projects that align with the 
PM-DevINE initiative, which focuses on larger and high-impact proposals 
under infrastructure, social development, and livelihood sectors. By

34. The Ministry, in its submissions, reaffirmed its commitment to enhancing 
the quality and durability of road construction, acknowledging that while

 36. On the matter of issues in drainage system along the National Highways, the 
Ministry has apprised the Committee that the dimensions and design of drainage 
systems are determined based on various factors, including natural or existing 
streams, catchment areas, runoff water, surface water flow, and local rainfall 
patterns and intensity. These parameters are thoroughly analysed by the Detailed 
Project Report (DPR) Consultant and the design team of the Engineering, 
Procurement, and Construction (EPC) Contractor, following which a finalised 
design undergoes proof-checking and safety verification by independent 
specialised agencies. To prevent waterlogging, regular maintenance is undertaken 
by the contractor throughout the construction and implementation phases, as well 
as during the Defect Liability Period (DLP) and subsequent maintenance period. 
This ensures the optimal functionality of drains and cross-drainage structures, 
facilitating the unhindered flow of water and effective drainage management.

monsoon seasons before significant damage occurs.

    At the outset, the Chairman, welcomed the Members of the Committee and Secretary 
and other officials from the Ministry of Road Transport and Highways (MoRTH), National 
Highways Authority of India (NHAI), and National Highways and Infrastructure 
Development Corporation Limited (NHIDCL). 
2.  The Chairman outlined the primary objectives of the meeting , which centred on the scrutiny 
of budget allocations, the efficiency of p roject implementation, the status of road infrastructure 
projects, and the effectiveness of road safety measures . He underscored the urgency of ensuring 
the timely completion of national highways, addressing infrastructural impediments, and 
implementing sustainable transport solutions.  
3.  The Ministry commenced its presentation by outlining the budgetary allocations for the 
financial year 2025 -26, reporting a total allocation of ₹2,87,000 crore . The primary budgetary 
provisions comprised ₹2,76,500 crore for National Highways Development and Maintenance, 
₹10,400 crore for State Roads under the Central Road and Infrastructure Fund (CRIF), ₹400 
crore for Road Transport and Road Safety Initiatives, and ₹300 crore for the development of 
Ropeways and Cable Car Projects. The Ministry further reported the expenditure status for FY 
2024-25, noting that ₹2,44,500 crore had already been utilised, amounting to 85% of the total 
allocation, with an expected utilisation of 90% by the financial year’s conclusion. 
4.  On t he investment and funding mechanisms , the Ministry highlighted its reliance on private 
sector participation and asset monetisation . It reiterated its dependence on public-private 
partnerships (PPPs), the Toll Operate Transfer (TOT) model, and Infrastructur e Investment 
Trusts (InvITs) to ensure sustained financial inflows for national highway development. 
5.  Regarding national highway expansion and connectivity improvements, the Ministry affirmed 
its commitment to constructing 10,000 km of new national high ways in FY 2025 -26, with a 
focus on enhancing connectivity in the North-Eastern region (1,100 km) and tribal areas (750 
km). It further detailed its plan to expand high -speed corridors from 4,600 km to 5,800 km, 
develop 10 port -connectivity roads, and cons truct 15 new ropeways to improve accessibility in 
hilly terrains. The Ministry acknowledged that, as of July 2024, 697 projects remained pending , 
with the highest number of delays recorded in Maharashtra (98), Karnataka (40), and Haryana 
(21) with the the  principal causes of project delays being attributed to land acquisition hurdles, 

40 
 
railway clearance bottlenecks, financial constraints affecting contractors, and regulatory delays . 
Land acquisition issues, which accounted for 35% of all project delays , were  attributed to 
protracted disputes over compensation, inaccuracies in land records, and opposition from local 
stakeholders, often resulting in legal cases. Furthermore, 30% of all stalled projects were delayed 
due to railway clearance processes , particular ly in securing approvals for road overbridges 
(ROBs) and underpasses . The Ministry informed the Committee that inter-ministerial 
consultations with Indian Railways have been initiated to streamline approval processes and 
introduce time-bound clearance protocols. 
6.  The Ministry highlighted contractors’ financial distress , noting that several firms were 
struggling with escalating project costs, delays in government disbursements, and increasing 
input costs. It reiterated its commitment to facilitating improved credit access for contractors and 
enforcing a performance -based evaluation framework to enhance financial discipline and 
accountability. Additionally, the Ministry highlighted delays in regulatory and environmental 
clearances, particularly for projects  traversing protected forest areas and ecologically sensitive 
zones. It assured the Committee that efforts were being made to fast-track approvals through a 
single-window clearance mechanism and enhanced coordination with the Ministry of 
Environment, Forest and Climate Change (MoEFCC). 
7.  On the subject of road safety, the Ministry presented its Vision 2028 strategy, which seeks to 
reduce road fatalities by 95% by 2028. The Ministry outlined its efforts to rectify 1,000 accident-
prone black spots during FY  2025-26, with the broader objective of eliminating all hazardous 
locations by FY 2027-28. It further detailed the expansion of Driving Training Centres (DTCs), 
with the aim of establishing at least one centre in each district  to enhance driver competence and 
licensing standards. Updates were also provided on the cashless treatment scheme for accident 
victims, which are presently at the pilot stage in six states , with a commitment to nationwide 
implementation in the ensuing fiscal year . The Ministry stated its intention to introduce 
Intelligent Traffic Management Systems (ITMS), AI -driven automated surveillance, and stricter 
penalties for traffic violations and also integration of  drone-based monitoring of encroachments 
into the enforcement framework, with s cheduled quarterly assessments in urban regions and 
biannual inspections in rural areas. 
8. During the deliberations of the Meeting, Members  raised several concerns and sought 
clarifications on the following issues: 
 Members questioned the Ministry’s assertion that road fatalities could be reduced by 95% 
by 2028 , given the slow pace of black spot rectification and inadequate enforcement 
mechanisms. 
 Concerns were expressed regarding the insufficient number of operational DTCs , with 
only 28 centers established for 650 districts. 
 Members sought explanations for several highway projects that had been inaugurated but 
remained stalled for over two years. 
 Query was made on the Mumbai Coastal Road (₹14,000 crore) , which required repairs 
within a year of completion which may point to serious lapses in quality control. 
 Challenges in awarding contracts and monitoring quality control. 
 Gatishakti Mission and need for breaking departmental silos and fostering integrated 
planning for infrastructure projects. 
 Members widely c riticized the unilateral decision -making by NHAI officials , without 
engaging local MPs and state authorities in highway planning. 

41 
 
9.  To address these concerns effectively, the Committee made the following actionable 
recommendations: 
 Expediting the establishment of Driving Training Centres (DTCs)  in every district within 
two years, using Viability Gap Funding (VGF) or Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) 
contributions. 
 Implementing a structured six -monthly review mechanism  to assess the progress of 
black-spot rectification efforts. 
 Ensuring mandatory deployment of ambulances  along accident-prone National Highway 
stretches. 
 Introducing third-party quality control audits  for all major National Highway projects to 
ensure contractor accountability. 
 Institutionalising consultations with MPs and state governments  to improve highway 
alignment and infrastructure planning. 
 Enhancing inter -ministerial coordination  between the Ministries of Road Transport, 
Railways, and Ports to eliminate procedural delays. 
10. The Chairman concluded the meeting by stressing the urgency of improving transparency, 
efficiency, and accountability  in the transport sector. While reminding all attendees of the 
confidentiality of the proceedings, the Ministry was directed to submit a written response within 
one week , detailing measures for expediting pending projects, implementing black -spot 
rectifications, and enhancing road safety initiatives.
