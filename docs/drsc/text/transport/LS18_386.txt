Introduction 
 I, the Chairman, Department-related Parliamentary Standing Committee  on
 Transport,  Tourism and Culture, having been authorised by the 
 Committee, to present on its behalf this Three Hundred  and  Eighty-Sixth 
 Report on the subject “Holistic Development  of India’s Tourism Sector 
 through  Formal  Skill  Development  University- Level Courses 
 and Professional  Training  for Guides, Hospitality and Eco-Tourism.” 
 
2. The Committee selected this subject in view of the growing strategic importance of 
tourism as a pillar of economic growth, employment generation, cultural preservation, 
and India’s global engagement. Tourism is uniquely positioned as a sector that 
integrates infrastructure, services, heritage, livelihoods, and soft power. The 
Committee notes that while physical infrastructure and destination promotion have 
received sustained policy attention in recent years, the human dimension of tourism 
has not evolved at a commensurate pace. 
 
3. The Committee is of the considered view that the future trajectory of India’s tourism 
sector will be determined not merely by the creation of assets, but by the quality, 
integrity, and professionalism of the human interfaces through which tourists 
experience the country. Guides, hospitality professionals, destination managers, 
cultural interpreters, and facilitators collectively shape visitor perception. Deficiencies 
in training, ethical conduct, and career structures in these roles directly erode trust and 
suppress tourism’s economic potential. 
 
4. Recent policy initiatives announced by the Government, including the creation of a 
National Destination Digital Knowledge Grid and the institutional upgradation of the 
National Council for Hotel Management and Catering Technology into a National 
Institute of Hospitality, represent significant structural interventions. The Committee, 
however, observes that such initiatives can yield sustained outcomes only when 
anchored in a comprehensive framework for formal education, professional 
certification, continuous skill upgradation, and institutional accountability. 
 
5. In the course of examination of the subject, the Committee held multiple oral 
evidence sessions with the Secretary and senior officials from the Ministry of 
Tourism. The Committee also received detailed presentations from academic and 
training institutions, including the National Council for Hotel Management and 
Catering Technology, Indian Institute of Tourism and Travel Management, Indian 
Culinary Institute, Jamia Millia Islamia, State Institute of Hotel Management 
Faridabad, Banarsidas Chandiwala Institute of Hotel Management, Madurai Kamaraj 
University, the Central University of Tamil Nadu, FHRAI,IATO, Hotel Association 
of India (HAI), and ATOAI. The Committee places on record its sincere appreciation 
for the comprehensive presentations and inputs received during the examination of 
this subject. During its sitting on 4 February 2026 with the Secretary, Ministry of 
Tourism, the Committee examined in detail the Ministry’s three‑pillar skill 
development framework, institutional presentations from NCHMCT, IITTM, ICI, 
universities and industry associations, and the alignment of tourism skilling with the 
Union Budget 2026‑27 priorities of employment generation and local economic 
development. 
 

iii 
 
 
6. To examine implementation at the regional and institutional level, the Committee 
undertook a study visit to Chennai on 21 January 2026. During this visit, the 
Committee interacted with officials of the Ministry of Tourism, representatives of the 
Government of Tamil Nadu, and faculty members of Central and State universities. 
These interactions provided valuable insights into decentralised models of tourism 
education, university-community partnerships, and the integration of heritage, 
research, and livelihood development. An oral evidence was again held in New Delhi 
on 4th February, 2026 with the Ministry of Tourism and other industry stakeholders. 
 
7. This Report synthesises evidence received from the ministry of Tourism, institutions, 
industry stakeholders, and academic experts. It places human capital at the centre of 
tourism policy and emphasises the need for standardisation, ethical conduct, digital 
capability, and coordination between the Ministry and other industry stakeholders. 
The Committee expects that the recommendations contained in this Report will 
contribute to professionalising the tourism sector, restoring visitor trust, and 
positioning India as a globally competitive tourism destination. 
 
8. The Committee places on record its appreciation of the assistance rendered by the 
officers of the Rajya Sabha Secretariat and the cooperation extended by the Ministry 
of Tourism, institutions, and stakeholders who contributed to this examination. 
9. The Committee considered and adopted the Report in its meeting held on the 11th 
February, 2026.

Executive Summary 
1. This Report presents a comprehensive assessment of the present state of tourism in 
India, with particular reference to formal skill development, university‑level education 
and professional training frameworks. The Committee notes that the Union Budget 
2026‑27 explicitly identifies tourism as a significant employer and a key driver of 
local economic development, which in turn underscores the need for a structured 
human capital framework rather than a series of project‑centric interventions. 
 
2. The Committee observes that tourism has emerged as one of the most 
employment‑intensive sectors of the Indian economy, with strong multiplier effects 
across agriculture, handicrafts, transport, construction and allied services. The sector 
currently contributes approximately five to six and a half per cent of national GDP 
and sustains nearly twelve to thirteen per cent of total employment. 
 
3. The Committee notes that India’s distinctive cultural, historical, spiritual and natural 
endowments provide a robust foundation for expanding India’s share of global 
tourism. Comparative analysis placed before the Committee, however, shows that 
countries with far fewer attractions record higher international tourist arrivals and 
tourism receipts, indicating that India’s under‑performance is not primarily on account 
of infrastructure gaps or under‑promotion. 
 
4. The Committee’s review points instead to systemic weaknesses in service delivery 
and human engagement as key constraints. Evidence received highlights a persistent 
trust deficit at several tourist destinations, arising from untrained or inadequately 
trained human interfaces, inconsistent service standards and unethical practices such 
as touting, harassment and extortion, which not only deter repeat visits but also create 
negative digital narratives that undermine national tourism branding. 
 
5. The Committee also notes a structural paradox. While tourism is projected to generate 
employment at scale, there is declining student interest in traditional hospitality and 
tourism courses, high vacancy levels in training institutions and significant attrition at 
entry level. Stakeholders attributed this trend to low starting wages, unclear career 
progression, limited professional recognition and the perception of tourism as an 
informal vocation rather than a structured profession. 
 
6. Oral evidence indicated that current formal tourism and hospitality training pipelines 
together reach only tens of thousands of learners annually, whereas the projected 
workforce requirement for a target of 100 million foreign tourists by 2047, along with 
associated domestic growth, is in the range of many millions. The Committee notes 
with concern that only about one per cent of the current tourism workforce is 
estimated to be formally skilled. 
 
7. The Committee therefore regards human capital not as an ancillary input but as the 
defining asset of the tourism economy. The Report accordingly advocates a shift from 
fragmented skilling schemes to a nationally coherent professional framework, centred 
on structured career pathways supported by formal education, mandatory institutional 
attachments, continuous professional development, and periodic certification and 
re‑certification. 
 
8. The Committee places particular emphasis on alignment of tourism education with the 
National Education Policy 2020. Flexibility through multiple entry and exit options, 
credit linkage via the Academic Bank of Credits, and a strong focus on experiential 
learning are viewed as essential to attract diverse learners and reduce drop‑outs, with 

v 
 
Central Universities envisaged as hubs for research and specialised training and State 
Universities and affiliated colleges as spokes for mass skilling and regional delivery. 
 
9. Ethical conduct and trust restoration form a core pillar of the Report. The Committee 
recommends embedding ethical training across all tourism education programmes, 
instituting enforceable licensure regimes, strengthening grievance redress mechanisms 
and linking professional certification to digital reputation and feedback systems, so 
that visitors can rely on transparent, accountable and respectful engagement at 
destinations. 
 
10. Recognising the rapid transformation of global tourism through technology, the 
Committee underlines the need to build digital and creative capabilities within the 
tourism workforce, including competencies in artificial intelligence, immersive 
technologies and digital storytelling, so that India develops the capacity to design, and 
not merely consume, tourism technology and content. The Report also flags emerging 
and under‑served segments such as border tourism, spiritual tourism, medical tourism, 
ecological circuits, cruise tourism and sites of historical trauma, each of which 
requires specialised skills, regulatory oversight and ethical sensitivity; without 
targeted human capital development, infrastructure‑led efforts in these segments risk 
significant under‑performance. The Committee is of the view that the 
outcome‑oriented and implementation‑focused recommendations contained in this 
Report, taken together, can professionalise tourism, strengthen visitor trust, generate 
dignified employment and position India as a global hub for tourism education and 
expertise in alignment with the national vision of Viksit Bharat 2047.

REPORT 
1 Context and Rationale 
1.1 Tourism as an Economic System 
1.1.1 The Committee observes that tourism is not a standalone sector but a complex economic system 
that integrates multiple industries, services, and livelihoods. The tourism economy encompasses direct 
expenditure by visitors on accommodation, food, transport, attractions, and shopping; indirect economic 
activity generated through supply chains serving tourism establishments; induced effects arising from 
expenditure by tourism workers in local economies; and catalytic impacts on infrastructure development, 
entrepreneurship, and regional investment. 
1.1.2 Unlike manufacturing or extractive industries, tourism distributes economic benefits across a 
wide geographic and social spectrum. Expenditure by tourists flows directly into local communities, 
including artisans, farmers, transport operators, guides, and small entrepreneurs. The Committee notes 
that this decentralised multiplier effect makes tourism uniquely suited for inclusive growth, particularly in 
regions with limited industrial alternatives, and aligns with recommendations for high-value spiritual and 
cultural tourism pathways (Recommendation 21) and targeted skilling for underrepresented groups 
(Recommendation 28). 
1.1.3 The Committee further notes that tourism has one of the lowest capital-to-labour ratios among 
major economic sectors. This characteristic enables large-scale employment generation with relatively 
modest capital investment, making tourism particularly relevant for absorbing India’s young workforce 
and addressing regional employment imbalances through knowledge-based roles for humanities graduates 
(Recommendation 23) and national apprenticeship programmes (Recommendation 20). 
1.2 Employment and Demographic Relevance 
1.2.1 Evidence placed before the Committee indicates that the tourism sector currently supports 
approximately twelve to thirteen per cent of total employment in the country. Employment opportunities 
span the full skill spectrum, from entry-level service roles to highly specialised positions in heritage 
interpretation, destination management, digital content creation, and tourism technology, supported by a 
dedicated Knowledge and Interpretation Stream within a National Tourism Professional Cadre 
(Recommendation 5). 
1.2.2 The Committee notes that tourism offers significant opportunities for women, youth, and rural 
populations. Many roles in tourism leverage local knowledge, cultural familiarity, and interpersonal skills 
rather than formal technical training alone. This makes tourism an important pathway for integrating 
traditionally underrepresented groups into the formal economy, as emphasized in recommendations for 
women and inclusion (Recommendation 28) and local community capacity building (Recommendation 
26). 
1.2.3 The Committee also observes that tourism employment has strong linkages with informal and 
micro-enterprise activity. While this flexibility provides livelihood opportunities, it has also resulted in 
fragmented skill development, inconsistent service quality, and limited professional recognition. 
Addressing this imbalance between accessibility and professionalism is central to the Committee’s 
examination, through Recognition of Prior Learning (Recommendation 15) and continuous upskilling 
(Recommendation 29). 

2 
 
1.3 Global Tourism Context and India’s Position 
1.3.1 The Committee notes that global tourism contributes approximately ten per cent of world GDP 
and supports one in ten jobs worldwide. International tourist arrivals have rebounded strongly following 
the pandemic, demonstrating the sector’s resilience and adaptability. 
1.3.2 Despite possessing a civilisational heritage, cultural diversity, and natural endowments 
unmatched by most destinations, India’s share of global tourism remains comparatively modest. 
Countries with far fewer historical and cultural assets attract substantially higher numbers of international 
visitors and generate greater tourism receipts. 
1.3.3 The Committee examined multiple explanations for this gap, including infrastructure, 
connectivity, marketing, and visa facilitation. While acknowledging that challenges remain in these areas, 
the Committee is of the view that the most persistent constraint lies in the quality of on-ground experience 
delivery, which is fundamentally a function of human capability, addressed through redesigning tourism 
curricula (Recommendation 7) and international collaboration (Recommendation 31). 
1.4 The Centrality of Human Interfaces 
1.4.1 The Committee observes that tourism is experienced through people. Guides, hotel staff, transport 
providers, site managers, and facilitators constitute the human interface between the visitor and the 
destination. Even the most iconic monument or pristine landscape can fail to deliver value if mediated 
through untrained, indifferent, or unethical human engagement. 
1.4.2 Evidence presented to the Committee highlighted recurring issues at tourist destinations, 
including misinformation, lack of interpretative depth, inconsistent service standards, and unethical 
practices such as touting and coercive selling. These experiences erode visitor trust and generate 
reputational damage that far exceeds the immediate transaction, mitigated by ethical guardrails 
(Recommendation 13) and linking ethical evaluation to certification (Recommendation 14). 
1.4.3 The Committee notes that digital platforms amplify both positive and negative visitor 
experiences. A single adverse interaction can influence thousands of potential visitors through online 
reviews and social media. Human capital deficiencies therefore have systemic consequences for national 
tourism performance, supported by national digital platforms for guide matching (Recommendation 17). 
1.5 Trust Deficit as a Structural Constraint 
1.5.1 The Committee identifies the trust deficit as one of the most damaging structural constraints 
facing India’s tourism sector. Trust encompasses safety, transparency, professionalism, and respect. 
Tourists gravitate towards destinations where they feel protected and valued, regardless of price or 
distance. 
1.5.2 The Committee observes that trust deficits arise not only from criminal or unethical behaviour but 
also from inadequate training, poor communication skills, and lack of accountability. In many cases, 
individuals operating at tourist sites are neither formally trained nor institutionally supported, leading to 
inconsistent behaviour and outcomes, addressed via destination-level narrative teams (Recommendation 
3). 
1.5.3 The Committee is of the view that restoring trust requires systemic intervention rather than 
isolated enforcement measures. Professionalisation through education, certification, ethical training, and 
institutional oversight is essential for sustainable trust restoration, including a national tourism 
professionals database (Recommendation 16). 

3 
 
1.6 Tourism Education: From Vocation to Profession 
1.6.1 The Committee notes that tourism in India has historically been treated as a vocational activity 
learned informally or on the job. While this approach enabled rapid workforce expansion, it has not kept 
pace with the increasing complexity of tourism as an experience-driven and technology-enabled sector. 
1.6.2 Contemporary tourism requires competencies in history, culture, communication, digital tools, 
sustainability, crisis management, and ethics. These competencies cannot be reliably developed through 
ad-hoc training alone. The Committee therefore emphasises the need to reposition tourism as a formal 
profession grounded in structured education, continuous learning, and research, through curriculum 
redesign (Recommendation 7) and long-duration field residencies (Recommendation 8). 
1.6.3 The Committee also observes that the absence of clear career pathways and professional 
recognition has contributed to declining student interest in tourism and hospitality courses. Elevating 
tourism education within the higher education ecosystem is essential to attract talent and retain skilled 
professionals, via clear career pathways (Recommendation 6) and early education integration 
(Recommendation 33). 
1.7 Rationale for Parliamentary Intervention 
1.7.1 The Committee considers that tourism’s multi-sectoral nature necessitates parliamentary 
oversight and coordinated policy direction. Human capital development in tourism intersects with 
education, culture, skill development, technology, internal security, and external engagement. 
1.7.2 Individual institutional or ministerial measures can deliver local gains, but a joined-up approach 
is required to tackle systemic challenges at scale. A coherent national framework, guided by 
parliamentary recommendations, is required to align institutional roles, standardise outcomes, and ensure 
accountability, through inter-ministerial coordination (Recommendation 10). 
1.7.3 This Report is therefore anchored in the Committee’s mandate to examine policy coherence, 
institutional effectiveness, and long-term national interest. The recommendations that follow seek to 
establish a sustainable human capital architecture for tourism that supports economic growth, social 
inclusion, cultural preservation, and global engagement, avoiding new apex bodies (Recommendation 9) 
and declaring narrative leadership as India’s advantage (Recommendation 25). 
2 The Tourism Workforce Landscape 
2.1 Overview of the Tourism Workforce 
2.1.1 The Committee observes that the tourism workforce in India is diverse, decentralised, and 
heterogeneous in nature. It spans multiple sub-sectors, including hospitality, travel and transport services, 
heritage and cultural interpretation, destination management, adventure and eco-tourism, spiritual 
tourism, medical tourism, and emerging digital tourism services. This diversity reflects the breadth of 
India’s tourism assets but also poses challenges for standardisation, quality assurance, and professional 
recognition. 
2.1.2 The tourism workforce comprises both organised and unorganised segments. While a portion of 
the workforce is employed in formal establishments such as hotels, airlines, and travel companies, a 
substantial proportion operates in informal or semi-formal arrangements. Guides, facilitators, small 
transport operators, homestay owners, and local cultural practitioners often function outside structured 
employment frameworks, with limited access to training, certification, or social security, integrated 
through Recognition of Prior Learning (Recommendation 15). 

4 
 
2.1.3 The Committee notes that this fragmented structure has enabled rapid expansion of tourism-
related livelihoods but has also resulted in uneven service quality, weak accountability, and limited career 
mobility. Addressing these structural limitations requires a comprehensive understanding of the 
workforce landscape and its underlying dynamics, via a national tourism professionals database 
(Recommendation 16). 
2.2 Skill Spectrum and Occupational Diversity 
2.2.1 Evidence placed before the Committee indicates that tourism employment spans the full skill 
spectrum. Entry-level roles include frontline service staff, site attendants, junior guides, and basic 
hospitality workers. Mid-level roles encompass supervisors, destination coordinators, senior guides, tour 
managers, and operations executives. Senior roles include destination strategists, policy advisors, 
institutional leaders, educators, and technology specialists. 
2.2.2 The Committee observes that many tourism roles require a combination of technical, cognitive, 
and interpersonal skills. Knowledge of history, culture, and geography must be complemented by 
communication ability, emotional intelligence, problem-solving capacity, and ethical judgement. 
Increasingly, digital literacy and familiarity with technology platforms are also becoming essential across 
roles, enhanced by digital training platforms (Recommendation 11). 
2.2.3 The Committee further notes that traditional training frameworks have focused predominantly on 
operational competencies, often neglecting higher-order skills such as interpretation, narrative building, 
leadership, and strategic planning. This imbalance constrains the sector’s ability to move up the value 
chain and deliver premium experiences, addressed by anchoring tourism knowledge in universities 
(Recommendation 4). 
2.3 Organised Workforce: Institutions and Outcomes 
2.3.1 The organised segment of the tourism workforce is primarily supplied by institutions under the 
Ministry of Tourism and allied Ministries. These include Institutes of Hotel Management, Indian Institute 
of Tourism and Travel Management, Indian Culinary Institute, and affiliated universities and private 
institutions. 
2.3.2 The Committee acknowledges the historical contribution of these institutions in creating a skilled 
hospitality workforce and establishing India’s reputation in global hospitality markets. Alumni from these 
institutions occupy leadership positions in domestic and international organisations, demonstrating the 
foundational strength of the system. 
2.3.3 At the same time, the Committee notes emerging challenges. Several institutions report declining 
enrolment, vacant seats, and reduced student interest in traditional programmes. Stakeholders attributed 
this trend to wage compression at entry levels, long working hours, limited work-life balance, and unclear 
career progression pathways, mitigated by CSR-linked internships (Recommendation 19). 
2.3.4 The Committee is of the view that without structural reforms in curriculum design, professional 
recognition, and career architecture, the organised training system risks losing relevance among younger 
cohorts, through industry-academia linkages (Recommendation 30). The Committee acknowledges the 
inputs received from industry associations including FHRAI, IATO, Hotel Association of India (HAI), 
and ATOAI on workforce challenges, skill gaps, attrition patterns, and industry expectations. The 
evidence on entry-level wage structures, career progression bottlenecks, and changing operational 
requirements informed the Committee's recommendations on professional cadre structures, career 
pathways, and mid-career training frameworks.The Committee is concerned that existing institutional 
capacity, estimated at around 25,000 learners at a given time across MoT‑linked institutions, is not 
commensurate with projected employment needs and therefore cannot on its own shift the sector from 

5 
 
informality to professionalism. 
2.4 Unorganised Workforce and Informality 
2.4.1 A significant proportion of tourism-related human interfaces operate in the unorganised sector. 
These include informal guides, seasonal facilitators, local transport providers, community elders, artisans, 
and gig-based tourism workers. 
2.4.2 The Committee recognises that many individuals in the unorganised segment possess deep local 
knowledge, linguistic ability, and cultural familiarity. Their exclusion from formal frameworks is not due 
to lack of capability but due to structural barriers such as educational prerequisites, geographic distance 
from training centres, cost of certification, and limited access to information, formalised via national 
online platforms for micro-guides (Recommendation 18). 
2.4.3 The persistence of informality has multiple consequences. It creates income instability for 
workers, limits accountability, and exposes tourists to inconsistent or unethical practices. The Committee 
emphasises that informality should be addressed through inclusion and professionalisation rather than 
punitive measures, including incentives for training investment (Recommendation 34). 
2.5 Wage Structures and Career Progression 
2.5.1 The Committee notes that wage structures in the tourism sector remain a critical area of concern. 
Entry-level wages are often low relative to working hours and job demands. In the absence of transparent 
career ladders, many skilled workers exit the sector within a few years, leading to high attrition and loss 
of institutional memory. 
2.5.2 Evidence indicates that the absence of formal mid-career training and leadership development 
contributes significantly to stagnation. Workers often remain confined to operational roles without 
opportunities to transition into supervisory or strategic positions. 
2.5.3 The Committee is of the view that tourism must be restructured as a career, not merely a job. This 
requires defined progression pathways, recognition of experience through certification, and access to 
periodic skill upgradation linked to promotion and remuneration, as in clear career pathways 
(Recommendation 6). 
2.6 Proposal for a Structured Professional Cadre 
2.6.1 In view of the above, the Committee proposes structuring the tourism workforce into a formal 
National Tourism Professional Cadre, comprising multiple functional streams including a specialised 
Knowledge and Interpretation Stream. This framework is intended to provide clarity of roles, standardise 
competencies, and create transparent progression pathways across the sector, per Recommendation 5. 
2.6.2 The proposed cadre structure includes three broad levels: 
● Entry Level: Professionals equipped with foundational education in tourism, hospitality, or 
heritage, combined with mandatory institutional attachments and site-based training. 
● Mid-Career Level: Experienced professionals responsible for team leadership, destination 
coordination, and complex operations, supported by structured management training and 
certification. 
● Senior Leadership Level: Strategists, policy contributors, institutional leaders, and mentors 
responsible for destination planning, policy formulation, and international engagement. 
2.6.3 Progression between levels is envisaged to be competency-based rather than time-bound. Periodic 
assessment, refresher training, and evaluation through practical assignments are considered essential to 

6 
 
maintain professional standards, aligned with continuous upskilling (Recommendation 29). 
2.7 Mid-Career Training and Continuous Learning 
2.7.1 The Committee observes that continuous learning is critical in a sector characterised by changing 
visitor expectations, technological evolution, and regulatory frameworks. Mid-career training must 
therefore be institutionalised rather than left to individual initiative, via national apprenticeship 
programmes (Recommendation 20). 
2.7.2 The Committee supports the development of structured mid-career management programmes in 
partnership with reputed institutions. Such programmes should focus on leadership, crisis management, 
sustainability, digital transformation, and ethical decision-making. 
2.7.3 Mandatory refresher courses at defined intervals are considered necessary to ensure that certified 
professionals remain updated with new developments, including archaeological discoveries, regulatory 
changes, and emerging technologies. 
2.8 Addressing Geographic and Demand Mismatch 
2.8.1 The Committee notes a mismatch between the geographic distribution of trained professionals 
and areas of tourism demand. High-potential destinations often face shortages of trained personnel, while 
some regions with limited tourism activity produce surplus graduates. 
2.8.2 The Committee emphasises the need for national-level placement coordination, mobility 
incentives, and region-specific training programmes to address this imbalance. Decentralised training 
delivery combined with centralised standards is considered the optimal approach, including border-area 
tourism skills (Recommendation 27). 
2.9 Observations 
2.9.1 The Committee observes that the tourism workforce landscape reflects both opportunity and 
vulnerability. While tourism has the capacity to generate large-scale employment, its human capital 
framework remains fragmented and under-institutionalised. 
2.9.2 Professionalisation of the workforce through structured education, certification, career pathways, 
and continuous learning is essential to enhance service quality, restore trust, and improve economic 
outcomes. 
2.9.3 The Committee is of the view that reforms in the workforce landscape form the foundation upon 
which institutional, technological, and ethical interventions discussed in subsequent chapters must be 
built. 
3 Unorganised Guides and Informal Human Interfaces 
3.1 Significance and Nature of Informal Human Interfaces 
3.1.1 The Committee observes that informal human interfaces constitute a substantial and visible 
component of India’s tourism ecosystem. At many destinations, particularly heritage sites, religious 
centres, rural circuits, and emerging tourist locations, the first point of contact for visitors is often an 
individual operating outside formal institutional frameworks. 
3.1.2 These informal actors include site‑based guides, seasonal facilitators, community elders, local 
language interpreters, transport intermediaries, and gig‑based tourism workers. Their modes of 

7 
 
engagement vary, but collectively they shape visitor experience, perception, and trust. 
3.1.3 The Committee is of the considered view that such interfaces should not be approached solely as 
a regulatory challenge. They represent a reservoir of local knowledge, cultural familiarity, and lived 
experience that formal systems have not fully harnessed. Exclusion of this workforce from structured 
frameworks has resulted in both economic inefficiency and reputational risk, which the Committee seeks 
to address through systematic integration of informal knowledge holders (Recommendation 15). 
3.2 Typology of Unorganised Guides and Facilitators 
3.2.1 Based on evidence received, the Committee identifies multiple categories within the unorganised 
guiding and facilitation ecosystem: 
3.2.2 Site‑based informal guides operate at specific monuments, temples, museums, and natural 
attractions. Many possess inherited or self‑acquired knowledge but lack formal certification. 
3.2.3 Seasonal and event‑based facilitators emerge during festivals, pilgrimage seasons, and peak 
tourist periods. Their engagement is demand‑driven and often temporary. 
3.2.4 Community custodians and cultural practitioners, including village elders, tribal leaders, artisans, 
and performers, provide authentic insights into local traditions, folklore, and intangible heritage. 
3.2.5 Language facilitators assist visitors in multilingual regions, particularly where formal guides with 
requisite language skills are unavailable. 
3.2.6 Gig‑based tourism workers, often using digital platforms or informal networks, provide sporadic 
services such as walking tours, food trails, and experiential activities. 
3.2.7 The Committee notes that this diversity reflects the organic evolution of tourism livelihoods. Any 
formalisation strategy must therefore be flexible and context‑sensitive, including through national online 
platforms for micro‑guides (Recommendation 18). 
3.3 Structural Causes of Persisting Informality 
3.3.1 The Committee examined the reasons underlying the persistence of informality in tourism 
guiding and facilitation. 
3.3.2 High barriers to entry, including rigid educational prerequisites and centralised training 
requirements, exclude individuals with substantial practical knowledge but limited formal schooling. 
3.3.3 Language and literacy constraints further limit participation, as many certification programmes 
are delivered primarily in English or Hindi without adequate regional language support. 
3.3.4 Geographic concentration of training institutions creates access challenges for individuals in 
remote, border, or rural areas. Travel and accommodation costs act as deterrents. 
3.3.5 In some locations, informal monopolies and exclusionary practices restrict entry into formal 
guiding systems, perpetuating parallel informal arrangements, which can be reduced through national 
digital platforms for tourist–guide matching (Recommendation 17). 
3.3.6 The Committee also notes that the absence of clear pathways from informal engagement to 
formal recognition discourages voluntary participation in certification processes. 
 

8 
 
3.4 Consequences of Informality 
3.4.1 The Committee observes that persistent informality has multiple adverse consequences. Members 
underlined that existing online‑heavy guide training formats do not reflect the reality that guiding is 
predominantly a practical vocation requiring on‑site mentoring, multi‑domain competence (for example, 
in history, basic medical awareness, and hospitality), and destination‑specific exposure. 
3.4.2 For workers, informality results in income instability, absence of social security, lack of 
professional dignity, and vulnerability to arbitrary enforcement actions. 
3.4.3 For tourists, it contributes to inconsistent service quality, misinformation, and exposure to 
unethical practices such as touting, coercive selling, and overcharging. 
3.4.4 At the systemic level, informality undermines trust, weakens destination branding, and limits the 
effectiveness of skill development investments and infrastructure creation. 
3.4.5 The Committee is of the view that addressing informality is essential not only for improving 
visitor experience but also for achieving inclusive and sustainable tourism growth, including through 
measures such as mandatory local micro‑guide certification for homestay licensing (Recommendation 
26). 
3.5 Guiding Principles for Integration into the Formal System 
3.5.1 The Committee emphasises that integration of unorganised guides and facilitators must be guided 
by principles of inclusion, dignity, and practicality. 
3.5.2 Formalisation should be facilitative rather than punitive. The objective must be to enable 
transition into recognised frameworks without disrupting livelihoods. 
3.5.3 Recognition of existing skills and experience is essential. Formal systems must adapt to local 
realities rather than imposing uniform, one‑size‑fits‑all models. 
3.5.4 Decentralised delivery, regional language support, and modular certification are considered 
critical to successful integration. 
3.6 Mechanisms for Transition, Recognition and Certification 
3.6.1 The Committee recommends the adoption of bridge certification models that allow partial credit 
for demonstrated skills and knowledge. Short, modular courses can enable incremental progress towards 
full certification without requiring long periods away from work. 
3.6.2 Recognition of Prior Learning (RPL) mechanisms should be expanded and strengthened. Practical 
assessments, oral evaluations, and site‑based demonstrations should be used to validate informal 
expertise, rather than relying solely on written examinations. 
3.6.3 Site‑specific certification is considered particularly relevant for heritage monuments, 
eco‑sensitive zones, and religious sites, where local knowledge is paramount and generic training is 
insufficient. 
3.6.4 Local facilitation camps at district or block levels should be organised in collaboration with local 
authorities to bring certification processes closer to communities. 
3.6.5 Mobile training and assessment units can be deployed in remote and border regions to overcome 
geographic barriers and seasonal constraints. 

9 
 
3.6.6 Amnesty‑based onboarding windows may be considered to encourage voluntary registration and 
certification of existing informal workers without fear of penal action, aligned with women and inclusion 
initiatives (Recommendation 28). 
3.7 Digital Identity, Registries and Accountability 
3.7.1 The Committee notes the potential of digital systems in formalising informal human interfaces. 
3.7.2 A verifiable digital identity linked to certification can enhance accountability, reduce 
impersonation, and enable tourists and employers to distinguish trained professionals from unverified 
operators. 
3.7.3 Digital registries can also facilitate grievance redress, feedback collection, and periodic renewal 
of credentials, thereby linking professional behaviour to continued licensure. 
3.7.4 The Committee cautions that digital systems must be designed with safeguards for data privacy 
and accessibility, particularly for individuals with limited digital literacy, consistent with treating digital 
platforms as interpretation infrastructure (Recommendation 11). 
3.8 Expected Behavioural and Economic Impact of Integration 
3.8.1 The Committee anticipates that systematic integration of informal guides into formal frameworks 
will yield positive behavioural and economic outcomes. 
3.8.2 Professional recognition and more predictable earning opportunities are expected to reduce 
aggressive practices and improve service conduct. 
3.8.3 Income stabilisation and access to training are likely to enhance self‑esteem and community 
status of tourism workers. 
3.8.4 For tourists, improved consistency, transparency, and accountability are expected to restore trust 
and increase satisfaction. 
3.8.5 At the destination level, integration is expected to enhance reputation, increase visitor spending, 
and strengthen local economic linkages, supported by incentives for training investment 
(Recommendation 34). 
3.9 Role of State Governments and Local Institutions 
3.9.1 The Committee emphasises the critical role of State Governments, district administrations, and 
local bodies in identifying, mobilising, and supporting informal tourism workers. 
3.9.2 Panchayats, municipalities, and local cultural institutions can act as intermediaries in outreach, 
verification, mentoring, and monitoring, ensuring that formalisation efforts are locally grounded. 
3.9.3 Coordination between the Ministry of Tourism and State authorities is essential to ensure 
consistency of standards while respecting local contexts and avoiding duplication. 
3.10 Overall Observations 
3.10.1 The Committee observes that informal human interfaces are an integral part of India’s tourism 
reality and cannot be eliminated through enforcement alone. 
3.10.2 Inclusive formalisation, grounded in recognition, training, and accountability, offers a sustainable 

10 
 
pathway to improving service quality and livelihoods simultaneously. 
3.10.3 The Committee is of the view that successful integration of unorganised guides and facilitators 
will significantly contribute to trust restoration, professionalisation of the tourism workforce, and 
inclusive tourism development. 
4 Institutional Framework and Decentralised Models 
4.1 Overview of the Institutional Landscape 
4.1.1 The Committee observes that tourism human resource development in India is supported by a 
diverse institutional ecosystem comprising Central Autonomous Bodies, universities, State institutions, 
and private providers. This multi-layered structure reflects the breadth of tourism activity but also 
presents challenges in coordination, standardisation, and outcome alignment. 
4.1.2 The Ministry of Tourism currently oversees a network of Central Autonomous Bodies tasked 
with delivering education, training, and capacity-building across the tourism and hospitality spectrum. 
These include Institutes of Hotel Management, the Indian Institute of Tourism and Travel Management, 
and the Indian Culinary Institute. 
4.1.3 The Committee notes that while this institutional infrastructure provides nationwide reach, its 
effectiveness depends on coherence of mandate, curricular relevance, faculty capacity, and alignment 
with evolving sectoral needs, enhanced by avoiding new apex bodies through institutional dovetailing 
(Recommendation 9). 
4.2 Institutes of Hotel Management and NCHMCT 
4.2.1 The Committee notes that the Institutes of Hotel Management, operating under the academic 
umbrella of the National Council for Hotel Management and Catering Technology, have historically been 
the backbone of India’s hospitality education system. 
4.2.2 With a network of Central, State, and private institutes, the system ensures geographic spread and 
accessibility. Degrees awarded through a Central university framework have enhanced academic 
recognition and global portability. 
4.2.3 The Committee acknowledges that graduates from these institutions occupy leadership positions 
in the hospitality industry both within India and internationally, demonstrating the foundational strength 
of the system. 
4.2.4 At the same time, the Committee observes emerging challenges. Traditional course structures 
have not fully adapted to changes in industry operations, including increased outsourcing, automation, 
and digitalisation. Certain modules require reorientation to reflect managerial, technological, and 
experiential dimensions of contemporary hospitality, per curriculum redesign (Recommendation 7). 
4.2.5 The Committee supports the restructuring of curricula in alignment with the National Education 
Policy 2020, including the introduction of four-year degree programmes with research and 
entrepreneurship components, and multiple entry and exit options.The Committee acknowledges the 
detailed presentation by the National Council for Hotel Management and Catering Technology outlining 
its institutional evolution, network expansion, NEP 2020 alignment efforts, and challenges in maintaining 
student enrollment. The insights on curriculum restructuring, industry partnerships, and faculty 
development provided valuable inputs for the Committee's recommendations on institutional reform and 
career pathway development. The Committee takes note of recent initiatives where all 21 Central IHMs 
have entered MoUs with leading hotel groups for curriculum co‑development, structured internships, 

11 
 
faculty exposure and placement support, illustrating the benefits of institutionalised industry–academia 
partnerships. 
4.3 Indian Institute of Tourism and Travel Management 
4.3.1 The Committee observes that the Indian Institute of Tourism and Travel Management plays a 
distinct and critical role within the institutional ecosystem. Unlike hospitality-focused institutions, IITTM 
addresses the broader tourism value chain, including travel management, destination planning, logistics, 
and niche tourism segments. 
4.3.2 IITTM functions as an intellectual and policy support institution for the Ministry of Tourism, 
contributing to research, advisory services, and specialised training programmes. 
4.3.3 The Committee takes note of IITTM’s role as Central Nodal Agency for delivering standardised 
training in areas such as rural tourism, eco-tourism, adventure tourism, water sports, and tourist guide 
training. 
4.3.4 The Committee considers the Central Nodal Agency model to be an effective mechanism for 
ensuring national consistency while allowing region-specific adaptation. This model is particularly 
relevant for emerging tourism segments and under-served regions, supported by inter-ministerial 
coordination (Recommendation 10). The Committee acknowledges the presentation by the Indian 
Institute of Tourism and Travel Management on its role as Central Nodal Agency for specialized tourism 
segments, research contributions, and policy advisory functions. The evidence on decentralized training 
delivery models, regional tourism development, and niche segment capacity building informed the 
Committee's recommendations on segment-specific skill development and coordination mechanisms. 
4.4 Indian Culinary Institute 
4.4.1 The Committee observes that the Indian Culinary Institute occupies a specialised niche in 
preserving, documenting, and advancing Indian culinary traditions. 
4.4.2 Beyond conventional culinary training, the Institute has a mandate to research Indian cuisines, 
standardise recipes, and promote culinary diplomacy. 
4.4.3 The Committee is of the view that culinary education should extend beyond kitchen skills to 
include food anthropology, sensory analysis, nutrition, and content creation. Such an approach can elevate 
Indian cuisine as a global cultural asset and create diversified career pathways. 
4.4.4 The Committee also notes the Institute’s role in training ex-servicemen for hospitality roles and 
considers this an effective model for skill redeployment and dignified employment, aligned with mid-
career transitions (Recommendation 20). The Committee acknowledges the presentation by the Indian 
Culinary Institute highlighting its specialized role in culinary education, documentation of Indian 
cuisines, and training of ex-servicemen. The Institute's approach to integrating food anthropology, 
sensory analysis, and culinary diplomacy provided insights that shaped the Committee's recommendations 
on expanding culinary education beyond operational skills and leveraging culinary heritage as a cultural 
asset. 
4.5 Role of Universities in Tourism Education 
4.5.1 The Committee places particular emphasis on the role of universities in advancing tourism 
education beyond vocational training. 
4.5.2 Universities are uniquely positioned to integrate tourism with disciplines such as history, 
archaeology, anthropology, ecology, public health, technology, and management. This multidisciplinary 

12 
 
capability is essential for developing higher-order skills in interpretation, planning, and research, per 
anchoring tourism knowledge in universities (Recommendation 4). 
4.5.3 Evidence placed before the Committee demonstrates that Central and State universities have 
successfully implemented innovative models linking tourism education with community engagement, 
heritage research, and regional development.The Committee acknowledges the presentations by Jamia 
Millia Islamia, State Institute of Hotel Management Faridabad, and Banarsidas Chandiwala Institute of 
Hotel Management, which provided practical insights into curriculum delivery, industry engagement, 
student outcomes, and implementation challenges at the ground level. The evidence on admission trends, 
placement records, faculty constraints, and regional skill requirements informed the Committee's 
recommendations on decentralized delivery models, university partnerships, and institutional capacity 
building. The Committee also notes submissions from private institutes that currently fall across multiple 
regulatory umbrellas (AICTE, UGC, NCHMCT) and report limited structured engagement with the 
Ministry of Tourism, despite being substantial providers of tourism and hospitality education. 
4.6 Hub-and-Spoke Model for Decentralised Delivery 
4.6.1 The Committee is of the view for adoption of a formal hub-and-spoke model for tourism 
education. 
4.6.2 Under this model, selected Central Universities function as hubs responsible for advanced 
research, curriculum design, faculty development, and specialised training in areas such as heritage 
interpretation and Indian Knowledge Systems. 
4.6.3 State Universities and their affiliated colleges operate as spokes, delivering standardised diploma 
and degree programmes to a larger student base while contextualising content to regional tourism assets. 
4.6.4 The Committee is of the view that this decentralised model ensures scale, inclusivity, and 
responsiveness while maintaining national quality benchmarks, enhanced by industry-academia linkages 
(Recommendation 30). The Committee further acknowledges the contributions of Madurai Kamaraj 
University and Central University of Tamil Nadu during the study visit to Chennai on 21 January 2026. 
The interactions provided valuable insights into university-led tourism education, integration of heritage 
research with community development, and regional models of tourism skill delivery. These inputs 
strengthened the Committee's conviction regarding the hub-and-spoke model and university-anchored 
knowledge creation. 
4.7 Integration with National Education Policy 2020 
4.7.1 The Committee emphasises that tourism education must align fully with the National Education 
Policy 2020. 
4.7.2 Multiple entry and exit options, credit portability through the Academic Bank of Credits, and 
emphasis on experiential learning are considered essential to attract diverse learners and reduce attrition. 
4.7.3 The Committee supports integration of tourism programmes with community projects, heritage 
walks, live assignments, and student-led tourism initiatives, including long-duration field residencies 
(Recommendation 8). 
4.8 Faculty Development and Institutional Capacity 
4.8.1 The Committee observes that faculty capacity is a critical determinant of educational quality. 
4.8.2 Continuous faculty upskilling, exposure to industry practices, and engagement with research are 
necessary to keep curricula relevant. 

13 
 
4.8.3 The Committee supports dedicated funding for faculty development, international exposure, and 
research initiatives in tourism and hospitality education, via international collaboration (Recommendation 
31). 
4.9 Coordination and Governance 
4.9.1 The Committee notes that institutional effectiveness depends on clear delineation of roles and 
coordination mechanisms. 
4.9.2 The Ministry of Tourism should function as a standard-setter, certifier, and regulator rather than a 
centralised provider of education. 
4.9.3 Universities and autonomous institutions must retain academic autonomy while adhering to 
national standards and accountability frameworks. 
4.10 Observations 
4.10.1 The Committee observes that India possesses a substantial institutional base for tourism 
education, but its potential remains under-utilised due to fragmentation and uneven capacity. 
4.10.2 A decentralised, university-led model anchored in national standards offers a sustainable pathway 
to professionalising tourism human capital. The Committee therefore expects that the proposed 
hub‑and‑spoke and accreditation frameworks will explicitly accommodate and support both public and 
private providers. 
4.10.3 The Committee is of the view that strengthening institutional frameworks is foundational to the 
success of certification, technology integration, ethical standards, and global positioning discussed in 
subsequent chapters. 
5 Certification, Accreditation and Standardisation 
5.1 Need for a National Standards Architecture 
5.1.1 The Committee observes that a professional tourism workforce cannot be sustained without a 
robust framework for certification, accreditation, and standardisation. In the absence of nationally 
recognised standards, training outcomes vary widely across institutions and regions, leading to 
inconsistent service quality and erosion of visitor trust. The Committee notes from evidence that multiple, 
uncoordinated certification regimes currently exist across Sector Skill Councils, State governments and 
institutional programmes, with no single ‘gold standard’ for tourism professionals, and that a large 
majority of tourism establishments operate outside any accreditation framework. 
5.1.2 Tourism, by its very nature, operates across jurisdictions and sectors. Tourists experience 
destinations as a single continuum rather than as discrete institutional offerings. The Committee therefore 
emphasises that standards must be nationally coherent while allowing contextual adaptation at the local 
level. 
5.1.3 The Committee is of the view that certification and accreditation serve three critical functions: 
assurance of minimum competency, professional recognition for workers, and accountability to visitors 
and regulators, supported by accreditation of tourism programs (Recommendation 32). 
5.2 Role of the National Institute of Hospitality 
5.2.1 The Committee notes that the proposed National Institute of Hospitality is uniquely positioned to 

14 
 
serve as the apex authority for tourism education standards, certification, and accreditation. 
5.2.2 The Committee is of the view that the mandate of the National Institute of Hospitality extend 
beyond academic delivery to include curriculum harmonisation, faculty benchmarking, accreditation of 
institutions, and testing and certification of professionals. 
5.2.3 As an apex standards body, the National Institute of Hospitality should function as a custodian of 
national and international benchmarks, ensuring that qualifications issued across the tourism education 
ecosystem are comparable, portable, and credible, aligned with avoiding new apex bodies 
(Recommendation 9). 
5.3 Professional Certification and Licensure 
5.3.1 The Committee observes that several tourism roles, particularly guides and facilitators operating 
at heritage and eco-sensitive sites, exercise significant influence over visitor experience and safety. The 
absence of enforceable licensure for such roles has contributed to inconsistent quality and unethical 
practices. 
5.3.2 The Committee recommends the establishment of a National Tourism Licensure Framework 
covering key professional categories, including guides, adventure tourism operators, and specialised 
facilitators. 
5.3.3 Licensure should be competency-based and linked to successful completion of approved training 
and assessment. Renewal of licences should be contingent upon periodic refresher training and adherence 
to ethical standards, per linking ethical evaluation to certification (Recommendation 14). 
5.3.4 The Committee emphasises that licensure is not intended to restrict livelihoods but to 
professionalise engagement and protect both workers and visitors. 
5.4 Recognition of Prior Learning and Modular Certification 
5.4.1 The Committee reiterates the importance of Recognition of Prior Learning as a central pillar of 
certification policy, particularly for integrating informal and community-based tourism workers. 
5.4.2 Modular, credit-based certification allows individuals to progress incrementally towards full 
qualification without disengaging from livelihoods. This approach aligns with the flexibility envisaged 
under the National Education Policy 2020. 
5.4.3 The Committee proposes that assessments under Recognition of Prior Learning frameworks 
emphasise practical demonstration, site-based evaluation, and oral examination rather than written tests 
alone, as in Recommendation 15. 
5.5 Institutional Accreditation 
5.5.1 The Committee observes that institutional accreditation is essential to ensure consistency and 
credibility of training outcomes. 
5.5.2 Accreditation criteria should cover curriculum relevance, faculty qualifications, infrastructure 
adequacy, industry linkage, student outcomes, and ethical standards. 
5.5.3 The Committee suggests that all institutions offering tourism and hospitality programmes under 
the purview of the Ministry of Tourism be subject to periodic accreditation by the National Institute of 
Hospitality or an authorised body. 

15 
 
5.5.4 Accreditation outcomes should be transparent and linked to eligibility for public funding, student 
intake approvals, and participation in national programmes, per accreditation of tourism programs 
(Recommendation 32). 
5.6 International Benchmarking and Alignment 
5.6.1 The Committee notes that global comparability of qualifications enhances mobility of 
professionals and international recognition of Indian tourism education. 
5.6.2 The Committee recommends systematic benchmarking of Indian tourism programmes against 
leading international institutions, with periodic curriculum review. 
5.6.3 International alignment should focus on competency equivalence rather than wholesale adoption 
of foreign models, ensuring that Indian cultural and contextual strengths are preserved, via international 
collaboration (Recommendation 31). 
5.7 Ethical Standards and Disciplinary Mechanisms 
5.7.1 The Committee observes that certification without enforceable ethical standards is insufficient to 
restore trust in the tourism ecosystem. 
5.7.2 Ethical conduct should be embedded as a mandatory component of certification and licensure, 
with clear codes of conduct applicable across professional categories. 
5.7.3 The Committee recommends establishment of streamlined disciplinary mechanisms, including 
suspension or revocation of licences in cases of proven misconduct, harassment, or extortion. 
5.7.4 Due process, transparency, and opportunity for corrective training should be integral to 
disciplinary frameworks, aligned with embedding ethical guardrails (Recommendation 13). 
5.8 Digital Registries and Transparency 
5.8.1 The Committee notes the potential of digital systems to enhance transparency and accountability 
in certification and accreditation. 
5.8.2 A national digital registry of certified tourism professionals and accredited institutions can enable 
verification by tourists, employers, and regulators. 
5.8.3 Such registries can also facilitate data-driven policy decisions, monitoring of workforce 
distribution, and identification of skill gaps, per developing a national tourism professionals database 
(Recommendation 16). 
5.9 Role of State Governments 
5.9.1 The Committee emphasises that effective implementation of certification and licensure 
frameworks requires active participation of State Governments. 
5.9.2 State authorities should be involved in outreach, facilitation, enforcement, and grievance redress, 
within nationally defined standards. 
5.9.3 Coordination between the Ministry of Tourism and State Governments is essential to avoid 
duplication and ensure uniformity. 
 

16 
 
5.10 Observations 
5.10.1 The Committee observes that certification, accreditation, and standardisation are foundational to 
professionalising tourism and restoring visitor trust. 
5.10.2 A nationally coherent framework, implemented through decentralised institutions and supported 
by digital systems, offers a sustainable pathway to quality assurance. 
5.10.3 The Committee is of the view that without enforceable standards and accountability mechanisms, 
investments in training and infrastructure will not yield optimal outcomes. 
6 Digital Systems, Technology and Registries 
6.1 Digital Transformation of Global Tourism 
6.1.1 The Committee observes that tourism globally is undergoing rapid digital transformation. Digital 
platforms now influence how destinations are discovered, journeys are planned, experiences are 
navigated, and memories are shared. Artificial intelligence, immersive technologies, and data-driven 
systems are increasingly central to tourism competitiveness. 
6.1.2 The Committee notes that digital transformation in tourism extends beyond marketing and 
booking systems. It encompasses content creation, experience design, safety management, reputation 
systems, language translation, and personalised engagement across the visitor lifecycle. 
6.1.3 The Committee is of the view that India’s tourism sector must proactively adapt to these changes 
in order to remain competitive in global markets, through treating digital platforms as interpretation 
infrastructure (Recommendation 11). 
6.2 National Destination Digital Knowledge Grid 
6.2.1 The Committee takes note of the announcement regarding the creation of a National Destination 
Digital Knowledge Grid. This initiative provides a foundational infrastructure for organising, 
authenticating, and disseminating tourism-related information. 
6.2.2 The Committee observes that the effectiveness of this Grid will depend not merely on its 
technical architecture but on the quality, accuracy, and richness of the content populated within it. 
6.2.3 The Committee proposes that the Grid be designed as a dynamic knowledge ecosystem rather 
than a static repository. Content should include verified historical narratives, cultural interpretations, 
ecological information, visitor advisories, and accessibility details. 
6.2.4 The Committee further proposes that the Grid be leveraged as a training and learning sandbox for 
tourism students and professionals, enabling experiential learning through content creation and validation, 
aligned with creating India-origin digital heritage content (Recommendation 12). 
6.3 Artificial Intelligence in Tourism 
6.3.1 The Committee observes that artificial intelligence has significant potential to enhance tourism 
experiences through personalisation, efficiency, and accessibility. 
6.3.2 AI-enabled systems can analyse visitor preferences and behaviour to provide customised 
recommendations for destinations, itineraries, and services. Real-time language translation and virtual 
assistance can improve inclusivity for international visitors.

6.3.3 The Committee emphasises that AI should be used to augment human interaction rather than 
replace it. Tourism remains fundamentally a human experience, and technology must enhance, not erode, 
personal engagement. 
6.3.4 The Committee proposes that AI literacy be incorporated into tourism education and professional 
training at appropriate levels, ensuring that practitioners understand both capabilities and limitations of AI

6.4 Immersive Technologies and Heritage Interpretation

7 Ethics, Professional Conduct and Trust Restoration 
7.1 Trust as the Foundation of Tourism 
7.1.1 The Committee observes that trust is the single most critical determinant of tourism performance. 
Tourists choose destinations where they feel safe, respected, and treated fairly. Regardless of the quality 
of infrastructure or attractions, a deficit of trust undermines visitor confidence and suppresses repeat 
visitation. 

19 
 
7.1.2 The Committee notes that trust in tourism is built through consistent, ethical, and professional 
human engagement. Every interaction, whether with a guide, hotel staff, transport provider, or site 
attendant, contributes cumulatively to visitor perception. 
7.1.3 The Committee is of the view that trust restoration must be addressed as a systemic issue rather 
than as a series of isolated enforcement actions, shifting strategy to narrative impact (Recommendation 2). 
7.2 Nature and Extent of Ethical Concerns 
7.2.1 Evidence placed before the Committee highlighted recurring concerns at tourist destinations, 
including touting, misrepresentation of information, coercive selling, overcharging, harassment, and lack 
of transparency in pricing. 
7.2.2 The Committee observes that while the scale and intensity of such practices vary across 
destinations, their reputational impact is magnified in the digital age through online reviews and social 
media platforms. 
7.2.3 The Committee further notes that unethical conduct often arises from inadequate training, 
absence of professional identity, income insecurity, and lack of institutional oversight rather than 
deliberate malfeasance alone, addressed by embedding ethical guardrails (Recommendation 13). 
7.3 Ethics as a Core Professional Competency 
7.3.1 The Committee emphasises that ethical conduct must be treated as a core professional 
competency rather than an ancillary behavioural expectation. 
7.3.2 Ethics education should therefore be embedded throughout tourism training and education 
programmes, from entry-level certification to advanced professional development. 
7.3.3 Ethical training should address practical scenarios commonly encountered in tourism settings, 
including conflict of interest, consent, transparency in transactions, safety responsibilities, and respectful 
engagement with diverse visitors, per curriculum redesign (Recommendation 7). 
7.4 Codes of Conduct and Professional Oaths 
7.4.1 The Committee proposes the adoption of a codified code of conduct applicable to all certified 
tourism professionals. 
7.4.2 Such codes should clearly articulate acceptable and prohibited behaviours, delineate 
responsibilities towards visitors and host communities, and specify consequences for violations. 
7.4.3 The Committee further proposes that graduates and certified professionals formally affirm their 
commitment to ethical conduct through a standardised professional pledge at the time of certification. 
7.5 Institutional Accountability and Enforcement 
7.5.1 The Committee observes that ethical standards must be supported by enforceable accountability 
mechanisms to be effective. 
7.5.2 Certification and licensure frameworks should incorporate provisions for investigation of 
complaints, imposition of graduated penalties, and opportunities for corrective training. 
7.5.3 The Committee emphasises the importance of due process, transparency, and proportionality in 
disciplinary actions.

10 Consolidated Outcomes and Recommendations 
10.1 India's tourism challenge is not infrastructure—it is interpretation. We have built world-class 
corridors but staffed them with untrained, uncertified, sometimes unethical human interfaces. The result: 
visitors see monuments but leave without understanding India. This Report repositions tourism as a 
knowledge profession, not a service vocation. We seek to transform every guide into an interpreter, every 
homestay owner into a micro-storyteller, and every heritage site into a living classroom. 
Recommendation 1: Recasting Major Religious Destinations as Civilisational Knowledge Hubs 
10.2 The Committee recommends that select major religious destinations developed under flagship 
initiatives such as PRASHAD, Kashi Vishwanath Corridor, Ayodhya, Ujjain and Char Dham be 
consciously repositioned as Civilisational Knowledge Hubs, without altering their spiritual sanctity or 
ritual primacy. The Committee observes that substantial public investment has transformed physical 
infrastructure and crowd management, yet visitor engagement remains largely limited to ritual 
participation and movement management. These destinations embody layered traditions of philosophy, 
art, architecture, astronomy, music, ecology and social history that remain largely unexplained to visitors. 
10.3 The Committee is of the view that structured, knowledge-led interpretation can coexist with faith 
and, in fact, deepen reverence by providing context and continuity. Repositioning these sites as 
knowledge hubs allows visitors to understand not only religious practice, but the civilisational ideas, 
historical processes and social values that produced these sacred landscapes. Such interpretation enhances 
respect for traditions, improves visitor experience, and extends engagement beyond the act of visitation. 
The Committee considers this shift essential to convert infrastructure investment into durable cultural, 
educational and soft-power outcomes for the nation. To enhance pilgrimage and spiritual tourism, 

26 
 
PRASHAD and Swadesh Darshan projects should explicitly include skill-development for pilgrims’ 
needs, with guides at temples and dharamshalas trained in local languages, religious history, basic first-
aid, crowd management, and elderly care. Short courses through ITIs or vocational centers should certify 
local youth as pilgrimage guides or volunteers, aligning with PRASHAD’s mandate for livelihoods and 
skill development at sacred sites. 
Recommendation 2: End the "Numbers Game" — Measure Tourism Success by Visitor 
Understanding, Not Arrivals 
10.4 The Committee recommends a recalibration of national tourism strategy from an exclusive 
emphasis on footfall, arrivals and revenue to a broader assessment of narrative impact and visitor 
understanding. The Committee notes that infrastructure and connectivity attract visitors, but narratives 
shape memory, trust and repeat engagement. Evidence examined during the study indicates that 
destinations with coherent interpretation and storytelling command higher satisfaction and longer 
engagement regardless of visitor volume. 
10.5 The Committee further observes that India’s G20 presidency demonstrated the effectiveness of 
disciplined, authentic storytelling rooted in civilisational confidence. Tourism, as the most continuous 
interface between India and the world, must institutionalise this approach. The Committee therefore 
recommends that destination performance also be evaluated through indicators such as quality of 
interpretation, coherence of narratives, visitor comprehension and feedback. This shift aligns tourism 
policy with India’s soft-power objectives and ensures that growth is accompanied by intellectual and 
cultural leadership. The Committee believes that narrative impact is the true multiplier of tourism value, 
converting visits into understanding, respect and long-term global engagement. 
Recommendation 3: Establishing Destination-Level Narrative Teams Using Existing Institutions 
10.6 The Committee recommends that major religious and heritage destinations be supported by 
destination-level narrative teams drawn from existing universities, cultural institutions and tourism 
bodies. These teams should consist of researchers, cultural interpreters, storytellers and media 
professionals working collaboratively to translate scholarship into accessible visitor narratives. The 
Committee emphasises that this recommendation does not require creation of new authorities or statutory 
bodies, but purposeful coordination and redeployment of existing intellectual capacity. 
10.7 Such teams would ensure consistency, authenticity and depth across physical signage, audio 
guides, digital platforms and immersive experiences. They would also serve as custodians of narrative 
integrity, preventing misinformation, sensationalism or dilution of cultural meaning. The Committee is of 
the view that professionalising interpretation through institutional collaboration is essential to elevate 
visitor experience while maintaining fiscal prudence and administrative simplicity. This approach 
strengthens destination identity and reinforces trust without expanding bureaucratic structures. 
Recommendation 4: Anchoring Tourism Knowledge Creation in Universities 
10.8 The Committee recommends that universities be formally recognised as the primary anchors for 
tourism knowledge creation and interpretation. In alignment with the National Education Policy 2020, 
multidisciplinary universities possess the capability to integrate history, philosophy, archaeology, 
linguistics, ecology, design and technology into coherent tourism narratives. The Committee observes that 
ad-hoc outsourcing of interpretation often results in shallow or inconsistent content. 
10.9 University anchoring ensures research rigour, peer validation and continuity of knowledge. It also 
creates dignified employment pathways for graduates in humanities and social sciences, addressing 
educated unemployment while upgrading tourism quality. The Committee considers this approach 
essential for grounding tourism narratives in scholarship rather than promotion, and for ensuring that 

27 
 
interpretation evolves with research rather than remaining static. To integrate Italian cultural depth, 
universities should emphasize heritage-focused modules in art history, conservation, and local traditions, 
drawing from Italy's model of cultural diplomas to enhance civilisational literacy and sustainability in 
tourism education. The Annexure-I is the indicative National Framework for University-level tourism 
education which the committee recommends. 
Recommendation 5: Create India's First "Heritage Interpreters Cadre" — A New Professional 
Class for Tourism 
10.10 The Committee recommends creation of a dedicated Knowledge and Interpretation Stream within 
the National Tourism Professional Cadre to anchor knowledge‑intensive roles such as heritage 
interpretation, destination storytelling, and thematic circuit design. This cadre should include Cultural 
Interpreters, Heritage Researchers, Narrative Designers and Digital Heritage Specialists, clearly 
distinguished from operational hospitality roles. The Committee notes that absence of professional 
identity has resulted in informal, inconsistent and undervalued interpretation services. 
10.11 A defined cadre with recruitment standards, certification, career progression and remuneration 
benchmarks will attract talent, ensure accountability and restore dignity to tourism professions. The 
Committee notes India’s experience with structured cadres in education, administration and technical 
services demonstrates the effectiveness of this model. Establishing such a cadre elevates tourism from 
casual service provision to a recognised knowledge profession with long-term career viability. To create a 
coherent framework linking jobs to competencies, empower the Tourism Sector Skill Council (NSDC) 
with industry and academia to map tourism roles (heritage guide, rural hospitality manager, eco-tour 
operator, etc.) to standardized NSQF levels and curricula, introducing a unified certification such as a 
national tourist guide license to elevate quality and allow career planning. Implementation should proceed 
in phases, beginning with a limited number of high‑traffic ASI and PRASHAD sites, with clear targets for 
the number of Certified Interpreters deployed, evaluation of visitor feedback, and subsequent scaling 
based on outcomes. 
Recommendation 6: Establishing Clear Career Pathways Beyond Entry-Level Tourism Roles 
10.12 The Committee recommends formal articulation of clear career pathways within tourism 
professions extending beyond entry-level roles. A structured progression from Junior Interpreter to Senior 
Interpreter, Master Interpreter and Destination Strategist should be defined, supported by experience 
thresholds, refresher training and performance evaluation. The Committee observes that lack of visible 
mobility contributes significantly to attrition and declining student interest in tourism education. 
10.13 Clear career pathways restore dignity, incentivise excellence and encourage long-term 
commitment. Tourism must be presented as a profession offering growth, leadership and intellectual 
advancement rather than a temporary occupation. The Committee considers this reform essential to 
workforce stability, institutional memory and service quality.

Literacy

interdisciplinary civilisational literacy. Tourism professionals must understand Indian philosophy, 
religious traditions, art and architecture, archaeology, sacred geography, ecology and comparative 
civilisations. Operational skills alone are inadequate for meaningful interpretation and engagement. 
10.15 The National Education Policy 2020 enables such multidisciplinary learning through flexible 
credits and the Academic Bank of Credits. Embedding this curriculum transforms tourism graduates into 
interpreters of India rather than mere service providers. The Committee believes this reform is critical to

improving visitor experience and strengthening India’s intellectual representation globally. To enhance,

conservation, local arts and crafts), aligning with PRASHAD’s emphasis on skills at heritage sites;

10.16 The Committee recommends mandatory long-duration field residencies at heritage and religious

10.17 These residencies should be academically assessed and linked to production of documented

Recommendation 9: Avoiding Creation of New Apex Bodies Through Institutional Dovetailing 
10.18 The Committee explicitly recommends against creation of new apex institutions or statutory 
bodies for tourism narratives or heritage interpretation. India already possesses universities, ASI, tourism 
institutes and public broadcasters. The challenge lies in alignment, not absence. 
10.19 Reform should occur through dovetailing mandates, assigning research to universities, training to 
tourism institutes, validation to cultural bodies and dissemination to media institutions. This ensures 
speed, fiscal prudence and institutional ownership. Any tourism‑specific accreditation or standard‑setting 
mechanisms shall operate as internal divisions or cells within existing institutions such as the proposed 
National Institute of Hospitality, and shall not be constituted as separate apex statutory bodies.

Recommendation 21: Developing High-Value Spiritual and Cultural Tourism Pathways 
10.42 The Committee recommends targeted development of high-value, low-volume spiritual and 
cultural tourism pathways focused on interpretation quality rather than volume. 
10.43 This increases revenue without ecological or social overload and aligns with India’s civilisational 
positioning. The Committee notes overcrowding risks. High-value paths would balance growth. It has 
seen benefits in similar initiatives. The Committee believes this promotes sustainability. These pathways 
should be designed across multiple segments, including spiritual, ecological, heritage, and wellness 
tourism, with clear carrying‑capacity norms and interpretation standards, so that high‑value growth does 
not replicate the overcrowding and stress observed at current iconic sites. 
Recommendation 22: Creating Interpretation Frameworks for Sites of Memory and Conscience 
10.44 The Committee recommends specialised interpretation frameworks for sites associated with 
sacrifice and historical trauma. Narratives must be factual, empathetic and restrained. 
10.45 India can set global benchmarks in ethical remembrance tourism. The Committee observes 
sensitivity needs at such sites. Frameworks would ensure respect. From evidence, empathetic approaches 
build trust. The Committee views this as a global standard-setter. 
Recommendation 23: Absorbing Humanities Graduates Through Knowledge-Based Tourism Roles 
10.46 The Committee recommends explicitly positioning tourism as a knowledge employment sector 
for humanities and social science graduates. Interpretation and research are cognitive professions. 
10.47 This addresses educated unemployment while upgrading tourism quality. The Committee notes 
underused humanities talent. Positioning tourism as knowledge-driven would absorb it. It draws from 
sector demands. The Committee is persuaded this upgrades quality. 
Recommendation 24: Encouraging Comparative Civilisation Framing for Global Audiences 
10.48 The Committee recommends training interpreters to present Indian civilisation through 
comparative global frames. This aids comprehension, avoids insularity and builds respect. 
10.49 Comparative framing enhances intellectual credibility. The Committee observes that comparisons 
aid understanding. Training would broaden appeal. From reviews, this avoids isolation. The Committee 
believes it strengthens global engagement. 
Recommendation 25: Declaring Narrative Leadership as India’s Tourism Advantage 
10.50 The Committee concludes that India’s decisive tourism advantage lies in narrative leadership. No 
country matches India’s civilisational depth. 
10.51 The challenge is organisation, not invention. Tourism must become the disciplined voice of that 
civilisation. The Committee notes India's unique depth. Organised narratives would leverage it. It has 
examined effective examples. The Committee views this as a core strength. 
Recommendation 26: Mandatory Local Micro-Guide Certification for Homestay Licensing 
10.52 The Committee recommends that issuance and renewal of homestay licences by State 
Governments and local authorities be made conditional upon completion of a basic local micro-guide 

33 
 
certification by at least one registered member of the homestay household. The Committee observes that 
homestays are often the first and most trusted point of contact for tourists, particularly in rural, border, 
ecological and spiritual destinations. However, hosts are currently licensed only for accommodation, 
despite routinely providing informal guidance, interpretation and local advice to visitors. 
10.53 The Committee is of the view that this represents a missed opportunity to strengthen visitor 
experience, trust and local employment simultaneously. A short, modular micro-guide certification, 
focused on local history, culture, ecology, ethics, safety and responsible tourism, would enable homestay 
hosts to act as reliable first-level interpreters without turning them into full-time guides. This certification 
should be delivered digitally and locally, aligned with the proposed national micro-guide platform and 
Recognition of Prior Learning frameworks. 
10.54 Making such certification mandatory for licensing does not impose an external burden, but rather 
formalises what already occurs informally. It professionalises homestays, improves quality assurance for 
tourists, and embeds knowledge capability directly within local communities. The Committee considers 
this a low-cost, high-impact intervention that integrates accommodation, interpretation and livelihood 
generation into a single coherent tourism ecosystem. To promote inclusive development, leverage 
schemes like PRASHAD, Swadesh Darshan and CBSP to train and certify local residents as tourism 
service providers, including budget lines for local skill training in local languages covering guiding, 
hospitality, homestay management and traditional crafts, certifying village youth as heritage guides or 
artisans. For rural tourism, embed training in community development schemes under DAY-NRLM/VB-
G RAM G, with modules on homestay hospitality, organic farm management and handicraft marketing; 
mandate training villagers in guest services and basic digital marketing in Swadesh Darshan rural circuits, 
offering certificate courses in rural tourism management through local vocational institutes. The 
Committee recognises that licensing is a State subject and therefore recommends that States adapt this 
requirement to local contexts, including reasonable transition periods and low‑cost, digitally delivered 
modules, to avoid exclusion of small homestays while steadily raising minimum standards. 
Recommendation 27: Border-Area Tourism Skills 
10.55 The Committee recommends specialized programs for border-area guides and operators in 
frontier tourism with Nepal, Bhutan, Bangladesh and China. Training should cover foreign languages 
(e.g., Nepali, Burmese, Mandarin), regional history, cultural protocols and emergency response for 
remote terrains. Joint programs between Indian and neighboring authorities could develop curricula for 
trans-boundary heritage circuits. Vocational centres in border states should offer certificate courses in 
adventure tourism and cross-cultural liaison, financed via DoNER/NEC schemes. This boosts local 
incomes, regional goodwill, and integrates with ‘Neighbourhood First’ and Act East initiatives through 
coordination between Tourism and External Affairs ministries. 
10.56 The Committee observes untapped potential in border regions. Specialized training would 
empower locals. It notes diplomatic benefits from joint programs. The Committee believes this aligns 
with national initiatives. 
Recommendation 28: Women and Inclusion in Tourism 
10.57 The Committee stresses targeted skilling of women, SC/ST and other disadvantaged communities 
to empower underrepresented groups. Training centers, especially rural ITIs/Polytechnics, should reserve 
seats and outreach for women. Courses in hospitality management, handicrafts, guiding and 
entrepreneurship must be accessible via special scholarships. Promote self-help group models like 
women-run homestays or craft cooperatives under skill and livelihood schemes. This fosters inclusive 
economic growth, new opportunities for marginalized groups, and a diverse tourism workforce, aligning 
with Skill India’s social focus. 

34 
 
10.58 The Committee notes persistent disparities. Targeted measures would promote equity. It has seen 
success in self-help models. The Committee is convinced this drives growth. 
Recommendation 29: Continuous Upskilling for Tourism Workers 
10.59  The Committee recommends establishing a system of continuous professional development for 
tourism workers, including periodic short courses or online refreshers in new technology, safety norms or 
customer service. Leveraging online platforms, experienced staff can upgrade skills using government-
supported content. This ensures the workforce stays current on best practices like hygiene and digital 
tools, improving service quality, and can be encouraged through industry bodies and institutes offering 
continuing education funded via corporate social responsibility. 
10.60 The Committee observes rapid changes in the sector. Continuous development is essential. It 
draws from effective online models. The Committee views this as key to quality.The Committee 
recommends that participation in recognised continuing professional development be progressively linked 
to renewal of individual licences under the National Tourism Licensure Framework and to institutional 
accreditation scores, to embed continuous learning into sector culture rather than treating it as an optional 
add‑on. 
Recommendation 30: Industry–Academia Linkages 
10.61 The Committee recommends that every tourism and hospitality education institution establish a 
formal industry advisory board, drawing representation from hotels, tour operators, online travel 
companies, heritage and museum professionals, and emerging segments such as eco‑tourism and digital 
tourism. These boards should meet at defined intervals to review curricula, identify new skill needs, and 
advise on course content, practical components, and assessment methods so that programmes stay aligned 
with fast‑changing industry realities. Minutes and actions arising from these meetings should be 
documented and integrated into periodic curriculum revisions, in harmony with NEP‑2020’s emphasis on 
flexibility and experiential learning. 
10.62 The Committee further recommends that institutes institutionalise structured mechanisms for 
faculty industry exposure, including short-term attachments, sabbaticals, joint projects, and co‑taught 
modules with practitioners. Regular exposure will help faculty incorporate current practices, technology 
use, revenue models, and service standards into teaching, reducing the lag between classroom content and 
workplace requirements. On the student side, institutions should build long‑term placement and internship 
arrangements with tourism businesses through MoUs, covering paid internships, apprenticeship-style 
training, project work, and priority recruitment days. These linkages should extend beyond large hotel 
chains to include small and medium enterprises, destination management organisations, and community 
tourism initiatives, reflecting the sector’s actual structure. 
10.63 Regulatory guidelines issued by the Ministry of Tourism, in consultation with the Ministry of 
Education and industry associations, can make such boards and linkages a standard expectation for all 
recognised tourism programmes. National bodies such as NCHMCT and IITTM already demonstrate the 
benefits of close industry collaboration through curriculum revamp task forces, hotel‑chain MoUs, and 
improved placement metrics, and their experience should inform a broader framework for all institutions. 
The Committee is of the view that systematic, mandatory industry–academia engagement will narrow the 
current gap between education and employment, improve job readiness, support smoother school‑to‑work 
transitions, and enhance the credibility and attractiveness of tourism education among prospective 
students. 
Building on the existing MoT‑facilitated MoU model between Central IHMs and hotel chains, the 
Committee recommends that similar structured partnership templates be extended to State IHMs, 
universities and private institutes, with periodic reporting on faculty trained, students placed and 

35 
 
curriculum changes undertaken. The Committee recommends that, within three years, all publicly funded 
tourism and hospitality institutions report at least one active industry advisory board and formal MoUs 
covering internships and placements, with these metrics disclosed annually in the public domain. 
Recommendation 31: International Collaboration for Training 
10.64 The Committee recommends a structured programme of international collaboration to raise the 
quality and global relevance of tourism and hospitality training in India. Formal partnerships should be 
established with leading foreign institutions, including hospitality schools in Singapore, universities in 
Japan, and hotel management and culinary colleges in Italy, with clear objectives and time-bound work 
plans. These collaborations should cover joint curriculum design, faculty exchange, visiting 
professorships, co-taught modules, and credit-bearing student internships in partner countries. 
Memoranda of Understanding under Skill India’s international skilling initiatives can provide the 
enabling framework, including mutual recognition of short-term training, standard operating procedures 
for internships, and safeguards for student welfare. 
10.65 The Committee is of the view that such partnerships will allow Indian institutions to 
systematically internalise tested global practices, such as Singapore’s customer service excellence 
models, Japanese approaches to precision, safety and hospitality, and Italy’s strengths in culinary tourism, 
wine tourism and heritage-based destination development. Joint curriculum work should ensure that 
imported standards are adapted to Indian contexts, rather than copied mechanically, preserving India’s 
civilisational and cultural distinctiveness while meeting international expectations. For students, 
structured international internships and exchange semesters will provide cross-cultural experience, 
workplace exposure, and foreign language practice that are increasingly necessary for employment in 
global tourism markets. For faculty, regular international attachment and collaborative research will keep 
course content updated and encourage innovation in pedagogy. The Committee notes evidence submitted 
on the positive impact of existing MOUs in related sectors and is persuaded that a scaled-up, tourism-
focused international collaboration framework will significantly elevate instructional quality, 
benchmarking, and global exposure across India’s tourism education ecosystem. 
10.66 The Committee further recommends that all such collaborations incorporate clear safeguards for 
student welfare abroad, including minimum stipend norms, grievance channels, and recognition of credits 
on return, to ensure that international exposure remains equitable and safe. 
10.67 The academic partnerships/ tie-ups for each of 21 Indian of Hotel Management (IHM) operating 
under the aegis of National Council for Hotel Management and Catering Technology (NCHMCT) needs 
to be viewed from a nuanced lens. Each of the IHMs needs to be mapped, based on the unique training 
needs of their students, to a mentor training institute offering hospitality studies, a leader from some other 
country. A similar model was adopted during the infancy years of the Indian Institutes of Technology 
(IITs) and Indian Institutes of Management (IIMs).  
10.69 Every effort should be made to ensure that no two IHMS are mentored by the same parent institute 
to ensure sufficient diversity. Further, diversity should be maintained in tie-ups within country 
groupings—for instance European Union (E.U), Asia Pacific and the like.  
 
10.68 The mapping exercise needs to be carried out with utmost care to include not just training needs 
but also cultural sensitivities. Therefore, the expertise of the Ministry of Culture may be sought in this 
regard. The mentoring should continue for a sufficient period of time, say a decade, after which newer 
partnerships may be explored.  
 
10.69 Lastly, it should be ensured that the administrative leadership form all 21 IIMS is represented in 
the governing body of NCHMCT along with GoI nominated representatives from both the Ministry of 
Tourism and Ministry of Culture to ensure seamless coordination and effect holistic decision-making in 
the country’s hospitality education.

10.70  Further, the IHMs can also move into a mentorship role for other government and private 
institutes in the country that lack the resources for foreign tie-ups. This will ensure the percolation of 
quality hospitality education down to the very last hospitality-training institute in the country.  
Recommendation 32: Accreditation of Tourism Programs 
10.71 This recommendation responds to the Committee’s finding that fragmented accreditation and the 
absence of a single sectoral reference standard undermine both student choice and employer confidence. 
The Committee recommends the establishment of a sector-specific accreditation cell for tourism and 
hospitality education within the proposed National Institute of Hospitality, functioning in close 
coordination with the Ministry of Tourism and the Ministry of Education. This cell should operate as a 
specialised arm of existing national quality assurance systems rather than as a new standalone authority, 
thereby reinforcing frameworks such as NAAC while addressing the distinct needs of tourism education. 
10.72 Under this mechanism, all tourism-related programmes, including those offered by Institutes of 
Hotel Management, universities, community colleges, ITIs, and private providers, would be periodically 
evaluated against transparent criteria. These criteria should include curriculum relevance to contemporary 
tourism needs, faculty qualifications and continuous development, adequacy of laboratories and training 
infrastructure, strength of industry linkages, student support systems, graduate employability, and 
adherence to ethical and inclusive practices. Institutions meeting defined benchmarks would receive 
graded accreditation status linked to tangible incentives, such as preferential access to central and state 
grants, eligibility for sponsored pilot projects, and formal recognition in official prospectuses and digital 
platforms. 
10.73 For students and parents, an accreditation mark specific to tourism will serve as a clear quality 
signal, enabling informed choice in an otherwise fragmented landscape. For institutions, regular 
accreditation cycles will create a structured incentive for continuous improvement in teaching, 
infrastructure, and placement outcomes. By embedding tourism-specific parameters into the national 
quality architecture, the Committee believes this reform will reduce current variations in programme 
standards, protect learners from substandard offerings, and support the emergence of a coherent, credible 
pipeline of tourism professionals. 
Recommendation 33: Early Education Integration for Tourism 
10.74 The Committee recommends that tourism and hospitality be introduced early in the education 
system so students see it as a serious knowledge-based career option rather than a fallback choice. 
Introductory modules on tourism, heritage, and hospitality in secondary school curricula should 
familiarise students with concepts such as visitor behaviour, basic service standards, cultural sensitivity, 
and responsible travel. Tourism clubs, local heritage walks, and structured field visits to museums, 
monuments, and ecological sites would provide practical exposure and connect classroom learning with 
lived experience. 
10.75 At the higher education level, colleges and universities should offer minors, electives, and 
certificate courses in tourism and hospitality that are credit-linked under NEP 2020, delivered in 
partnership with recognised tourism institutes and industry bodies. Open-source learning materials and 
digital resources can be used to reduce costs and enable uniform access, especially in smaller towns and 
rural areas. To embed Indian civilisational depth, universities should design heritage-focused modules in 
art history, conservation, intangible cultural practices, and local traditions, with a strong emphasis on 
communication skills. Together, these measures will spark early interest, expand the future talent pool, 
and produce graduates capable of interpreting India’s cultural landscapes with academic rigour and 
public-facing clarity.

Recommendation 34: Incentives for Training Investment 
10.76 Incentives for training investment should directly reward organisations that treat human capital as 
a core tourism asset, not a cost line. The Committee recommends a calibrated package of fiscal measures, 
combining targeted tax rebates, CSR co-matching, and outcome-linked grants to nudge employers and 
local institutions towards sustained skilling efforts. Companies that invest in structured upskilling, 
certified courses, and continuous professional development for tourism workers would become eligible 
for defined tax deductions linked to verified training expenditure and completion outcomes. 
10.77 At the community level, grants could support Panchayats, cooperatives, and local bodies that run 
accredited tourism workshops in guiding, hospitality, digital skills, and responsible tourism practices, 
with simplified access for rural and border areas. To avoid input-centric schemes, incentives should be 
tied to measurable results such as improved guest satisfaction scores, higher repeat visitation, increased 
local hiring, or formalisation of informal workers. Alignment with existing government tools, including 
credit-linked subsidies and CSR frameworks, will minimise administrative complexity while increasing 
uptake. Clear guidelines, transparent reporting, and periodic evaluation will be needed so that public 
support translates into higher-quality services, better livelihoods, and a culture of continuous learning 
across India’s tourism ecosystem. 
11 Conclusion 
11.1 The Committee unequivocally states that India’s tourism challenge is not infrastructure. It is 
interpretation. The country has constructed world-class corridors; what is now required to be done is to 
train world-class storytellers. The Committee notes that the country have undertaken initiatives that 
symbolise modern development; now we have to create professionals who can tell five thousand years of 
civilisation with correctness, dignity and pride. This Report represents an enormous shift. Tourism is no 
longer a service vocation that is learned on the job. It is a knowledge profession earned in the classroom, 
refined in the field, and renewed through continuous learning. As medical students become doctors 
through residencies, tourism students must now be heritage interpreters with attachment requirements at 
archaeological sites, museums and UNESCO World Heritage locations. Just as engineers construct 
infrastructure, so tourism professionals must erect trust from within their communities. 
11.2 To undertake this transformation, the Committee has built a structure using three buildings to 
embody the following key elements: 
 
First Pillar: Universities as Knowledge Anchors. Curriculum design, research excellence and 
integration of Indian Knowledge Systems will drive tourism education in multidisciplinary universities. 
The National Education Policy 2020 means that tourism graduates will no longer be trained in operation 
only. They will also learn history, archaeology, philosophy, ecology and comparative civilisations. They 
will have the intellectual acumen to tell the world India. 
Second Pillar: Destination-Level Narrative Teams. Teams of researchers, cultural interpreters, 
storytellers and digital creators will support each of those major religious and heritage destinations. For 
these, those teams will translate university work into visitor experience via physical signage, audio 
guides, immersive technologies and authentic digital content. Narrative coherence will supplant narrative 
chaos. 
Third Pillar: National Tourism Professional Cadre. A unique Knowledge and Interpretation Stream 
in this cadre will give birth to India’s first professional group of Heritage Interpreters, Cultural 
Storytellers, Destination Strategists and Digital Tourism Creators. Career pathways from entry-level 
interpreter to senior leadership will return tourism professions to dignity and entice the very best. 
 
11.3 The Committee has worked hard on ensuring that this architecture is inclusive, not exclusive. 
Recognition of Prior Learning will incorporate informal guidance and knowledge-holders without 

38 
 
displacement of the job market. National digital platforms will allow tourists to book verified certified 
guides directly—an “Uber for tour guides” free of touting and where all you need is good name 
recognition. Compulsory micro-guide certification for homestay licensing will professionalise rural and 
border tourism, maintaining authenticity as well. Women, Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and 
youth will be supported to gain entry into knowledge-based tourism roles. 
 
11.4 Ethics have been built into the foundation of the Committee as a very well-embedded aspect. 
Zero tolerance for tout culture. Ethical evaluation is mandatory for every guide licence. Licence 
renewal will rely on verified feedback, continuing learning. Scoring of the digital reputation will inspire 
honest behavior for market purposes. Tourism Protection Officers at popular destinations will offer 
direct assistance to harassed tourists. All professional misconduct will be swiftly and fairly 
suspended or revoked. 
 
11.5 The Committee interpreted technology as an interpretational infrastructure. The National 
Destination Digital Knowledge Grid acts as a training sandbox for students to develop accredited 
heritage content as coursework. With AI-driven personalisation, AR/VR immersive experiences & India-
origin digital narratives, India will become a creator, not consumer, of tourism technology. In that 
knowledge domain, so does Digital India and Atmanirbhar Bharat. 
 
11.6 The Committee has designed modes of operation that work and do not create new bureaucracies. 
No new apex bodies: This should bring existing universities, tourism institutes, cultural institutions and 
broadcasters in common hand through a lightweight inter-ministerial coordination mechanism. 
Institutional dovetailing replaces institutional proliferation. Standards will be established nationally; 
the delivery will be spread out regionally. There will be implementation on the ground from State 
Governments, Panchayats, community colleges and local bodies. 
 
11.7 The Committee has learned from the world but remained rooted in India. International 
collaboration will bring global standards without erasing civilisational identity. Comparative framing 
in interpretation will help international visitors understand Indian philosophy through familiar reference 
points. India will forge international benchmarks for ethical remembrance tourism, spiritual-luxury 
pathways and narrative-driven destination management. 
 
 
11.8 The Committee rejects the numbers game. The success of tourism is not in the visitor turnout. 
Visitor understanding is. The one visitor who leaves India understanding the philosophical dimension of 
a temple sculpture is worth something more than ten who leave with photographs. The key metrics are 
these: Do visitors understand what they see? Do they trust whom they meet? Do they depart as 
ambassadors or critics? A count of arrivals does not answer these questions. They call for measurement 
of the quality of interpretations, ethical conduct, professional competency and narrative coherence. 
 
11.9 The Committee has accordingly recommended that India’s tourism strategy shift from volume to 
value, from infrastructure to interpretation, from footfall to impact. High-value, low-volume spiritual 
and cultural tourism pathways will produce more revenue without overtaxing the ecosystem. Sites of 
civilisational significance will be reframed as knowledge hubs, not transit points. Border and rural 
circuits will share benefits well beyond established destinations. That pivot is not a rejection of growth. It 
redefines growth. India can compete with France, Spain and Thailand not by duplicating their 
example, but by exploiting what they cannot copy: five thousand years of uninterrupted civilisation, 
articulated by individuals prepared to read it with depth, dignity and accuracy. 

39 
 
 
11.10 This report goes beyond tourism reformation. It is about India's voice in the world. Every 
tourist who goes to India comes home with a story—accurate or not, respectful or dismissive, 
knowledgeable or ignorant. That story determines how a billion people perceive us. It shapes investment, 
diplomacy, cultural exchanges and strategic alliances. For too long, we have let India’s story be told by 
others or narrated poorly by ourselves. 
 
This Report aims to correct that. It is in professionalising interpretation that we recover narrative 
sovereignty. We communicate that our civilisation is not a relic to be visited, but a living knowledge 
system to be studied by trained Heritage Interpreters as rigorously as we train engineers.  
11.11 The Committee proudly notes that from India’s G20 Presidency came the lesson learnt of 
disciplined and confident storytelling which is, in itself, rooted in a deep civilisational mooring. That 
approach now needs to be institutionalised through tourism. Tourism is India's most continuous 
interface with the world. Once that interface is mediated through untrained guides and unethical 
practices, no amount of diplomacy can make up for lost time. If it is mediated by certified professionals 
telling India’s story with accuracy and pride, no amount of criticism can undermine that.  
11.12 The Committee records that this transformation is achievable. India has the universities, the 
scholars, the institutions and the political will. What has been lacking is alignment, not capacity. These 
recommendations present the alignment framework. They do not call for huge budgets, or decades of 
gestation. They need strong action, institutional collaboration and continued oversight. 
The Committee's Expectations:- 
 
11.13 The Committee invites the Government to take these recommendations seriously as they deserve. 
They embody Parliament's considered judgement concerning what India's tourism industry needs to 
unlock its potential. They are based on a wealth of oral evidence, institutional presentations, field visits, 
comparative analysis and scholarly consultation. These are not suggestions; they are recommendations 
carrying the authority of a Parliamentary Standing Committee constituted under the extant Rules of 
Procedure.  
 
11.14 The Committee concludes with a measure of confidence with the hope that the 
observations/recommendations enumerated in the preceding paragraphs will be implemented by the 
Ministry in a systematic manner. India’s tourism future is not constrained by assets. We have monuments 
that dwarf those of competitor nations. We have cultural diversity unmatched anywhere. We have 
spiritual traditions that attract seekers from across the world. What we hadn't had, what we lacked, was 
the human capital to turn those assets into experiences that develop trust, respect and understanding. This 
Report is a blueprint for how to build that human capital. Informal guides to certified interpreters. The 
spectrum from ad-hoc training to structured education. From growth fueled by volume to impact borne by 
narrative. From service vocation to knowledge profession. This is the transformation India’s tourism 
sector needs and deserves. The monuments will stand. The question is: who will explain them? The 
Committee answered this question. Professionals trained in universities. Certified through rigorous 
standards. Guided by ethics. Equipped with technology. Rooted in civilisation. Confident in their 
knowledge. Proud of their nation. That is the tourism workforce that India must build. That is the tourism 
sector India must become. That is the India the world must experience.

ANNEXURE I 
NATIONAL FRAMEWORK FOR UNIVERSITY-LEVEL 
TOURISM EDUCATION: BROAD CURRICULUM GUIDELINES 
PART A: FRAMEWORK PRINCIPLES 
A.1 Preamble 
This Annexure operationalizes Recommendation 4 by providing broad curriculum guidelines for 
university-level tourism education across India. The framework is anchored in the National Education 
Policy 2020 and positions universities as the primary architects of tourism knowledge creation and 
professional interpretation. 
The guidelines establish what must be taught while leaving how it is taught to the academic autonomy 
of universities and regulatory bodies. The Committee's intent is not to prescribe detailed syllabi but to 
ensure national coherence in competency outcomes while respecting institutional diversity and regional 
context. 
A.2 Core Principle: Tourism as Knowledge Profession 
The curriculum framework rests on a fundamental repositioning: tourism education must produce 
interpreters and knowledge professionals, not service workers. Every graduate must possess the 
intellectual depth to explain India's civilizational heritage, natural diversity, and contemporary reality 
with accuracy, dignity, and scholarly rigor. 
This requires integration of disciplines that formal education systems have historically separated: history 
and ecology, archaeology and technology, philosophy and hospitality, ethics and commerce. Universities, 
with their multidisciplinary capacity, are uniquely positioned to deliver this integration. 
A.3 Programme Structure 
Undergraduate Programme: Four-year Bachelor's degree in Tourism Studies (BA/BSc) with Honours, 
aligned with NEP 2020 provisions for multiple entry-exit options. 
Postgraduate Programme: Two-year Master's degree in Tourism Studies (MA/MSc) with mandatory 
research dissertation and extended field residency. 
Integrated Programme: Five-year integrated Master's combining undergraduate and postgraduate study 
with enhanced field exposure. 
Exit Flexibility: Universities shall design exit pathways at Year 1 (Certificate), Year 2 (Diploma), and 
Year 3 (Degree without Honours) to accommodate diverse student circumstances while maintaining 
academic standards. 

41 
 
PART B: CORE KNOWLEDGE DOMAINS (Mandatory for All Students) 
Universities shall ensure that all tourism graduates, regardless of specialization, acquire foundational 
competence across the following eight knowledge domains. Academic bodies shall determine course 
structures, pedagogical methods, and assessment frameworks. 
Domain B.1: Indian Civilisation and Comparative Philosophy 
Competency Objectives: 
● Articulate India's civilisational contributions within global historical and philosophical contexts 
● Interpret religious, philosophical, and cultural traditions with scholarly accuracy 
● Communicate complex civilisational concepts to diverse audiences including international 
visitors 
Indicative Content Areas: 
● Historical evolution: Indus Valley through contemporary India; major empires, kingdoms, and 
cultural syntheses 
● Philosophical traditions: Six orthodox and heterodox schools; Vedanta, Buddhist, Jain, Sikh, Sufi, 
and syncretic thought 
● Religious diversity: Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, Sikhism, Islam, Christianity, Judaism, 
indigenous traditions; ritual systems and pilgrimage cultures 
● Comparative frameworks: India-China, India-Greece, India-Persia, India-Southeast Asia; 
philosophical parallels, artistic exchanges, trade networks 
● Indian Knowledge Systems: Astronomy, mathematics, medicine, architecture, performing arts; 
historical development and contemporary relevance 
Outcome Indicators: Graduates shall demonstrate capacity to contextualize monuments, practices, and 
traditions within accurate historical and philosophical frameworks; provide comparative perspectives that 
enhance international visitor comprehension; avoid both Orientalist exoticism and defensive nationalism 
in interpretation. 
Domain B.2: Natural Heritage and Ecological Science 
Competency Objectives: 
● Understand India's geological formations, climatic systems, and biodiversity patterns 
● Interpret natural heritage sites with scientific accuracy 
● Apply ecological principles to tourism planning and visitor management 
 
Indicative Content Areas: 
● Geology and physical geography: Himalayan formation, Western Ghats, Deccan Plateau, coastal 
and marine systems, desert ecology 

42 
 
● Biodiversity: Forest types, wildlife species (flagship and endemic), avian diversity, freshwater 
and marine ecosystems 
● Climate science: Monsoon systems, climate zones, climate change impacts on tourism 
destinations 
● Conservation biology: Protected area management, human-wildlife conflict, carrying capacity, 
ecosystem services 
● Disaster preparedness: Floods, landslides, earthquakes, cyclones; tourist safety protocols 
● Sustainable tourism: IUCN guidelines, environmental impact assessment, waste management, 
circular economy principles 
Outcome Indicators: Graduates shall conduct basic ecological site assessments; design low-impact 
tourism interventions; interpret natural heritage through ecological rather than mythological lenses while 
respecting cultural narratives; understand conservation challenges and visitor responsibility. 
Domain B.3: Art, Architectural, and Archaeological Heritage 
Competency Objectives: 
● Develop chronological understanding of Indian art and architecture from ancient to contemporary 
periods 
● Interpret architectural features, iconography, and artistic traditions on-site 
● Connect historical narratives to physical heritage assets 
Indicative Content Areas: 
● Ancient period: Indus Valley urban planning; Mauryan stupas and pillars; Gandhara and Mathura 
art; Ajanta-Ellora caves 
● Classical and medieval: Gupta temples; Pallava, Chola, Rashtrakuta monuments; Delhi Sultanate 
architecture; Vijayanagara complexes; Mughal monuments and gardens 
● Regional traditions: Nagara and Dravidian temple architecture; stepwells; Buddhist monasteries; 
vernacular architecture across regions 
● Colonial heritage: Portuguese, Dutch, French, British; Indo-Saracenic style; Art Deco; railway 
and industrial heritage 
● Archaeological methods: Epigraphy, stratigraphy, dating techniques, conservation principles 
● Iconography and symbolism: Hindu, Buddhist, Jain, Islamic artistic traditions and interpretive 
frameworks 
Outcome Indicators: Graduates shall read architectural features and explain construction techniques; 
interpret inscriptions and sculptural narratives; provide historically accurate timelines; distinguish 
between evidence-based history and legendary narratives while respecting both. 
 
Domain B.4: Cultural Anthropology and Living Traditions 
Competency Objectives: 
● Understand India's social, linguistic, and cultural diversity 

43 
 
● Appreciate and interpret intangible cultural heritage 
● Facilitate ethical cultural tourism that respects community dignity 
Indicative Content Areas: 
● Social structures: Caste, tribe, religion; linguistic diversity; regional identities 
● Performing arts: Classical music (Hindustani, Carnatic); classical dance forms; folk traditions; 
theatre and storytelling 
● Material culture: Textile traditions (weaving, printing, embroidery); handicrafts (pottery, metal, 
wood, stone); artisan lineages and Geographical Indications 
● Festivals and rituals: Major religious festivals; regional celebrations; life-cycle rituals; pilgrimage 
traditions

PART D: MANDATORY FIELD RESIDENCIES 
The Committee mandates that all tourism programmes, regardless of specialization, include 
substantial field-based learning. Classroom knowledge must be tested, refined, and internalized through 
extended professional practice in authentic tourism settings. 
Residency Principles: 
● Duration: Minimum cumulative 24 weeks across the programme 
● Supervision: Joint supervision by academic faculty and professional practitioners 
● Assessment: Performance evaluation by site supervisors; reflective documentation by students; 
capstone projects demonstrating applied competence 
● Sites: Archaeological sites and museums; National Parks and Biosphere Reserves; pilgrimage 
centers and temple complexes; culinary destinations and artisan villages; tourism enterprises and 
technology companies; government planning and management agencies 
Institutional Partnerships: Universities shall establish formal Memoranda of Understanding with the 
Archaeological Survey of India, State Museums, Ministry of Environment Forest and Climate Change, 
State Tourism Departments, major pilgrimage trusts, and leading tourism enterprises to ensure structured, 
supervised field learning opportunities. 
The Committee expects field residencies to function as professional apprenticeships where students 
transition from observers to practitioners under mentorship, producing tangible outputs (interpretation 
scripts, conservation plans, digital content, management frameworks) that benefit host institutions. 
PART E: MULTILINGUAL DELIVERY AND REGIONAL 
ADAPTATION 
Language Policy: While English remains the primary medium for academic rigour and global 
comparability, universities shall ensure accessibility through: 
● Hindi and regional language instruction for students preferring vernacular education 
● Bilingual learning materials in eight Scheduled Languages (Hindi, Bengali, Tamil, Telugu, 
Marathi, Gujarati, Kannada, Malayalam) 
● Regional language proficiency as alternative to foreign language requirements 
● Regional heritage case studies integrated into curriculum reflecting state and local cultural assets 
Regional Contextualization: Universities shall adapt generic frameworks to regional realities: 
● South Indian universities emphasize Dravidian architecture, Carnatic music, temple economy 
management 
● Himalayan universities emphasize mountain ecology, Buddhist heritage, adventure tourism, 
border area dynamics 

48 
 
● Coastal universities emphasize marine ecology, port heritage, cruise tourism, maritime trade 
history 
● Tribal-majority regions emphasize indigenous knowledge systems, community-based tourism, 
ethnographic interpretation 
This regional adaptation must occur within national quality standards to ensure graduate mobility and 
credential portability across India. 
PART F: INCLUSION AND ACCESSIBILITY 
Universities shall embed inclusion across programme design: 
Socio-Economic Inclusion: Reserved seats as per national policy (SC/ST/OBC/EWS); scholarships and 
fee waivers; remedial coaching; targeted outreach in rural, tribal, and border areas. 
Gender Inclusion: Minimum 33% female enrollment targets; Prevention of Sexual Harassment 
committees; women-focused mentorship; safe field residency protocols. 
Disability Inclusion: Accessible infrastructure; assistive technologies; modified assessments; adapted 
field residencies prioritizing accessible heritage sites. 
Recognition of Prior Learning: Portfolio-based credit for documented professional experience 
(maximum 20% of programme); bridge courses addressing theoretical gaps; multilingual assessment 
options. 
PART G: QUALITY ASSURANCE FRAMEWORK 
Internal Mechanisms: Universities shall establish robust internal quality systems including student 
feedback mechanisms; peer teaching observations; annual curriculum reviews; graduate tracking and 
employability studies; employer feedback on graduate preparedness. 
External Accreditation: All tourism programmes shall undergo periodic external accreditation through: 
● National Assessment and Accreditation Council (NAAC) for overall institutional quality 
● Sector-Specific Accreditation Cell within the proposed National Institute of Hospitality for 
tourism programme quality 
● International benchmarking through partnerships with UNWTO TedQual or leading global 
hospitality schools 
Accreditation Criteria: Curriculum relevance and rigour; faculty qualifications and continuous 
development; infrastructure adequacy (laboratories, libraries, field equipment); field residency quality and 
institutional partnerships; graduate employability outcomes; research output and knowledge contribution. 
Graded Recognition: Accreditation outcomes shall be graded and publicly disclosed, with high-
performing programmes receiving preferential access to government funding, international partnerships, 
and advanced research opportunities.

PART H: IMPLEMENTATION RESPONSIBILITIES 
University Grants Commission (UGC): Recognize tourism studies as a formal academic discipline; 
approve programme structures aligned with NEP 2020; facilitate Academic Bank of Credits integration; 
support credit transfer and mobility frameworks. 
Ministry of Education: Coordinate with UGC for curriculum approval; allocate resources for 
infrastructure development; facilitate faculty recruitment and training; support multilingual content 
development. 
Ministry of Tourism: Provide sector expertise for curriculum validation; facilitate institutional 
partnerships with ASI, museums, protected areas, pilgrimage centers; fund field residency infrastructure; 
establish sector-specific accreditation cell within National Institute of Hospitality. 
Central Universities (Hub Institutions): Develop detailed syllabi and course materials operationalizing 
these broad guidelines; train faculty at spoke institutions; conduct curriculum research and periodic 
reviews; establish field residency site networks. 
State Universities (Spoke Institutions): Adopt hub-developed curricula with regional adaptations; 
deliver programmes at scale; establish regional field residency sites; contribute regional case studies and 
content. 
National Institute of Hospitality: Function as apex quality assurance body; develop accreditation 
standards and processes; coordinate sector-wide faculty development; facilitate international 
benchmarking; maintain national registry of accredited programmes.
