1.0 Purpose of the Committee's Report. 
1.1 Examination of the Civil Aviation Safety Environment Amidst Rapid Growth 
1.1.1 India’s civil aviation sector has, over the last two decades, undergone a period of unprecedented expansion and 
transformation, firmly establishing itself as the third -largest domestic aviation market in the world. This remarkable growth 
trajectory, while a testament to the nation's economic dynamism, carries with it a profound and non -negotiable responsibility 
to ensure that safety remains the paramount priority in every facet of aviation operations. The Ministry of Civil Aviation, 
supported by its attache d and autonomous organisations —including the Directorate General of Civil Aviation (DGCA), the 
Airports Authority of India (AAI), the Bureau of Civil Aviation Security (BCAS), and the Aircraft Accident Investigation 
Bureau (AAIB)—has endeavoured to maintai n a robust safety oversight framework in consonance with the international 
standards and recommended practices prescribed by the International Civil Aviation Organisation (ICAO). 
1.1.2  However, the very success of the sector has introduced a fresh challen ge that defines the current safety landscape. 
The rapid induction of aircraft into the fleet of airline operators , a surge in passenger traffic, and the proliferation of 
aerodromes under regional connectivity initiatives such as the UDAN scheme have create d new and complex challenges. 
These pressures test the limits of the existing regulatory and operational infrastructure, raising critical questions about t he 
adequacy of technical manpower, the presence of infrastructural constraints at Tier -II and Tier-III airports, the maturity and 
implementation of safety management systems, and the capacity of regulatory surveillance mechanisms to keep pace with 
the industry they oversee. The UDAN (Ude Desh ka Aam Nagrik) scheme, a Regional Connectivity Scheme (RCS), wa s 
launched by the Government of India in October 2016. Implemented by the Ministry of Civil Aviation, its primary objective 
was to democratize air travel, which was historically perceived as accessible only to a privileged few. The scheme has been 
seminal in transforming India’s regional connectivity landscape by improving flight operations and optimizing the use of 
unserved and underserved airports across the country. The policy's success in activating these smaller regional airports is, 
however, directly linked to the emergence of new safety challenges. These newly operational aerodromes often lack the 
robust infrastructure, experienced technical manpower, and advanced emergency response capabilities characteristic of 
major metropolitan hubs. Consequently,  the laudable policy goal of rapid expansion is creating a significant and growing 
stress test on a safety framework that has not been scaled at a commensurate pace. 
1.1.3 It is against this backdrop of dynamic growth and escalating complexity that the Com mittee considered it both 
timely and imperative to undertake a comprehensive review of the overall safety environment within the civil aviation 
sector. This examination was conceived not merely to scrutinise the current regulatory architecture and its effi cacy, but to 
conduct a holistic assessment of the adequacy of measures being adopted by all stakeholders. The ultimate goal is to ensure 
the safe and secure carriage of passengers and cargo within an aviation ecosystem that continues to expand at a formida ble 
rate, making certain that the foundations of safety are strong enough to support the industry's future growth. 
1.2 Objectives and Scope of the Comprehensive Review 
1.2.1 The Committee’s examination of this critical subject has been guided by a clear an d comprehensive set of 
objectives, designed to probe the systemic health of India’s aviation safety regime. The primary objectives of this review 
were as follows: 
i. To review the overall state of civil aviation safety in India, placing it in the context of r apid growth in fleet, traffic, 
and operational complexity, with particular attention to the nation's compliance with ICAO Standards and 
Recommended Practices (SARPs). 
ii. To evaluate the effectiveness of the Directorate General of Civil Aviation (DGCA) in disc harging its core mandate 
of safety oversight, certification, surveillance, and enforcement, including a thorough review of its financial, 
technical, and human resource capacities. 

11 
iii. To examine the Airports Authority of India’s (AAI) dual mandate of enhancing  air connectivity across the country 
while simultaneously ensuring the safety and efficiency of flight operations within Indian airspace. 
iv. To assess the implementation of Safety Management Systems (SMS) and adherence to the State Safety Programme 
(SSP) by airlines, aerodrome operators, Air Navigation Service Providers, and other key service providers. 
v. To evaluate the operational preparedness of airports nationwide, with a focus on critical areas such as runway 
safety, air traffic management infrastructure, and emergency response mechanisms. 
vi. To analyse the findings of recent safety audits, inspections, and accident investigations to ascertain the extent to 
which their recommendations have been effectively implemented. 
vii. To benchmark India’s regulatory framework and prevailing safety culture against those of leading global aviation 
regulators, such as the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) of the United States and the European Union 
Aviation Safety Agency (EASA), to identify global best practices that may be adopted. 
1.2.2 To achieve these objectives, the Committee pursued a rigorous, evidence -based methodology. This involved 
convening a formal sitting on 9 July 2025, where detailed presentations were heard from the Ministry of Civil Aviation, the 
DGCA, the AAI, and a cross-section of industry stakeholders including airlines and airport operators. This oral evidence was 
supplemented by an in-depth review of written submissions, statutory instruments, and international assessments, such as the 
ICAO Universal Safet y Oversight Audit Programme (USOAP) and the FAA’s International Aviation Safety Assessment 
(IASA) findings. A comparative analysis of the structures and practices of leading international regulators further enriched 
the Committee's perspective, informing a  nuanced and comprehensive assessment of India's safety framework is placed at 
Annexure I. 
2.0 Background of Civil Aviation Safety in India. 
2.1 Sector Expansion and Emerging Challenges. 
2.1.1 The growth of India's civil aviation sector has been nothing sh ort of remarkable, with passenger traffic soaring to 
239.4 million in the 2024 –25 period, cementing the nation's status as a global aviation powerhouse. This expansion, 
however, is not merely a story of numbers; it has fundamentally altered the operational  landscape, introducing new and 
multifaceted challenges that demand proactive and sophisticated safety management. The rapid increase in fleet size and the 
extension of air services to smaller Tier -II and Tier -III cities have placed immense strain on the e ntire ecosystem. Key 
challenges that have emerged include ensuring an adequate supply of qualified technical manpower to regulate and operate 
the expanding fleet, addressing significant infrastructural and operational constraints at newly activated regiona l airports, 
and ensuring the consistent and effective implementation of modern Safety Management Systems across a diverse range of 
operators and service providers. 
2.2  Statutory and Regulatory Framework Evolution. 
2.2.1 The legal foundation of India’s civil aviation safety regime has recently undergone a significant and necessary 
modernization. The legacy Aircraft Act of 1934 and its associated rules have been replaced by the Bharatiya Vayuyan 
Adhiniyam, 2024. This landma rk enactment serves to consolidate and update the legal framework governing civil aviation, 
aligning it with contemporary international standards and best practices. Crucially, the new Adhiniyam is designed not only 
to address current realities but also to  be forward-looking, providing enabling powers to accommodate future technological 
developments such as unmanned aerial systems (UAS), remotely piloted aircraft, and emerging navigation technologies. 
This new Act, in conjunction with the comprehensive body  of subordinate legislation and the detailed Civil Aviation 
Requirements (CARs) issued by the DGCA, constitutes the overarching statutory mandate for all aspects of safety oversight, 
including certification, licensing, surveillance, and enforcement.  
2.3  Organisational Structure for Safety Oversight. 
2.3.1 The architecture for aviation safety oversight in India is a multi -tiered structure, with distinct roles and 
responsibilities assigned to several key organisations  to ensure a system of checks and balances. At the apex is the Ministry 
of Civil Aviation, which formulates national policy and exercises overall supervision of the sector. The primary operational 

12 
regulator is the Directorate General of Civil Aviation (DGC A), which is responsible for the technical aspects of safety, 
including airworthiness standards, flight operations, and the licensing and certification of all personnel, aircraft, and 
operators. The DGCA’s Surveillance and Enforcement Division (SED) overse es safety aspects including air traffic control 
(ATC), communication, navigation, surveillance systems, and pre -flight medical evaluations, monitoring ground activities 
and aircraft movements for regulatory compliance and identifying areas for improvement.  This role is complemented by the 
Airports Authority of India (AAI), which manages the bulk of the nation's airports and provides critical air navigation 
services. Security, an element inextricably linked to safety, is overseen by the Bureau of Civil Aviat ion Security (BCAS). 
Finally, in the event of an accident or serious incident, the Aircraft Accident Investigation Bureau (AAIB) conducts 
independent investigations to determine probable cause and issue systemic safety recommendations to prevent recurrence.  
2.4  International Safety Standing and Audit Performance. 
2.4.1 India's commitment to maintaining a high standard of aviation safety is reflected in its performance in key 
international audits. As a contracting State to the Chicago Convention, India is obligated to establish and maintain safety 
oversight systems that align with the Standards and Recommended Practices (SARPs) set forth by the International Civil 
Aviation Organisation (ICAO). In the most recent ICAO Universal Safety Oversight Audit Program me (USOAP), India 
achieved an Effective Implementation score of 85.65%, a commendable figure that exceeds the global average and signifies 
a robust regulatory framework on paper. 
2.4.2 Furthermore, India has successfully retained its Category 1 status unde r the International Aviation Safety 
Assessment (IASA) program of the U.S. Federal Aviation Administration (FAA). This status is a critical enabler for Indian 
air carriers, permitting them to operate and expand their services to the United States without re striction and serving as a 
global benchmark of regulatory credibility. 
2.4.3 However, a central theme emerging from the Committee's review is the paradox that exists between these high -
level international validations and the evidence of significant systemi c weaknesses on the ground. While the high audit 
scores suggest that India's documented regulatory framework is well -aligned with international standards, the subsequent 
sections of this report detail profound challenges in implementation and capacity. Issues such as critical staff shortages, a 
large backlog of unresolved safety findings, and the recurrence of specific operational risks point to a dangerous gap 
between de jure  compliance and de facto safety reality. The high scores, while laudable, may refl ect past efforts and the 
strength of the documented system, but the internal data on deficiencies and resource constraints serves as a more potent 
leading indicator of future risk. This gap suggests that the system's resilience under the immense pressure o f rapid growth 
may be more fragile than the audit scores alone would indicate. 
2.5  Establishment of Safety Management and Reporting Systems. 
2.5.1 To manage safety in a proactive and data -driven manner, India has established a framework rooted in modern 
safety management principles. The cornerstone of this approach is the State Safety Programme (SSP), first instituted in 2010 
and comprehensively revised in 2021 to align with ICAO Annex 19. The SSP provides a structured methodology for the 
systematic identification, analysis, and mitigation of operational safety risks. It requires the establishment and maintenance 
of eight critical elements of a safety oversight system, covering everything from legislative provisions to the resolution of  
safety concerns.Flowing from the SSP is the National Aviation Safety Plan (NASP) for 2024 –2028. The NASP serves as the 
practical and time -bound roadmap for achieving the country's safety objectives. It sets out specific goals, timelines, and 
measurable Safety Performance Indi cators (SPIs) against which the health of the aviation system can be monitored. Key 
risks identified for monitoring under the NASP include mid -air collisions, controlled flight into terrain, runway excursions, 
and wildlife strikes, among others.  
2.5.2 This safety management framework is supported by a two -pronged reporting mechanism designed to capture 
critical safety data. The Mandatory Safety Reporting System (MSRS) requires operators to report any specified occurrence, 
such as a runway incursion or comp onent failure, to the DGCA within a stipulated timeframe. To supplement this, the 
Voluntary Safety Reporting System (VSRS), established as per Rule 29F of Aircraft Rules, 1937, provides a confidential and 
non-punitive channel for individuals to report safe ty concerns without fear of reprisal. All reported occurrences are 
scrutinised by an Occurrence Review Board (ORB), which convenes within 72 hours of a major incident to determine the 
appropriate investigative and follow-up actions, ensuring that safety data is translated into preventive measures. 
3.0  Key Areas for Systemic Improvement. 

13 
3.1  Enhancing Regulatory Autonomy and Capacity. 
3.1.1  Background: Critical technical staff shortages in the DGCA. 
3.1.1.1 The Directorate General of Civil Aviation, the nation's primary aviation safety regulator, is currently grappling 
with a profound and persistent shortage of technical and regulatory personnel. The data presented to the Committee reveals a 
stark reality: out of a total sanctioned strength of 1,063 posts , only 553 are currently filled. This represents a staggering 
shortfall of nearly 50% of the required manpower. This deficit is not a mere administrative statistic; it is a critical 
vulnerability that exists at the very heart of India's safety oversight sy stem, occurring precisely at a time when the sector's 
unprecedented growth demands more, not less, regulatory vigilance and capacity. While the Ministry has expressed an 
intention to increase the staffing level to approximately 850 within the next six mont hs, the current gap remains a matter of 
grave concern. 
3.1.2  History: Unimplemented recommendations from past expert committees. 
3.1.2.1 The critical need to empower the DGCA with greater autonomy is not a new discovery. For nearly two decades, 
this issue has been studied and highlighted by a series of high -level expert bodies. The Naresh Chandra Committee (2003), 
the Kaur Committee (2006), and, this  Committee in its 204th Report on the Creation of Civil Aviation of Authority of India 
Bill in the year 201 4 arrived at the same unanimous and unequivocal conclusion: to ensure the safety and efficiency of 
India’s aviation oversight, the DGCA must be granted statutory and administrative autonomy, with full control over its 
recruitment and administrative functio ns. The fact that these well -reasoned recommendations, repeatedly acknowledged by 
the Ministry, remain largely unimplemented points to a long -standing and systemic inertia in undertaking fundamental 
regulatory reform. 
3.1.3  Analysis: Ineffective recruitment via UPSC and deputation. 
3.1.3.1 The root of the DGCA's staffing crisis lies in a recruitment model that is not matching for a modern, specialized 
technical regulator. A significant number of vacant posts are pending recruitment through the Union Public  Service 
Commission (UPSC). The Ministry itself described this process as "slow and inflexible," resulting in unacceptable delays in 
filling critical regulatory positions that require niche expertise.  
3.1.3.2 The alternative channel, recruiting personnel on deputation, has proven equally ineffective, particularly for 
sourcing talent from the Indian Air Force. Experienced Air Force officers are unwilling to join the DGCA on deputation 
because doing so entails a significant loss of service benefits and allow ances, rendering the proposition financially 
unattractive. Consequently, posts reserved for this vital stream of expertise remain chronically unfilled. 
3.1.3.3 This leaves the DGCA in an unsustainable position. It currently lacks the independent authority to recruit its own 
personnel, to determine service conditions tailored to the specialized nature of the aviation sector, or to offer competitive , 
market-based remuneration. Unlike its international counterparts, the DGCA remains entirely dependent on  gove rnment 
allocations and procedural controls, making it impossible to attract and retain the highly skilled professionals —experienced 
pilots, airworthiness inspectors, and flight operations specialists —who are essential for conducting credible surveillance o f 
the private aviation industry. The Ministry on a specific query on the issue has submitted that there is no proposal under 
consideration for undertaking direct recruitment by DGCA. 
3.1.4  Interpretation: Lack of autonomy hinders the regulator's ability to attract talent and enforce compliance. 
3.1.4.1 The evidence leads to a conclusion that the DGCA, in its current form, is not in a position to discharge its duties 
for which it was established. It is tasked with overseeing a hyper -dynamic, technologically advanced, and rapidly expanding 
private industry, yet it is shackled by a slow -moving service recruitment structure followed by DGCA. Effective regulation 
demands that the regulator possesses expertise that is, at a minimum, on par with the industry it ov ersees. The private 
aviation sector offers highly competitive salaries and career paths for experienced professionals. The DGCA's current 
framework simply cannot compete with these market realities. 
3.1.4.2 This disparity creates a critical "competency gap ," where the regulator lacks both the quantity and, potentially, the 
requisite experience level of personnel needed to conduct rigorous and effective oversight. The staffing crisis is therefore not 
merely an administrative problem; it is an existential thr eat to the integrity of India's aviation safety system. This 
foundational weakness is a direct root cause of many of the other systemic issues detailed later in this report, including th e 

14 
alarming backlog in closing surveillance findings and a potential in ability to rigorously audit the influx of new aircraft, the 
commissioning of new airports, and the operations of new airlines. The lack of administrative and financial autonomy is the 
single greatest impediment to the DGCA's ability to fulfill its statutory mandate effectively. 
3.1.4.3 This critical deficit necessitates not only the ability to attract talent through competitive remuneration, but also a 
strategic national effort to cultivate a dedicated pipeline of regulatory professionals. Emphasizing domes tic manpower skill 
development within the safety network means proactively training and nurturing specialists in aviation law, airworthiness, 
flight operations, and surveillance methodologies from within the country to serve in critical public sector roles , ensuring 
that the regulator's expertise remains on par with the rapidly evolving industry it oversees 
3.1.5  Recommendations: A time-bound plan to grant the DGCA full administrative and financial autonomy. 
3.1.5.1 The Committee expresses its deep concern  over this persistent and critical issue. It is the considered view of the 
Committee that incremental solutions are no longer sufficient. A fundamental structural reform is urgently required. 
3.1.5.2 The Committee, therefore, recommends that the Ministry o f Civil Aviation develop a clear and time -bound plan to 
strengthen the human resource capacity of the DGCA. This plan must include the following key actions: 
i. Grant Full Autonomy: Immediately act upon the long -pending and unanimous recommendations of the Na resh 
Chandra and this Committee’s earlier recommendations contained in its 204 report on the Civil Aviation Authority 
of India Bill   to grant the DGCA full statutory, administrative, and financial autonomy.  The committee 
recommends that matters on which the government could override the authority of the proposed regulator/ 
regulatory body be clearly delineated in the rules and regulations framed subsequently 
ii. Establish a Specialized Recruitment Mechanism: Decouple DGCA recruitment from the framework of the  UPSC. 
A new, specialized recruitment body or mechanism must be established, with the flexibility to respond swiftly to 
the dynamic needs of the aviation sector. This mechanism should also explore dedicated training programmes and 
academies for regulatory professionals within India, potentially offering scholarships or assured placements to 
attract promising engineering, legal, and operational graduates directly into the DGCA's workforce, thereby 
building a sustainable domestic talent pool for the safety oversight function. 
iii. The Committee observed that the present system of recruitment to the Directorate General of Civil Aviation has 
scope for refinement in order to strengthen the organisation with enhanced professional and technical expertise. It 
is of the c onsidered view that the Ministry of Civil Aviation may explore the creation of a dedicated specialist 
cadre, drawing upon a Group ‘A’ service exclusively for the civil aviation sector. Such an initiative would, in the 
Committee’s assessment, help ensure th e sustained availability of officers with domain knowledge and long -term 
commitment to the sector, thereby augmenting the regulator’s capacity to effectively meet the evolving challenges 
of civil aviation oversight. Further, the Committee suggests a review  of existing remuneration structures and the 
introduction of a Civil Aviation Technical Allowance (CATA), in line with the National Civil Aviation Policy, 
2016, so as to make positions in the DGCA attractive for long-term careers. 
iv. Enable Market-Linked Remuneration: Grant the DGCA full financial authority to create posts and design salary 
and service structures that are commensurate with industry standards, enabling it to attract and retain top -tier talent 
directly from the aviation industry. 
v. Reform Deputati on Policy: Undertake a comprehensive review of the deputation policy, creating a revised 
framework that preserves the service benefits and allowances of officers from the armed forces and other 
government services, making deputation to the DGCA a viable and attractive career option. 
vi. Launch Focused Recruitment: Launch a focused recruitment campaign to ensure equitable staffing across all 
essential directorates, with priority given to those that are currently most underserved, such as Flight Operations, 
Airworthiness, and Cabin Safety. 
vii. Comprehensive Legislation: A new regulatory authority could be created, to replace the DGCA, by enacting a 
comprehensive legislation for India’s civil aviation industry by thoroughly analyzing the provisions of the 
Bharatiya Vayuyan Adhiniyam, 2024.  This, in the Committee’s view, is necessary to ensure that the proposed new 
regulatory body is able to achieve its objectives and avoid the shortcomings of the workings of the DGCA. 

15 
viii. The Nodal Authority/ Chairperson: The Committee is of the considered view that the apex authority of the 
proposed regulatory body/ authority may not be occupied by retired bureaucrats or secretary -level officers from 
MoCA, resulting in the undermining the independence of the regulatory authority, The committee suggests that 
such officers from MoCA may be appointed as ex-officio part-time members. 
ix. Technically-skilled Director General (DG) and Full -Time Members:  The Committee recommends that the 
proposed regulator be headed and accompanied by a pre -determined quorum of full -time members strictly from a 
technical background within the aviation sector.

3.2  Mitigating Air Traffic Controller Fatigue and Staffing Deficits. 
3.2.1  Background: Persistent ATCO shortages and high workload. 
3.2.1.1 The safety of Indian airspace is critically dependent on the vigilance and performance of its Air Traffic Controllers 
(ATCOs). However, evidence presented to th e Committee indicates that this vital human element of the system is under 
immense and sustained pressure. The Airports Authority of India (AAI), which provides nationwide air navigation services, 
acknowledged that the workload for ATCOs has increased cons iderably, driven by both the sheer volume of traffic and the 
growing complexity of airspace management. This pressure is most acute at the nation's busiest airports, such as Delhi, 
Mumbai, Bengaluru, and Hyderabad, where many controllers are reportedly ope rating under prolonged and fatiguing duty 
schedules.  
3.2.1.2 The AAI states that the deployment of ATCOs is undertaken based on the DGCA’s Civil Aviation Requirements 
(CAR) on Watch Duty Time Limitations (WDTL), which are aligned with international norms,  and considers air traffic 
volume to ensure safe and efficient air traffic management. They also maintain that aviation safety is achieved through 
optimal utilization of available manpower. 
3.2.1.3 Air Traffic Controllers’ Guild (India), the professional b ody representing licensed ATCOs apprised the Committee 
of a "persistent and serious shortage" of controllers at numerous airports. This deficit is so severe that it has reportedly led to 
the closure or merging of critical operational units, such as approach and area control sectors, particularly during night hours. 
This practice places an undue workload and immense mental strain on the limited number of ATCOs available to manage the 
traffic. 
3.2.2  History: Long-standing reliance on exemptions from Watch Duty Time Limitation (WDTL) regulations. 
3.2.2.1 The Directorate General of Civil Aviation has established a Civil Aviation Requirement (CAR) that specifies the 
Watch Duty Time Limitations (WDTL) for ATCOs. These regulations are not arbitrary; they are a cri tical safety barrier 
designed to manage fatigue and ensure that controllers remain alert and effective while on duty. However, the ATC Guild 
informed the Committee that due to the persistent staff shortages, the AAI has found it difficult to comply with th ese 
mandatory safety regulations. Instead of rectifying the underlying staff deficit, the AAI has, for many years, resorted to 
seeking and obtaining exemptions from the DGCA to operate outside the prescribed WDTL norms at numerous airports 
across the country. This practice has effectively institutionalized overwork and normalized a state of heightened fatigue risk 
within the air traffic management system. 
3.2.3  Analysis: Recruitment and training capacity mismatch. 
3.2.3.1 The staffing deficit is the result  of a multi -faceted failure in workforce planning. The AAI conceded that the 
recruitment of qualified controllers has not kept pace with the rapid growth in air traffic. Even when recruitment does occur , 
significant bottlenecks in the training pipeline exa cerbate the problem. The Committee was informed that the existing ATC 
training centres have a limited capacity, able to accommodate only 210 officers at a time. Consequently, when a large batch 
of, for instance, 400 ATCOs is recruited, nearly half of them are posted to airports without having undergone the requisite 
basic ATC training. These untrained officers then remain in non-operational roles for extended periods, waiting for a training 
slot to become available. This represents a gross inefficiency and a significant delay in deploying desperately needed 
personnel to operational duties. This inefficiency underscores a fundamental flaw in the long -term domestic manpower skill 
development strategy for ATCOs. The existing training capacity, limited to 210 of ficers at a time, is severely inadequate for 

16 
the nation's rapid aviation growth. A robust safety network demands not just recruitment, but a seamless and expanded 
training ecosystem that ensures the timely deployment of fully skilled controllers. 
3.2.3.2 The ATC Guild further submitted that the AAI lacks a structured and scientific manpower planning mechanism for 
this safety -critical cadre. This absence of long -term planning has led to situations where key supervisory roles —such as 
Tower Control Supervisor and Approach Control Supervisor —remain unstaffed, adversely affecting oversight and 
coordination. In some instances, controllers are forced to perform dual roles, simultaneously managing active traffic and 
fulfilling supervisory duties, a practice that con travenes safety guidelines and compromises the ability to respond effectively 
in an emergency. 
3.2.4  Interpretation: Operational overload poses a direct risk to airspace safety and controller well-being. 
3.2.4.1 The evidence points to a deeply troubling practice within India's air traffic management system: the normalization 
of deviance. The system is knowingly and consistently operating outside its own mandated safety limits (WDTL) through 
the routine use of exemptions. This is a classic organizational f ailure, where a known and significant risk —controller 
fatigue—is accepted as a normal part of operations due to persistent operational and resource pressures. 
3.2.4.2 The WDTL regulations exist for a clear, scientific reason: to prevent fatigue, which is u niversally recognized as a 
major contributing factor to human error in safety -critical professions. The act of seeking exemptions is a formal admission 
by the service provider (AAI) that it cannot function without violating its own safety rules. The act of  granting these 
exemptions year after year suggests that the regulator (DGCA) has been complicit in institutionalizing this high-risk practice. 
3.2.4.3 This chronic fatigue directly and significantly increases the probability of controller error. Such an e rror could 
manifest in various dangerous ways, including a loss of standard separation between aircraft, a runway incursion, a delayed 
or incorrect response to an emergency, or a complete breakdown in situational awareness. The ATCO shortage is, therefore,  
not just a staffing or administrative issue; it is an active and ongoing threat to the safety of the flying public. The relia nce on 
exemptions has created a fragile system where the safety of the entire airspace depends not on robust, systemic safeguards,  
but on the over-stretched cognitive endurance of a few hundred individuals. 
3.2.5  Recommendations: Immediate development of a fatigue risk management system and a comprehensive staffing 
audit. 
3.2.5.1 The Committee finds the current situation regarding A TCO staffing and fatigue to be unacceptable and to pose a 
clear and present risk to aviation safety. Addressing this requires immediate and decisive action. 
3.2.5.2 The Committee recommends the following: 
i. Cease WDTL Exemptions and Implement FRMS: The pract ice of granting routine exemptions from WDTL 
regulations must cease immediately. In its place, the Ministry must direct the AAI, in consultation with the DGCA, 
to develop and implement a modern, scientifically -based Fatigue Risk Management System (FRMS) fo r ATCOs, 
aligned with ICAO and EASA best practices. This system should include enforceable standards on work hours, 
rest periods, and roster design. 
ii. Conduct Comprehensive Staffing Audit: The Ministry must direct the AAI to undertake a comprehensive, airpor t-
wise staffing audit to scientifically assess ATCO requirements based on current and projected traffic levels, 
airspace complexity, and operational hours. This audit must form the basis for an urgent, time -bound recruitment 
plan to eliminate the existing deficit. 
iii. Expand Training Capacity: The Ministry must oversee a plan to significantly expand the capacity of ATC training 
centres to match recruitment levels. This should include exploring the establishment of a new training facility to 
clear the existing b acklog and ensure the timely training and deployment of all newly recruited ATCOs. This 
expansion must be accompanied by a future -oriented skill development roadmap for ATCOs, incorporating 
advanced simulation technologies and specialised training modules for complex airspace management, thereby 
ensuring that newly trained personnel are equipped for the increasing complexity of Indian airspace. 

17 
iv. Ensure Supervisory Oversight: The AAI must ensure that all designated supervisory positions in ATC units are 
staffed at all times and that the practice of controllers performing dual operational and supervisory roles is 
eliminated. 
3.3  Strengthening Surveillance and Enforcement Mechanisms. 
3.3.1  Background: A significant backlog of unresolved safety deficiencies. 
3.3.1.1 The DGCA's Annual Surveillance Programme (ASP) represents a cornerstone of its safety oversight function, 
designed to proactively identify and rectify non -compliance among operators and service providers. The scale of this effort 
is substantial; in the year 2024, a total of 4,027 surveillance exercises had been targeted, of which an impressive 3,995 were 
successfully conducted. These activities unearthed 12,683 deficiencies, comprising 81 serious Level I findings and 12,602 
Level II issues. 
3.3.1.2 However, the data also reveals a critical weakness in the system. While a large number of deficiencies were 
reported as corrected, a significant backlog of 961 unresolved Level II deficiencies remained pending at the close of 2024. 
This problem appears to have worsened dramatically in the subsequent year. For 2025, up to the month of April, surveillance 
activities had already detected 4,692 deficiencies. Of these, only 945 had been rectified, leaving a staggering 3,747 
deficiencies pending. Further, this backlog included 37 unresolved Level I deficiencies, which represent serious safety lapses 
requiring immediate attention. 
3.3.2  History: High volume of surveillance activities with lagging rectification. 
3.3.2.1 The data presented to the Committee reveals a consistent and troubling pattern over time. The DGCA demonstrates 
a high degree of effectiveness in the detection phase of surveillance. Achieving a completion rate of over 99% of targeted 
surveillance exercises in 2024 reflects a commendable operational effort. However, this strength in detection is starkly 
contrasted by a pronounced weakness in the rectification phase. The closure rate of identified findings is lagging 
significantly, leading to an accumulation of unresolved safety issues within the system. 
3.3.3  Analysis: Weakness in post-surveillance closure, particularly in Airworthiness and Aerodrome Standards. 
3.3.3.1 The backlog of unresolved deficiencies is not evenly distributed; it is heavily concentrated in directorates that are 
most critical to  operational safety. The Directorate of Airworthiness, responsible for ensuring the mechanical fitness of 
aircraft, carried a burden of 1,294 pending Level II and 10 unresolved Level I deficiencies as of April 2025. Similarly, the 
Aerodrome Standards Directorate had 764 pending Level II deficiencies, and the Flight Standards Directorate had 462. The 
presence of any unresolved Level I deficiency is a matter of grave concern, as these issues could have a direct and immediate 
impact on the safety of flight operations. The large and growing backlog in these key directorates points to a systemic failure 
in the post-surveillance follow-up and enforcement process. 
3.3.3.2 A prominent concern regarding safety has emerged regarding lapses in the process of safety aud it. Audit report 
submissions made by flight operators, such as "Action to be Taken on Investigation Report," could be susceptible to 
compromise in the absence of qualified technical staff from DGCA to double-check the audit responses. Furthermore, airline 
operators often lack experienced personnel specializing in the different aircraft types in their fleet, leading to situations  
where staff with maintenance experience of a particular aircraft type is delegated the responsibility of auditing another typ e 
without sufficient familiarization training, resulting in a cursory rather than detailed examination of the aircraft. What has 
also come to light is that in the trade -off between grounding an aircraft for maintenance and scheduling it for passenger 
journeys, airlines prioritize profiteering over safety concerns —even when an obvious maintenance flaw comes to light, the 
flight is not immediately grounded, rather the aircraft continues to be in operation for some more passenger flights before i t 
is grounded for maintenance purposes. 
3.3.3.3 This identified gap in specialized personnel within airline operators points to an urgent need for enhanced 
domestic manpower skill development within the operational entities themselves. Airlines must be incentivized and 
mandated to invest in comprehensive, aircraft -type-specific training and cross -familiarization programmes for their 
maintenance, audit, and quality assurance staff. Relying on inadequately trained personnel for critical safety audits and 
maintenance oversight f undamentally compromises the integrity of the safety network, potentially allowing 'profiteering 
over safety concerns' to persist. 

18 
3.3.4  Interpretation: The gap between deficiency detection and correction undermines the surveillance program's 
effectiveness. 
3.3.4.1 The evidence leads to the conclusion that India's aviation surveillance system, while robust in its ability to find 
problems, currently lacks the skill to ensure those problems are fixed. The primary purpose of surveillance is not merely to 
identify and document risks, but to ensure their timely mitigation. A large and growing backlog of open deficiencies, 
especially serious Level I findings in critical areas like airworthiness, indicates that this crucial mitigation step is failing. 
3.3.4.2 This failure can be attributed to a confluence of factors, all of which are interconnected. It may stem from an 
insufficient number of DGCA technical staff to effectively track and enforce the closure of thousands of findings, a problem 
directly linked to the human resource crisis. It could also reflect a lack of institutional will to impose stringent penalties, or a 
prevailing culture where operators and service providers can delay compliance without facing significant or timely 
consequences.  
3.3.4.3 In its current state, the surveillance program risks becoming a performative exercise. It is generating a vast and 
detailed record of the system's flaws without effectively compelling the system to improve. This creates a false sense of 
security, where the "ac tivity" of conducting thousands of audits is mistaken for the "outcome" of enhanced safety. The 
unclosed findings represent known risks that continue to exist within the operational environment. 
3.3.5  Recommendations: A time -bound mechanism for deficiency  closure and stronger enforcement actions, including 
financial penalties. 
3.3.5.1 The Committee is constrained to observe that while the detection of non -compliance is robust, the subsequent 
closure of these deficiencies is unacceptably slow. This systemic  weakness must be addressed with urgency. The findings of 
audits, surveillance, and spot checks should be followed up with the concerned operator for compliance, and observations 
closed after verification, with compliance verified during the next audit/sur veillance. In cases of violations or non -
compliance, enforcement action, including financial penalties, is imposed by the DGCA. 
3.3.5.2 The Committee recommends the following: 
i. Implement a Time-Bound Closure Mechanism: The Ministry must ensure the establishment of a strict, time-bound 
mechanism for the closure of all identified deficiencies. Level I matters must be accorded the highest urgency, 
with a mandated rectification and closure timeframe that is non -negotiable (e.g., 72 hours). Progress on t he 
rectification of all deficiencies should be monitored on a monthly basis at the highest level within the DGCA. 
ii. The Committee further recommends that investigations into structural safety incidents at airports must be 
completed within fixed timelines to avoid undue delays. Strict timelines for final report submissions may be 
introduced. A detailed time frame may be established for conducting all the investigations into safety issues at the 
airports. The Ministry should also ensure that findings of expert committees and third ‑party structural stability 
audits are released in a time‑bound manner to reinforce accountability and strengthen safety oversight 
iii. Strengthen Enforcement Measures: The DGCA must significantly strengthen its enforcement policy and 
procedures. Persistent or repeated non -compliance should not merely be met with reminders or advisories. The 
system must move towards a model of escalating enforcement, where such failures automatically trigger 
meaningful consequences, including substantial fina ncial penalties, the suspension of approvals, or, where 
warranted, the cancellation of authorizations. 
iv. Dedicate Resources to Clear Backlogs: The directorates with the heaviest burden of pending deficiencies —namely 
Aerodrome Standards, Airworthiness, and Fl ight Standards —should be provided with dedicated resources and 
tasked with a special drive to clear the accumulated backlog within a defined period. 
v. Commission an Independent Audit: The Ministry should commission an independent audit to evaluate the 
effectiveness of the DGCA’s entire enforcement process, from finding to closure. This audit should identify 
structural impediments and recommend improvements to ensure the Annual Surveillance Programme translates 
into tangible and sustained safety improvements. 
3.4   Standardizing Safety Protocols for Helicopter Operations. 

19 
3.4.1  Background: A series of accidents during high-risk Char Dham Yatra operations. 
3.4.1.1 The unique operational challenges of helicopter flights in India's mountainous regions were starkl y highlighted by 
a series of four accidents involving helicopters during the most recent Char Dham Yatra in 2025. These incidents occurred in 
a high -risk operational environment characterized by challenging topography, rapidly changing and often severe cli matic 
conditions, and high -density traffic. The Ministry acknowledged that these operations carry inherent risks that warrant 
immediate and serious intervention. 
3.4.2  History: State-level agencies managing critical operations with limited central oversight. 
3.4.2.1 A critical factor in this safety equation is the governance structure for these operations. The Committee was 
informed that helicopter services for the Char Dham Yatra are managed and operated not directly under the aegis of a 
national body, bu t by the Uttarakhand Civil Aviation Development Authority (UCADA), a State -level agency. The DGCA 
clarified its role as being primarily that of a licensing authority, which has issued safety guidelines to UCADA but does not  
exercise direct, proactive, and continuous supervision over the day -to-day operations. This arrangement raises fundamental 
questions about regulatory uniformity, the consistent application of national safety protocols, and ultimate systemic 
accountability when incidents occur. 
3.4.2.2 The Ministry of Civil Aviation (MoCA) is responsible for formulating national policies and programmes for the 
civil aviation sector, including helicopter regulation, and the DGCA is the regulatory body dealing with safety, airworthines s 
standards, and enforc ement for helicopters. The role of State Governments regarding helicopter management is mainly 
limited to infrastructure management of State Helipads and providing basic amenities at state -owned helipads. For the Char 
Dham sector, UCADA, a State Government  body, performs additional functions such as awareness and public information, 
selection of shuttle operators, flight scheduling and slot management, and fare regulation and monitoring. The DGCA has 
issued Ops Circular 02/2023 for “Conduct of Helicopter Pi lgrimage Operations” specifically addressing safety aspects for 
such operations. Furthermore, additional safety measures have been advised for short -term, medium -term, and long -term 
implementation for the Char Dham Yatra (Hilly Area Operation) by DGCA and the Government of Uttarakhand, including 
UCADA. 
3.4.3  Analysis: Reactive safety measures and lack of mandatory mountain-flying training. 
3.4.3.1 In the aftermath of the accidents, the DGCA instituted a series of measures. These included issuing an enhanced 
safety checklist to UCADA, conducting special audits of all Char Dham operators, suspending the operations of two service 
providers with poor sa fety records, and placing restrictions on flight density at the Kedarnath helipad. However, the 
Committee observed that these measures, while timely, were largely reactive, emerging only as a response to the accidents 
that had already occurred. Such an app roach does not reflect the proactive and anticipatory safety culture that ought to 
characterize aviation oversight in a known high-risk environment. 
3.4.3.2 Furthermore, the Committee noted a glaring regulatory gap of significant concern: the absence of an y mandatory, 
terrain-specific mountain-flying training and certification for pilots who operate in the treacherous Himalayan terrain. The 
DGCA itself expressed concern that UCADA lacked adequately trained and competent manpower for such high -density 
operations, yet a specific training mandate for these unique conditions was not in place. 
3.4.4  Interpretation: Regulatory ambiguity between central and state authorities creates safety gaps. 
3.4.4.1 The situation with helicopter operations in the Char Dham sec tor is a symptom of a larger and more dangerous 
structural flaw in India's aviation governance: a fractured and ambiguous regulatory command structure. There exists a clear 
jurisdictional ambiguity where the DGCA, as the apex national regulator, appears to  have ceded direct oversight of one of 
the country's most hazardous aviation activities to a state -level body whose capacity and expertise have been called into 
question. 
3.4.4.2 Aviation safety, by its very nature, demands uniform, high standards that are applied consistently, regardless of the 
operator, the region, or the nature of the operation. Allowing state -level agencies to manage complex commercial air 
services creates a patchwork of regulatory oversight, where national standards may be diluted or i nconsistently enforced. 
The DGCA's position of being a passive licensing authority in this context represents an abdication of its ultimate 
responsibility for ensuring the safety of all civil aviation activities within the country. This ambiguity creates a  
"responsibility vacuum," making it unclear who is ultimately accountable for safety —the state agency for the conduct of 

20 
operations, or the DGCA for the quality of oversight. This two -tiered safety system, with differing levels of scrutiny, is 
inherently unsafe and unsustainable. 
3.4.5  Recommendations: A uniform regulatory framework for all state -operated helicopter services and mandatory 
terrain-specific pilot training 
3.4.5.1 The Committee is not satisfied with the current regulatory arrangement and believes that the DGCA must play a 
more proactive and assertive supervisory role. To address these systemic gaps, the Committee recommends the following: 
i. Establish a Uniform National Framework: The Ministry of Civil Aviation, in collaboration with the DGCA , must 
develop and enforce a uniform regulatory framework applicable to all state -operated helicopter and other air 
services. This framework must ensure that such operations are subject to the same rigorous central oversight, 
safety protocols, audits, and crew competency standards as any other commercial air transport operator in the 
country. 
ii. Mandate Terrain-Specific Pilot Training: Recognizing the unique challenges of Himalayan flying, the DGCA must 
make terrain -specific training and certification a mandat ory prerequisite for any pilot to be granted operational 
authorization in mountainous regions like Char Dham. This should include training in high -altitude operations, 
weather interpretation, and emergency procedures specific to the terrain. 
iii. Create a Dedic ated Oversight Cell: The DGCA should constitute a permanent, specialized cell dedicated to the 
monitoring of high-altitude and other specialized helicopter operations nationwide. This unit should operate year -
round to provide a standing mechanism for oversight, data analysis, risk assessment, and continuous improvement, 
rather than reacting only when incidents occur. 
iv. Institute a Robust Certification Mechanism: A robust certification and accreditation system must be instituted for 
all state aviation agencies that wish to operate air services. This should involve periodic and independent audits of 
their technical, operational, and managerial preparedness, with a transparent and accountable system for enforcing 
compliance. 
3.5   Addressing Recurring Operational Risks. 
3.5.1 Background: Runway incursions by aircraft and loss of situational awareness events 
exceeding safety targets. 
3.5.1.1 An analysis of the Safety Performance Indicators (SPIs) for 2024 reveals that while the system is managing certain 
risks effectively, others remain stubbornly persistent and above acceptable tolerance levels. Specifically, runway incursions 
by aircraft were recorded at a rate of 14.12 per million movements, significantly exceeding the established target of 9.78. 
Similarly, incursions attributed to a loss of situational awareness stood at 5.91 per million movements, against a target of 
4.54. These are high -consequence events that pose a direct risk of collision on the ground. The fact that other indicators, 
such as those for incur sions by vehicles or persons and general ramp safety, were within their targets indicates that the 
problem is highly specific to the complex interaction between aircraft, pilots, and air traffic controllers in the runway 
environment. 
3.5.1.2 Regarding other recurring operational risks, the number of confirmed bird strikes was 1278 in 2024 and 511 in 
2025 (till June). The number of engine failure incidents was 21 in 2024 and 4 in 2025 (till June). To mitigate wildlife 
hazards, DGCA has mandated requirements for developing Wildlife Hazard Management Plans (WHMP) and procedures, 
issued regulations for management at licensed airports, and Rule 91 of The Aircraft Rule 1937 prohibits dumping of garbage 
and slaughter of animals within 10km of Aerodrome Reference Po int. Aerodrome Advisory Circular AD AC 06 of 2017 
mandates aerodrome operators to implement effective wildlife control, and AD AC 01 of 2022 identifies gaps and ensures 
strict implementation of hazard management plans. Procedures are regularly examined dur ing DGCA surveillance 
inspections. Measures include coordination with civil authorities for clean surrounding areas, proper waste management, 
regular inspections, conduct of Aerodrome Environment Management Committees (AEMC), engaging specialized agencies 
for wildlife study, maintenance of operational areas, spreading awareness, and deploying bird and animal scaring devices. 
3.5.2  History: Persistent incidents despite a dedicated Occurrence Review Board. 

21 
3.5.2.1 The DGCA has an established institutional mechanism to address such issues: the Occurrence Review Board 
(ORB). This board convenes with remarkable frequency—holding 103 meetings in 2024 to review over 7,500 occurrences—
and is mandated to examine every reported safety occurrence within 72 hours of its receipt. The ORB’s purpose is to 
determine the mode of investigation and direct follow -up actions. However, despite this high level of activity and timely 
review, the continued recurrence of specific incident types, particularly runway incursions and A IRPROX (near mid -air 
collision) events, strongly suggests a failure to translate the analysis from these reviews into effective, lasting prevention. 
3.5.3  Analysis: Lessons from incident reviews are not translating into operational practice changes. 
3.5.3.1 The Committee observes that while the ORB’s deliberations are prompt, the "follow -through in terms of 
implementing and enforcing corrective measures is not clearly visible". The data on AIRPROX reports, which also exceeded 
the acceptable benchmark in 20 24, further reinforces the conclusion that systemic issues related to airspace management, 
standard operating procedures, and pilot-controller communication are not being adequately resolved. The cycle of reporting 
and reviewing incidents appears to be fun ctioning, but the crucial final step —learning and implementing change to prevent 
recurrence—is weak. 
3.5.4  Interpretation: High -consequence risks recur due to inadequate root -cause analysis and implementation of 
corrective actions. 
3.5.4.1 The evidence suggests that the system's "review and report" safety cycle is incomplete. The system is excellent at 
collecting data on what went wrong and reviewing it quickly. However, it appears to be poor at understanding why it went 
wrong and ensuring it does not happ en again. The persistence of high runway incursion and AIRPROX rates indicates that 
the mitigation and prevention phases of the safety management cycle are broken. 
3.5.4.2 This points to a potentially superficial level of analysis within the current proces s. The system may be addressing 
the immediate, apparent cause of an incident (e.g., classifying it as "pilot error" or "controller error") without conducting  a 
deep, systemic root -cause analysis. Without a deeper level of inquiry, the ORB functions more as  a data collection and 
classification body than as a powerful agent for systemic change. It is destined to review an endless stream of similar, 
recurring incidents because the underlying conditions that create them remain unaddressed. The problem is not a lack of data 
on incidents, but a lack of deep analysis and enforced, systemic solutions. 
3.5.5  Recommendations: A detailed root-cause analysis for each incursion and focused remedial programs for areas with 
repeated occurrences. 
3.5.5.1 To break this cycl e of recurrence, the Committee recommends a shift from a reactive review process to a 
proactive, analytical safety improvement model. 
3.5.5.2 The Committee recommends the following: 
i. Mandate In-Depth Root-Cause Analysis: The DGCA must mandate that a formal,  in-depth root-cause analysis be 
conducted for every runway incursion and AIRPROX event. This analysis must look beyond individual human 
error to identify and address the contributing systemic factors, such as procedures, training, technology, and 
organizational culture. 
ii. Establish Focused Remedial Programmes: For areas with a high rate of recurring incidents, such as runway 
incursions, the Ministry should direct the DGCA and AAI to establish focused, time -bound remedial programmes. 
These programmes should h ave clear, measurable targets for improvement and should be required to report on 
their progress periodically to Parliament.  DGCA must work towards installation of fog navigation systems and 
Instrument Landing System at all functional airports to avoid frequency of accidents. 
iii. Strengthen the ORB's Mandate: The mandate of the Occurrence Review Board should be strengthened to include 
not just reviewing incidents, but also ensuring that its recommendations are tracked to completion. All directives 
issued by the ORB must be accompanied by mandatory implementation timelines and a robust tracking system to 
monitor and enforce compliance by all operators and service providers. 
iv. Disseminate Lessons Learned: The findings from these analyses and the resulting safety directives must be 
communicated in a clear, accessible, and timely manner to all stakeholders, including smaller operators and

regional aerodrome authorities, to ensure that operational improvements are effected promptly across the entire 
system. 
3.6   Establishing a "Just Culture" and Whistleblower Protection. 
3.6.1  Background: Imposition of financial penalties on individual ATCOs. 
3.6.1.1 A significant concern was brought to the Committee's attention by the Air Traffic Controllers’ Guild (India) 
regarding the regulatory practice of imposing heavy financial penalties on individual ATCOs. The Guild reported that 
controllers can be exposed to penalties of up to ₹25 lakh for operational decisions made under the immense pressure of a 
real-time, dynamic environment, even when such decisions are system-driven or are the result of a collective team action. 
3.6.2 History: A regulatory approach perceived as punitive rather than focused on systemic learning. 
3.6.2.1 The ATC Guild argued, and the Committee concurs,  that this punitive approach runs directly counter to the 
internationally endorsed principle of a "Just Culture". A Just Culture, as promoted by ICAO and leading aviation authorities 
worldwide, is a cornerstone of modern safety management. It seeks to crea te an environment where professionals are 
encouraged and feel safe to report their own errors and near -misses without fear of punishment. This approach differentiates 
between honest mistakes, negligent behaviour, and willful misconduct. By contrast, a pure ly punitive culture fosters fear and 
encourages the concealment of errors, which is detrimental to safety. 
3.6.3  Analysis: Limited clarity on protections for employees reporting safety violations. 
3.6.3.1 This issue extends beyond penalties to the broader framework for safety reporting. The Committee notes that while 
mechanisms for confidential and voluntary safety reporting exist, there is "limited clarity on the extent to which these 
provisions guarantee anonymity, ensure follow -up action, and prevent re prisals" against the person making the report. This 
ambiguity creates a significant trust deficit. The DGCA has established a Voluntary Safety Reporting System (VSRS) under 
Rule 29F of Aircraft Rules, 1937, to collect safety data and information on actual or potential safety deficiencies, which is 
stated to be non -punitive and confidential. However, the Committee's concern remains about the perceived clarity and 
effectiveness of these protections to encourage reporting without fear of reprisal. If employees —be they pilots, controllers, 
engineers, or cabin crew—are not confident that they will be protected from professional harm or victimization, they will be 
understandably hesitant to come forward with critical safety information. 
3.6.4 Interpretation: Fear of reprisal and penalties may deter reporting, undermining safety oversight. 
3.6.4.1 The current regulatory environment appears to be sending a fundamentally contradictory message. On one hand, it 
asks for voluntary and mandatory reporting to feed its safe ty management systems with data. On the other hand, it maintains 
a punitive approach that threatens individuals with severe financial penalties for the very errors it seeks to learn from. Th is 
contradiction is not merely a philosophical issue; it has profound, practical consequences for safety. 
3.6.4.2 Aviation safety is built upon the free and open flow of information about errors, mistakes, and latent risks. If 
operational personnel fear that reporting an error will lead to personal blame and punishment, the natural human instinct is to 
hide it. When errors are hidden, the organization and the regulator lose the invaluable opportunity to learn from them. The 
underlying systemic flaw that contributed to the error —be it a faulty procedure, a training deficie ncy, or a design flaw —
remains unaddressed, latent within the system, waiting to contribute to a future, and potentially catastrophic, accident. A 
punitive culture is, therefore, an unsafe culture because it actively suppresses the flow of the most critical  safety information 
available. Establishing a Just Culture is not about being "soft" on accountability; it is a highly sophisticated safety strat egy 
designed to maximize the capture of safety intelligence from the front lines. 
3.6.5  Recommendations: Revie w penal provisions applicable to ATCOs and institute a comprehensive whistleblower 
protection framework. 
3.6.5.1 To promote an environment of trust and open reporting, which is essential for proactive safety management, the 
Committee believes a fundamental shift in regulatory philosophy is required. 
3.6.5.2 The Committee recommends the following: 

23 
i. Review and Align Penal Provisions with Just Culture: The Ministry of Civil Aviation, in consultation with the 
DGCA, must undertake a comprehensive review of the ex isting penal provisions applicable to operational 
personnel like ATCOs and pilots. This framework must be revised to align with Just Culture principles, 
establishing a clear and transparent distinction between inadvertent human error (which should be addre ssed 
through training and system improvement), at -risk behaviour, and reckless or willful misconduct (which may 
warrant punitive action). The emphasis must shift from blame to learning. 
ii. Institute a Comprehensive Whistleblower Protection Framework: The Mini stry must examine the feasibility of 
instituting a robust, legally -backed whistleblower protection framework applicable to all aviation personnel. This 
framework is essential to build trust and must include: 
a. A secure, fully anonymous, and independent reporting channel, managed outside the direct control of the 
employee's organization. 
b. A well-defined and transparent process for the investigation of all reported concerns and for providing 
feedback to the whistleblower on the actions taken. 
c. Strong statutory sa feguards that explicitly prohibit any form of retaliation, professional harm, or 
victimization against employees who report safety concerns in good faith. 
iii. Promote and Publicize Protections: The existence of these Just Culture policies and whistleblower protections 
must be widely publicized and actively promoted throughout the industry. Visible enforcement of these protections 
is crucial to building the trust necessary to encourage proactive reporting. 
3.7  Developing Domestic MRO Capabilities. 
3.7.1  Background: A significant portion of MRO work for Indian carriers is outsourced 
abroad. 
3.7.1.1 The Committee has observed a significant and concerning trend within the Indian aviation ecosystem: a 
considerable proportion of the essential Maintenance, Repa ir, and Overhaul (MRO) work for aircraft operated by Indian 
carriers is presently undertaken at facilities located abroad. As per industry estimates, around 85% of MRO activities are 
done abroad in Centres certified by Original Equipment Manufacturers (OEM s). This outsourcing is common for heavy 
maintenance checks and major component overhauls, with airlines frequently sending their aircraft to regional aviation hubs 
in other countries for these critical services. 
3.7.2  History: Domestic MRO companies face challenges in securing business from local airlines. 
3.7.2.1 This reliance on foreign facilities exists despite the presence of several MRO companies within India. These 
domestic entities face significant challenges in securing adequate and consistent bus iness from local airlines, leading to the 
potential underutilization of indigenous technical expertise, infrastructure, and investment. This situation raises pressing 
questions regarding the cost competitiveness of Indian MROs, the substantial outflow of f oreign exchange, and the 
operational inefficiencies associated with longer aircraft turnaround times. 
3.7.3 Analysis: Constraints include high taxation on imported parts and infrastructure limitations. 
3.7.3.1 The Committee's examination reveals that the g rowth of a vibrant domestic MRO sector is constrained by a 
number of structural and policy -related factors. Key among these are a high taxation regime on imported spare parts and 
components, which inflates the cost of domestic MRO services compared to fore ign competitors. Additionally, complex 
customs procedures, limitations in airport infrastructure dedicated to MRO activities, and the stringent and time -consuming 
processes for obtaining advanced technical certifications from international bodies further i mpede the competitiveness and 
expansion of the domestic MRO industry. If left unaddressed, these constraints will perpetuate India's dependence on 
overseas facilities. 
3.7.3.2 The Government has taken a number of key initiatives to create a conducive envir onment for the development of 
the aircraft MRO sector and to bring business into India. These include:

3.7.4  Interpretation: Dependence on overseas facilities leads to foreign exchange outflow and underutilization of 
domestic capacity. 
3.7.4.1 The outsourcing of MRO services should not be viewed merely as a series of independent business decisions made 
by airlines based on cost. It is a national issue with significant strategic and economic vulnerabilities for India. The economic 
impact is clear and direct: it results in a s ubstantial and continuous outflow of foreign exchange that could otherwise be 
retained and reinvested within the Indian economy. India has the potential to become a global MRO hub due to its increasing 
fleet size, geographical location, and comparatively lower labour costs. 
3.7.4.2 Operationally, this dependency can lead to longer turnaround times for aircraft undergoing heavy maintenance, 
which directly affects fleet availability and an airline's operational efficiency. Strategically, it creates a critical  dependency 
on other nations for a fundamental aspect of aviation safety and operational readiness. This dependency could be disrupted 
or exploited during times of geopolitical instability or supply chain shocks, leaving Indian carriers vulnerable. 
3.7.4.3 Most importantly, this trend stifles the growth of a high -skilled, technologically advanced domestic MRO 
ecosystem. It limits the creation of quality jobs for engineers and technicians and hinders the transfer of cutting -edge 
maintenance technologies to I ndia. Building a robust domestic MRO industry is therefore not just a matter of import 
substitution; it is a critical component of achieving national aviation self -reliance and security. It is as fundamental to the 
nation's aviation infrastructure as building new airports and training new pilots. 
3.7.4.4 To truly leverage India's potential to become a global MRO hub, given its increasing fleet size, geographical 
location, and comparatively lower labour costs, there must be proactive, government -led, and ind ustry-partnered initiatives 
focused on cultivating a robust pipeline of highly skilled domestic manpower. This includes a strategic emphasis on 
specialised certifications, advanced training in cutting -edge maintenance technologies, and continuous upskillin g 
programmes for aviation engineers and technicians. Such a focus would not only curtail significant foreign exchange outflow 
but also strategically position India as a key player in the global MRO value chain, fostering true self-reliance. 
3.7.5  Recommendations: A policy review aimed at rationalizing taxation and providing incentives to make domestic 
MROs more competitive 
3.7.5.1 To reverse this trend and position India as a competitive regional MRO hub, the Committee recommends that the 
Ministry undertake a comprehensive policy review with a multi-pronged strategy. 
3.7.5.2 The Committee recommends the following: 

25 
i. Undertake a Comprehensive Policy Review: The Ministry should initiate a holistic policy review aimed at 
ensuring that a substantial and growing share of the MRO requirements of Indian airlines is met within the 
country. 
ii. Rationalize the Tax Structure: A key focus of this review must be the rationalization of the taxation structure for 
the MRO industry. This should include a review of the Go ods and Services Tax (GST) and customs duties 
applicable to imported aircraft spare parts, components, and tooling, with the goal of creating a level playing field 
for domestic MROs vis-à-vis their foreign competitors. While some initiatives have been take n to reduce GST and 
customs duties, further review may be necessary to ensure maximum competitiveness. 
iii. Provide Fiscal and Infrastructural Incentives: The government should introduce a scheme of targeted incentives to 
attract investment in state -of-the-art MRO facilities. This could include preferential lease rates for land at airport 
premises, viability gap funding for greenfield MRO projects, and the establishment of special economic zones for 
MRO activities. Existing MRO guidelines address aspects like royalties and land allotments at AAI airports. 
iv. Facilitate Skill Development and Technology Transfer: The Ministry should facilitate strategic collaborations 
between Indian MRO operators and established global service providers to promote technology transfer,  skill 
development, and adherence to the highest international safety and quality benchmarks. 
v. Encourage Domestic Allocation: The Ministry may examine the feasibility of introducing policy provisions that, 
while not mandating, strongly encourage domestic ai rlines to allocate a defined proportion of their annual MRO

monitoring, would help build confidence in the capabilities of Indian service providers. 
vi. Establish a National Aviation Skill Development Mission for MRO: Create a dedicated mission or task force

engineers, ensuring practical, hands-on training that aligns with international standards. 
vii. Incentivize Industry-Academia Collaboration and Training Centres: Offer fiscal incentives and preferential land

maintenance disciplines currently outsourced abroad. 
viii. Support Advanced Certification and Upskilling: Introduce government -subsidised or co -funded programmes for 
Indian technicians and engineers to acquire advanced technical certifications (e.g., EASA Part -66 equivalent, FAA 
A&P) necessary for high -level MRO work, thereby enhancing the quality and competitiveness of the domestic 
workforce and addressing the need for cutting-edge maintenance technologies. 
3.8  Aligning Infrastructure Growth with Fleet Expansion. 
3.8.1  Background: The rate of aircraft induction is outpacing the commissioning of new 
airport infrastructure. 
3.8.1.1 The Committee notes the exis tence of a critical and growing imbalance at the heart of India's aviation growth 
story. The data and stakeholder submissions indicate that the current pace of aircraft induction by airlines is significantly  
outstripping the rate at which new airport infra structure—including runways, taxiways, parking bays, and terminal 
capacity—is being developed and commissioned. As of June 2025, there are 846 aircraft registered on Air Operator 
Certificates (AOC) of Scheduled operators and 162 functional airports in the country. The ratio of aircraft induction to 
airport development is not fixed and can vary significantly depending on factors such as airline procurement cycles, 
retirement of older aircraft, airport project timelines, airport capacity addition, and changes in market demand. 
3.8.1.2 The existence of high -rise buildings in the airport periphery or in the path of takeoff and landing zones is 
addressed by Rule 12 of the Ministry of Civil Aviation (Height Restrictions for Safeguarding of Aircraft Operations) Rul es, 
2015. This rule stipulates that local, municipal, or town planning and development authorities, along with the airport 

26 
operator, are responsible for ensuring structure heights and locations comply with approved building plans and No Objection 
Certificates (NOCs). Monitoring and control of obstructions around airports are carried out regularly by the Airport 
Operator, with violations handled as per the Aircraft (Demolition of obstructions caused by Buildings and Trees etc.) Rules, 
1994. 
3.8.2  History: Rapid fleet and traffic growth is a core theme of the report. 
3.8.2.1 This imbalance is a direct and logical consequence of the rapid sectoral growth that forms the central context of 
this entire report. While airlines can induct new aircraft relatively qui ckly based on their commercial plans, the development 
of airport infrastructure is a long -term, capital-intensive process subject to numerous planning and regulatory hurdles. This 
has created a fundamental mismatch in their respective growth curves. 
3.8.3  Analysis: The imbalance puts pressure on existing facilities and regulatory oversight capacity. 
3.8.3.1 This desynchronized growth exerts immense and visible pressure on existing airport facilities, particularly at major 
metropolitan hubs. The impacts are  felt across the system, from degraded passenger service quality due to overcrowded 
terminals, to significant airside operational safety concerns arising from ramp congestion, increased runway occupancy 
times, and a higher density of ground vehicle movemen t. This congestion also places an additional and unsustainable burden 
on the already -strained capacities of the DGCA and the AAI, who are tasked with managing safety and efficiency in an 
increasingly saturated operational environment. 
3.8.4  Interpretation: A mismatch between fleet size and airport capacity can degrade service quality and strain safety 
margins. 
3.8.4.1 The current trajectory of uncoordinated growth between fleet size and infrastructure capacity is unsustainable and 
is pushing the aviation s ystem towards its saturation point. Adding more and more aircraft into a system with a relatively 
fixed infrastructure capacity is a recipe for chronic congestion, delays, and, most importantly, increased risk. When more 
aircraft are competing for the same  limited number of landing and takeoff slots, taxiways, and parking bays, it inevitably 
leads to ground delays, airborne holding patterns, and a more complex operational environment. 
3.8.4.2 This heightened complexity significantly increases the cognitive workload on both pilots and air traffic controllers, 
thereby increasing the probability of safety lapses such as ground collisions, runway incursions, and other operational error s. 
The system's safety margins, which are designed to absorb unexpected events, are progressively eroded by this constant state 
of high-density operation. Without a national -level strategic plan to align these two critical growth vectors, the system risks 
reaching a tipping point where these safety margins become dangerously thin, a nd the very growth that the sector celebrates 
becomes its greatest liability. 
3.8.5  Recommendations: Develop a comprehensive capacity alignment plan to synchronize airport development with 
fleet induction schedules. 
3.8.5.1 To ensure that growth remains safe and sustainable, it is imperative to move from a reactive posture to a proactive, 
planned approach to capacity management. 
3.8.5.2 The Committee recommends the following: 
i. Develop a National Capacity Alignment Plan: The Ministry of Civil Aviation, in c lose coordination with the 
DGCA, AAI, airport operators, and airlines, must take the lead in developing a long -term, comprehensive National 
Capacity Alignment Plan. 
ii. Synchronize Infrastructure and Fleet Growth: The primary objective of this plan should be t o synchronize airport 
infrastructure development timelines with the projected fleet induction schedules of Indian carriers. 
iii. Incorporate Phased Induction Schedules: The plan should explore mechanisms for a more phased approach to 
aircraft induction approvals, potentially linking them to the certified availability of adequate airport capacity (both 
airside and terminal-side) at the aircraft's proposed base of operations and key destinations. This would ensure that 
new capacity is absorbed by the system in a planned and orderly manner.

3.9  Modernizing ATC Automation and Airport Technology. 
3.9.1 Background: Performance degradation in automation systems at high-density airports. 
3.9.1.1 The Air Traffic Controllers' Guild (India) brought a critical technological issue to the Committee's attention. It was 
submitted that the existing automation systems used for air traffic control, particularly at high -density airports such as Delhi 
and Mumbai, have begun to exhibit significant performance degradation. This include s issues of system slowness, data 
processing lags, and a lack of modern decision -support features, which directly impact operational efficiency and 
compromise the safety margins within which controllers must operate. 
3.9.2  History: Pace of technological upgrades has not kept up with traffic expansion. 
3.9.2.1 The Committee observes that this issue is symptomatic of a broader trend: the pace of technological upgradation in 
India's critical Air Traffic Control infrastructure has failed to keep pace with the exponential growth in air traffic and 
airspace complexity. While the number of flights has soared, the core technological tools used to manage them have not 
evolved at a commensurate rate, leading to a growing gap between operational demands and system capabilities. 
3.9.3  Analysis: Existing systems lack advanced features for conflict detection and predictive analysis, increasing 
controller workload. 
3.9.3.1 The existing Indian ATC systems lack many of the advanced, integrated capabilities that are now stan dard in 
modern air traffic management systems used by global counterparts like EUROCONTROL or the FAA. These missing 
features include sophisticated, AI -enabled conflict detection and alerting tools, predictive analytics for traffic flow 
management, and sea mless, real -time data sharing capabilities between different control units and with aircraft. This 
technological deficit places an enormous additional cognitive strain on the already -overworked ATCOs. They are forced to 
manually compensate for the system's  shortcomings, performing mental calculations and cross -checks that a modern system 
would automate. This not only increases the risk of human error but also limits the overall capacity of the airspace. 
3.9.4  Interpretation: Outdated technology compromises operational efficiency and safety margins. 
3.9.4.1 The Indian aviation system is attempting to solve a digital -age problem of traffic complexity with tools that are 
becoming increasingly outdated. This creates a dangerous vulnerability in what is fundamen tally a human-machine system. 
The safety and efficiency of modern air traffic management depend on both the human and the machine components being 
robust and resilient. This report has already established that the human component (the ATCOs) is under sever e stress due to 
shortages and fatigue. This section reveals that the machine component is also failing them due to technological 
obsolescence. 
3.9.4.2 When both the human and the machine elements of a safety -critical system are under strain, the system's o verall 
resilience plummets. A single unexpected event, such as an unusual weather pattern or an aircraft emergency, is far more 
likely to cascade into a serious incident because neither the controller nor the system has the spare capacity to handle the 
deviation from the norm. Investing in modern ATC automation is therefore not a luxury or a mere efficiency upgrade; it is a 
critical and urgent necessity to de -stress the human element of the system. Advanced automation acts as a powerful safety 
net, reducing  controller workload, enhancing situational awareness, and automatically detecting potential conflicts long 
before they become critical. Neglecting this modernization directly exacerbates the risks associated with ATCO fatigue and 
high traffic density. 
3.9.5  Recommendations: A time -bound plan for a comprehensive overhaul of ATC automation systems, incorporating 
AI-driven tools. 
3.9.5.1 To address this critical technological gap and enhance the safety and efficiency of Indian airspace, the Committee 
recommends immediate and focused action. 
The Committee recommends the following: 
i. Initiate a Time-Bound Modernization Plan: The Ministry must direct the AAI to initiate a time -bound, funded plan 
for the comprehensive overhaul and modernization of ATC automation sy stems at all major and high-density ATC 
units across the country. 

28 
ii. Benchmark Against Global Standards: The procurement and development of these new systems must be 
benchmarked against the latest global standards and incorporate modern, essential features su ch as predictive 
analytics for traffic flow, AI -driven conflict resolution tools, advanced surface movement guidance, and real -time 
interoperability between air and ground systems. 
iii. Conduct a Technical Audit: A comprehensive technical audit of all existing automation platforms should be 
undertaken immediately to identify priority areas for intervention and to ensure that any immediate risks posed by 
system degradation are mitigated. 
iv. Involve End-Users in Development: The development and implementation of new systems must be done in close 
consultation with domain experts and, crucially, with serving ATCOs to ensure that the technology is user -
friendly, effective, and meets the real-world operational needs of the end-users. 
3.10   Enhancing Professional Representation and Governance. 
3.10.1 Background: The position of Member (ANS) on the AAI Board has been occupied by 
professionals without an ATC background. 
3.10.1.1 A fundamental issue of governance and professional representation was raised by the Air Traffic C ontrollers’ 
Guild (India). The Guild submitted that for the past several years, the crucial post of Member (Air Navigation Services) on 
the Board of the Airports Authority of India has been consistently occupied by professionals who, while accomplished in 
their own fields, lack a background in the highly specialized domain of Air Traffic Control. 
3.10.2  History: The Lahoti Commission recommended a dedicated Member (ATC) post after the 1996 Charkhi -Dadri 
collision. 
3.10.2.1 This concern is not new; it has a  long and tragic history. The Lahoti Commission, which was constituted to 
investigate the catastrophic Charkhi-Dadri mid-air collision in 1996—one of the deadliest in aviation history—had explicitly 
and strongly recommended the creation of a dedicated post  of Member (ATC) on the AAI Board. The rationale was clear: to 
ensure that this supremely safety-critical function was represented at the highest decision-making level by an individual with 
deep, first-hand domain expertise. That this key recommendation remains unimplemented decades later is a matter of serious 
concern. 
3.10.3  Analysis: A perceived neglect of critical ATC safety concerns and policy gaps due to lack of domain expertise at 
the board level. 
3.10.3.1 The ATC Guild contends that this persistent  lack of professional ATC representation at the board level has had 
tangible negative consequences. It has, they argue, led to a neglect of critical ATC -related safety concerns and the 
perpetuation of policy gaps. Decisions with substantial ramifications f or safety, technology, and human resources have 
allegedly been taken without a full and intuitive comprehension of the operational realities and intricate complexities of 
managing live air traffic. 
3.10.4  Interpretation: Decisions affecting a safety-critical function are made without adequate operational comprehension 
3.10.4.1 The current governance structure of the AAI, the sole provider of Air Navigation Services in the country, has a 
critical blind spot. Air Traffic Control is not just another departmen t; it is a uniquely specialized, technical, and safety -
sensitive domain where the tolerance for error is zero. Strategic decisions regarding technology procurement , long -term 
manpower planning , the definition of operational procedures, and airspace design require a deep and intuitive understanding 
of this domain that can only come from years of experience. 
3.10.4.2 A board member without an ATC background, however competent they may be in other areas like finance, 
engineering, or general administration, c annot provide this specific and vital expertise. They are forced to rely on second -
hand information and briefings, and may not be equipped to challenge flawed assumptions, ask the right critical questions, or 
fully grasp the cascading safety implications o f a given policy or investment decision. This creates a fundamental 
governance failure where the body responsible for a safety -critical function lacks expert leadership from within that function 

29 
at its highest level. This governance gap can be seen as a ro ot cause contributing to many of the other issues highlighted in 
this report, such as the failures in ATCO manpower planning and the slow pace of technological modernization. 
3.10.5  Recommendations: Creation of a dedicated post of Member (ATC) on the Board of AAI. 
3.10.5.1 Addressing this governance deficit is a prerequisite for tackling the many operational and technical symptoms it 
has created. The Committee believes that the time has come to act on the long-standing wisdom of the Lahoti Commission. 
3.10.5.2 The Committee therefore suggests that the Ministry of Civil Aviation examine the feasibility of creating a 
dedicated post of Member (Air Traffic Control) on the Board of the Airports Authority of India. This post must be held by a 
qualified and experi enced Air Traffic Control professional. Such an institutional arrangement would ensure that matters 
relating to ATC policy, safety, technology, and modernization receive the informed, focused, and expert attention they 
demand at the highest decision -making level, thereby strengthening the entire air navigation services framework of the 
country.

3.11.1.1 India’s civil aviation sector is undergoing unprecedented expansion, leading to a significant increase in fleet size

3.11.2.2 To enhance practical training capacity, DGCA has fa cilitated the induction of 34 aircraft into the training fleet of

3.11.3.1 The detailed measures taken by the Ministry and DGCA indicate a proactive approach to address the anticipated 
demand for pilots. The increase in the number of FTOs and training aircraft, coupled with efforts to streamline certification 
and improve training quality through a ranking framework and CAR amendments, suggests a strategic commitment to 
expanding pilot training infrastructure domestically. The focus on establishing FTOs at smaller, regional airports also aligns 
with the broader goal of expanding air services and connectivity across the country. 
3.11.3.2 These proactive measures represent a successful and critical investment in domestic manpower skill development, 
specifically for pilot supply. By increasing the number of Flying Training Organizations (FTOs) and training aircraft, 
coupled with efforts to streamline certification and improve training quality through a ranking framework and CAR 
amendments, India is actively building its self-sufficiency in a vital aviation skill. This strategic commitment is essential for 
ensuring a consistent and high-quality supply of qualified pilots for India’s rapidly growing aviation sector.

3.12   Strengthening Human Factors and Fatigue Management for Flight Crew. 
3.12.1 Background: Addressing stress and fatigue among pilots and air traffic controllers. 
3.12.1.1  Human factors, particularly stress and fatigue, are critical safety concerns in aviation, affecting both flight crews 
and air traffic controllers. Ensuring that operational personnel are well -rested and mentally fit is paramount  for maintaining 
high safety standards in a dynamic and demanding environment. 
3.12.2  History: Regulatory evolution for crew duty time limitations and mental health. 
3.12.2.1 To address the issue of stress and fatigue, the DGCA has promulgated Civil Aviation Requirement (CAR) Section 
7, Series J, Part III on Flight Duty Time Limitation (FDTL). This CAR broadly stipulates minimum/maximum rest and 
maximum duty requirements for pi lots. This CAR was updated on March 26, 2024, to further mitigate stress and fatigue 
factors for pilots. 
3.12.2.2 In addition to FDTL regulations, DGCA has also issued Medical Circular 01 of 2023 regarding Mental Health of 
flight crews and ATCOs. This circ ular, available on the DGCA website, lays down clear guidelines for operators (scheduled 
and non-scheduled) and DGCA Empaneled Medical Examiners concerning mental health. 
3.12.2.3 For all scheduled operators operating scheduled, charter, or non -scheduled f lights originating from India, each 
flight crew member and cabin crew member is subjected to pre -flight breath -analyzer examination at the first departure 
airport during a flight duty period. For other operators, pre -flight breath-analyzer examination is a lso required, or a post -
flight examination if infrastructure is not available. All positive or missed breath -analyzer examinations must be reported to 
the DGCA in accordance with CAR Section 5 Series F Part III, leading to necessary enforcement actions. 
3.12.3  Analysis: Proactive regulatory measures to enhance crew well-being. 
3.12.3.1 The proactive update to FDTL regulations and the issuance of comprehensive mental health guidelines 
demonstrate DGCA's commitment to addressing human factors in aviation saf ety. These measures aim to ensure that pilots 
and ATCOs operate within safe limits of duty and have access to support for mental well -being, directly impacting their 
performance and decision -making capabilities. The regular breath -analyzer examinations als o contribute to operational 
safety by monitoring crew fitness for duty. 

31 
3.12.4 Interpretation: Continuous enhancement of human factors in safety oversight 
3.12.4.1 The implementation of updated FDTL regulations and mental health guidelines signifies a cont inuous effort to 
enhance human factors in safety oversight. While fatigue management was previously highlighted in the context of ATCO 
staffing, these specific measures extend to flight crew and encompass a broader scope of human well -being, reflecting 
international best practices in aviation safety management. These measures are crucial for maintaining the cognitive and 
physical readiness of operational personnel, which is vital for preventing errors and ensuring safe flight operations. 
3.12.5  Recommendations: Strengthen implementation and evaluate effectiveness of human factors initiatives. 
3.12.5.1 To ensure the full effectiveness of these critical human factors initiatives, the Committee recommends a robust 
approach to their implementation and continuous evaluation. 
3.12.5.2 The Committee recommends the following: 
i. Ensure Strict FDTL Compliance: The DGCA must rigorously monitor and enforce compliance with the updated 
FDTL regulations for all flight crews, ensuring that operators do not circumvent these vital safety measures. 
ii. Promote Mental Health Awareness and Support: Actively promote mental health awareness programs across the 
aviation industry for both flight crews and ATCOs, ensuring that the guidelines in Medical Circular 01 of 2023 are 
fully implemented and that personnel feel safe and supported in seeking assistance.  Airlines must implement 
mandatory crew training for de -escalation and mental health crisis intervention to handle passengers efficiently. 
The DGCA should establish clear guideli nes for quick, empathetic and non -violent handling of such incidents. 
Apart from the training, the pilots should be given continuous counselling at frequent intervals. 
iii. Evaluate Impact of FDTL and Mental Health Initiatives: Conduct periodic studies and data  analysis to evaluate the

iv. Integrate Human Factors Training: Mandate and enhance human factors trai ning for all operational personnel, 
emphasizing fatigue risk management, stress coping mechanisms, and the importance of reporting concerns 
without fear of reprisal. 
v. Review Breath-Analyzer Examination Infrastructure: Continuously assess and expand the infr astructure for pre -
flight breath-analyzer examinations, particularly at airports where post -flight checks are currently the norm due to 
lack of facilities.

4.0 Summary of Key Recommendations. 
4.1 This report has identified twelve key areas requiring systemic improvement to ensure the continued safety and 
sustainable growth of India's civil aviation sector. The core recommendations for each area are summarized below: 
i. Enhancing Regulatory Autonomy and Capacity: The report recommends a time -bound plan to grant the 
Directorate General of Civil Aviation (DGCA) full administrative and financial autonomy. This is imperativ e 
to address critical technical staff shortages caused by an ineffective recruitment model, a problem highlighted 
by past expert committees but never resolved. Autonomy is essential for the regulator to attract talent, set 
industry-commensurate salaries, a nd effectively enforce compliance in a rapidly expanding sector. The 
Ministry's current stance is that direct recruitment by DGCA is not under consideration. 
ii. Mitigating Air Traffic Controller Fatigue and Staffing Deficits: An immediate recommendation is th e 
development of a national Fatigue Risk Management System (FRMS) for Air Traffic Controllers (ATCOs) 
and a comprehensive staffing audit. This is to end the long -standing and high -risk practice of seeking 
exemptions from mandatory duty time limitations. Th e current mismatch between recruitment and training 
capacity, coupled with operational overload, poses a direct and ongoing threat to airspace safety. The AAI 
acknowledges increasing workload but states ATCO deployment is based on DGCA CAR for WDTL and that 
1636 additional ATCO posts have been created recently. 
iii. Strengthening Surveillance and Enforcement Mechanisms: The Committee recommends establishing a time -
bound mechanism for the closure of all safety deficiencies, with priority for serious Level I matte rs, and 
implementing stronger enforcement actions, including financial penalties for non -compliance. This is a 
response to the significant and growing backlog of unresolved safety findings, which indicates a critical 
weakness in the post -surveillance recti fication process that undermines the entire oversight program. 
Concerns also exist about audit quality due to a lack of qualified DGCA staff and airlines prioritizing 
profiteering over immediate maintenance.

iv. Standardizing Safety Protocols for Helicopter Op erations: A uniform national regulatory framework for all 
state-operated helicopter services and mandatory, terrain -specific pilot training are recommended. This is 
prompted by a series of accidents in high -risk environments managed by state -level agencies  with limited 
central oversight, revealing a regulatory ambiguity that creates unacceptable safety gaps. While the Ministry 
defines central and state roles and DGCA has issued a circular for pilgrimage operations, the Committee 
highlights the need for more proactive and uniform central oversight. 
v. Addressing Recurring Operational Risks: The report suggests mandating a detailed root -cause analysis for 
every runway incursion and other recurring high -risk events, and establishing focused remedial programs for 
these areas. This is necessary because key safety targets for events like runway incursions are consistently 
being exceeded, indicating that the current incident review process is failing to translate lessons learned into 
effective operational changes. Spec ific data on bird strikes and engine failures underscores the need for 
effective remedial measures despite existing systematic oversight. 
vi. Establishing a "Just Culture" and Whistleblower Protection: The Ministry of Civil Aviation, in consultation 
with the DGCA, should review penal provisions applicable to operational personnel to align them with "Just 
Culture" principles and must institute a comprehensive, legally -backed whistleblower protection framework. 
This is to counter a punitive culture that deters th e open reporting of errors, which is essential for proactive 
safety management. While DGCA has a confidential and non -punitive Voluntary Safety Reporting System, 
the Committee emphasizes the need for clearer protections. 
vii. Developing Domestic MRO Capabilitie s: A comprehensive policy review is recommended, aimed at 
rationalizing taxation and providing incentives to make domestic Maintenance, Repair, and Overhaul (MRO) 
facilities more competitive. India's current dependence on foreign MROs (estimated at 85% of activities) 
results in significant foreign exchange outflow and creates a strategic vulnerability, hindering the 
development of a self -reliant national aviation ecosystem. The Government has implemented several 
initiatives, including GST and customs duty r eductions, and new MRO guidelines, to promote domestic 
MRO. 
viii. Aligning Infrastructure Growth with Fleet Expansion: The Committee recommends developing a 
comprehensive National Capacity Alignment Plan to synchronize airport infrastructure development with 
airline fleet induction schedules. The current imbalance, where aircraft induction is outpacing airport growth 
(846 aircraft vs. 162 functional airports as of June 2025), is putting unsustainable pressure on existing 
facilities, degrading service quality, and dangerously straining safety margins. 
ix. Modernizing ATC Automation and Airport Technology: A time -bound plan for a comprehensive overhaul of 
Air Traffic Control (ATC) automation systems, incorporating modern AI -driven tools, is recommended. The 
current systems are showing performance degradation and lack advanced safety features, increasing controller 
workload and compromising operational efficiency and safety in an increasingly complex airspace. 
x. Enhancing Professional Representation and Governance: The crea tion of a dedicated post of Member (ATC) 
on the Board of the Airports Authority of India (AAI) is suggested, acting on a long-pending recommendation 
from the Lahoti Commission. This is to address a critical governance failure where the most safety -sensitive 
function, air traffic control, lacks expert representation at the highest decision -making level, leading to policy 
gaps and a neglect of core safety concerns. 
xi. Expanding Pilot Training Infrastructure: The Committee recommends sustained investment and cont inuous 
monitoring of pilot training infrastructure. This is to ensure a sufficient and high -quality supply of qualified 
pilots for the rapidly expanding sector, supported by recent approvals for new Flying Training Organizations 
(FTOs), induction of training aircraft, and the development of a ranking framework for FTOs. 
xii. Strengthening Human Factors and Fatigue Management for Flight Crew: The Committee recommends 
rigorous implementation and continuous evaluation of human factors initiatives. This includes ens uring strict 
compliance with updated Flight Duty Time Limitation (FDTL) regulations for pilots and promoting mental 
health awareness and support for both flight crews and ATCOs, as outlined in recent DGCA circulars.
