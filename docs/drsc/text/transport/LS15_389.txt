INTRODUCTION 
1. I, the Chairman, Department-related Parliamentary Standing Committee on Transport, 
Tourism and Culture, having been authorised by the Committee, present this Report on the 
Demands for Grants (2026-27) of the Ministry of Ports, Shipping and Waterways (Demand 
No. 78). 
2. The Committee examined the Demands for Grants in the context of the strategic centrality 
of the maritime sector to India’s economic architecture, trade competitiveness, and logistical 
efficiency. India possesses a coastline of 11,098 km, 12 Major Ports, approximately 217 
other-than-major ports, and 111 declared National Waterways. The maritime sector transports 
approximately 95 per cent of India’s trade by volume and 68 per cent by value. The Ministry 
of Ports, Shipping and Waterways exercises policy and regulatory oversight across this 
sector, encompassing port development, shipping regulation, shipbuilding promotion, ship 
repair and recycling, dredging, lighthouse administration, and inland water transport. The 
pace and quality of infrastructure modernisation, regulatory reform, and capacity creation 
within this sector have a direct bearing on the nation’s logistics costs, trade facilitation, and 
the objective of establishing India as a global maritime leader under the Maritime Amrit Kaal 
Vision 2047. 
3. The Committee’s scrutiny was directed not merely at the adequacy of financial allocations 
but at the structural coherence between budgetary intent and ground-level outcomes. 
Particular attention was devoted to: the absorptive capacity of the Ministry for an 
unprecedented 78 per cent increase in net GBS; the operationalisation of three new schemes 
(SBFAS, SbDS, and MDF) approved barely three months before the commencement of the 
financial year; the persistent concentration of IWT cargo on five waterways out of thirty-two 
operational; the disparity between created port capacity and actual throughput; the rate of 
Sagarmala project completion after a decade of implementation; the adequacy of last-mile rail 
and road connectivity to ports; the implementation architecture for five landmark maritime 
Acts enacted in 2025; and the readiness of the maritime workforce for a projected forty-five-
fold expansion in shipbuilding capacity by 2047. 
4. In the course of its examination, the Committee issued a detailed questionnaire to the 
Ministry and scrutinized the following documents: 
(i) Detailed Demands for Grants (Demand No. 78), financial year 2026-27; 
(ii) Ministry’s Written Submission to the Committee’s Questionnaire; 
(iii) Ministry’s Depositional Notes (scheme-wise Reply/Status); 
(iv) Annual Report of the Ministry of Ports, Shipping and Waterways (2025-26); 
(v) Output-Outcome Monitoring Framework (OOMF), financial year 2026-27; 
(vi) Annual Action Plan 2026-27 and Sector-wise Budgetary Outlays;

5. The Committee’s examination encompassed six principal thematic dimensions: the overall 
financial architecture and its consonance with stated policy objectives; scheme-wise scrutiny 
of major programmes; a structured review of past demand versus fulfilment over three 
financial years; cross-cutting tests of budget realism, regional equity, port capacity utilisation, 
last-mile connectivity, efficiency-outcome linkage, and institutional governance; the green 
shipping transition and digital transformation; and a focused assessment of workforce 
development and legislative implementation readiness. The Committee further took oral 
evidence of the Secretary and senior officials of the Ministry on 25th February 2026. 
6. The Ministry, in its written submission to the Committee’s questionnaire, covered twenty-
two questions, spanning port modernisation, shipbuilding competitiveness, inland waterways, 
logistics cost reduction, digital initiatives, workforce development, environmental clearances, 
and the Maritime Development Fund. The reply disclosed, inter alia, that 145 out of 283 
berths at major ports are fully mechanised, handling 78 per cent of total cargo, with a target 
of 93 per cent by 2030; that cargo handling capacity has risen from 800.52 MTPA in 2013-14 
to 1,681 MTPA in 2024-25 (an increase of 110 per cent); that average turnaround time has 
improved from 93.6 hours to 49.5 hours (47 per cent reduction); that Indian shipyards face a 
25–30 per cent cost disadvantage relative to China, Korea, and Japan; and that 32 National 
Waterways are now operational, carrying 160.86 million tonnes up to December 2025. The 
Ministry’s depositional notes further recorded [Depositional Notes, Reply/Status] that only 5 
per cent of India’s EXIM cargo is carried by Indian-owned or Indian-flagged ships; that 
Indian-built ships account for merely 7.63 per cent of Indian-owned vessels; and that 
commercial shipbuilding capacity requires a forty-five-fold expansion from 0.1 million GT to 
4.5 million GT by 2047. These submissions have been examined in detail across Chapters 1 
through 5 and inform the Committee’s thirty-three observations and recommendations in 
Chapter 6. 
7. The OOMF for 2026-27 was examined with particular attention to the measurability of 
output and outcome indicators. The Committee note that both SbDS and SBFAS carry the 
designation ‘Target not amenable’ for all quantitative indicators in 2026-27, on the ground 
that the schemes are in their inaugural year and project identification is at an initial stage. The 
Jalvahak scheme targets 3,12,000 tonnes of modal-shift cargo and 16,800 tonnes through 20 
ICSL scheduled voyages. The IWT in NER programme targets 8 DPR/feasibility studies, 10 
vessels, and 2 terminals. These targets have been evaluated in the context of their adequacy 
relative to the allocations sought.

8. The Annual Report (2025-26) provided the Committee with a comprehensive factual 
foundation, including port-wise performance indicators, turnaround times, cargo traffic 
disaggregation, the status of PPP projects, the operational details of digital platforms, the 
green transition initiatives, the progress of Sagarmala projects, and the institutional 
developments at Cochin Shipyard Limited, Garden Reach Shipbuilders and Engineers 
Limited, Hooghly Cochin Shipyard Limited, the Shipping Corporation of India, the Dredging 
Corporation of India, the Sagarmala Maritime Finance Corporation Limited, and the Indian 
Port Rail and Ropeway Corporation Limited. The Committee have drawn upon this material

9. This Report synthesises the Committee’s analysis of the budgetary documentation, the 
Ministry’s written submission to the Committee’s questionnaire, the Ministry’s depositional 
notes, the Annual Report, the Annual Action Plan, and the OOMF. It places fiscal discipline, 
outcome accountability, maritime safety, equitable regional development, and workforce 
readiness at the centre of its assessment. The Committee’s thirty-three recommendations are 
framed in a spirit of constructive engagement and are intended to strengthen the planning, 
execution, and monitoring of the Ministry’s programmes. 
10. The Committee place on record their appreciation of the assistance rendered by the Rajya 
Sabha Secretariat, and of the cooperation extended by the Ministry of Ports, Shipping and 
Waterways and all organisations under its administrative purview in furnishing 
documentation and oral submissions. 
11. The Committee considered and adopted this Report at its meeting held on 23rd March 
2026.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY 
0.1.1 The total budgetary allocation under Demand No. 78 for 2026-27 is Rs. 5,764.80 crore 
(gross), comprising Rs. 4,464.88 crore under Revenue and Rs. 1,299.92 crore under Capital (net: 
Rs. 5,164.80 crore). The Internal and Extra Budgetary Resources (IEBR) of Rs. 12,702.94 crore 
bring the combined outlay to Rs. 17,867.74 crore. Three newly-approved schemes, SBFAS, 
SbDS, and MDF, command Rs. 1,765 crore (34 per cent of net GBS), notwithstanding their 
operational guidelines having been released only on 26th December 2025. 
0.1.2 The Ministry's depositional notes record that India ranks 17th in global shipbuilding, 
contributing less than 1 per cent to global capacity. Only 5 per cent of India's EXIM cargo is 
carried by Indian-owned or Indian-flagged ships. Indian-built ships constitute merely 7.63 per 
cent of Indian-owned ships. The sector carries an employment multiplier of 6.4 and an investment 
multiplier of 1.82. The Ministry's written submission to the Committee's questionnaire further 
disclosed that Indian shipyards face a 25-30 per cent cost disadvantage compared to major 
shipbuilding nations such as China, Korea, and Japan, attributable to higher financing costs, 
limited economies of scale, and gaps in advanced technology. The depositional notes further 
record that India's commercial shipbuilding capacity requires expansion from 0.1 million GT to 
approximately 4.5 million GT per annum by 2047, constituting a forty-five-fold increase. 
0.1.3 Against a BE of Rs. 3,470.58 crore for 2025-26, the RE was reduced to Rs. 2,899.11 crore 
(a contraction of 16.5 per cent), and actual expenditure as on 31st January 2026 stood at Rs. 
2,329.86 crore (80.4 per cent of RE). The OOMF lists 'Target not amenable' for virtually all 
quantitative indicators under SbDS and SBFAS, limiting outcome accountability in the inaugural 
year. Major Ports handled 854.86 MMT of cargo in 2024-25, with 8.22 per cent year-on-year 
growth. IWT cargo expanded from 18 MMT in 2013-14 to 146 MMT in 2024-25. Sagarmala has 
completed 277 of 839 projects (33 per cent) after approximately a decade. The SMFCL, now 
functioning as an NBFC (RBI licence dated 19th June 2025), has sanctioned Rs. 4,198.10 crore in 
lending. 
0.1.4 The Global Maritime India Summit 2025, held in October 2025 in Mumbai, was attended 
by 93 countries, and resulted in over 600 MoUs valued at approximately Rs. 12 lakh crore, 
signalling growing international engagement with India's maritime sector. 
0.1.5 This Report details thirty-seven observations and recommendations across seven thematic 
groups: financial management and budgetary discipline; port infrastructure, connectivity and 
capacity utilisation; shipbuilding, ship repair, recycling and dredging; inland waterways, cruise 
tourism and NW development; sector reforms and regulatory architecture; technology, digital 
governance and green transition; and workforce development, international connectivity and 
heritage.

CHAPTER 1 
Overall Assessment of the Ministry's Demands 
1.1 Role and Mandate 
1.1.1 The Ministry of Ports, Shipping and Waterways administers policy, regulation, and 
developmental oversight across India's maritime sector. The Ministry's jurisdiction 
encompasses 12 Major Ports, approximately 217 other-than-major ports, 111 declared 
National Waterways (of which 32 are operational across 20,187 km in 23 States and 4 Union 
Territories), the regulatory apparatus governing shipping, and the promotion of 
shipbuilding, ship repair, ship recycling, and dredging. The Ministry submitted in its Annual 
Report that it was reconstituted from the erstwhile Ministry of Shipping on 9th November 
2020, reflecting the policy emphasis on waterways development. 
1.2 Key Policy Priorities for 2026-27 
1.2.1 The Ministry's depositional notes set out its focus areas for 2026-27, which the 
Committee have examined. The Ministry submitted that its priorities include: development 
and operationalisation of 20 new National Waterways beginning with NW-5 (588 km); 
operationalisation of SBFAS and SbDS for shipbuilding expansion; establishment of the 
MDF; advancement of Sagarmala including the NMHC at Lothal; implementation of ONOP 
and Maritime Single Window 2.0 (MSW-Sagar Setu); development of river cruise tourism 
across 17 circuits; green shipping transition through hydrogen fuel-cell and electric vessels; 
procurement of 11 dredgers by DCI; establishment of ship repair ecosystems at Varanasi and 
Patna; setting up of Regional Centres of Excellence at Varanasi and Dibrugarh; and 
implementation of the five maritime Acts of 2025. 
1.2.2 The Ministry further submitted during oral evidence that three Budget 
announcements for 2026-27 pertain to the Ministry: a Rs. 10,000 crore Container 
Manufacturing Scheme over five years to scale domestic container production capacity from 
70,000 to approximately 7 lakh containers annually; the operationalisation of 20 new 
National Waterways with Regional Centres of Excellence and ship repair facilities at 
Varanasi and Patna; and a Coastal Cargo Promotion Scheme to raise the modal share of IWT 
and coastal shipping from 6 per cent to 12 per cent by 2047.

1.4 Growth Trajectory of the Maritime Sector 
1.4.1 The Annual Report records that cargo at 12 Major Ports grew from 819.29 MMT in 
2023-24 to 854.86 MMT in 2024-25. During April-December 2025, cargo reached 672.98 
MMT (8.22 per cent YoY growth). Container traffic stood at 193.52 MMT (13.54 million 
TEUs) in 2024-25. SMP Kolkata handled 50.977 MMT during April-December 2025 (growth 
of 15.18 per cent). Paradip Port recorded the highest volume of rail-borne traffic among 
Major Ports at 81.01 MMT. 
1.4.2 The Committee further note from the Annual Report that 57 PPP projects valued at Rs. 
42,235 crore are operational, contributing approximately 660 MTPA of additional capacity. 
A further pipeline of 48 projects worth Rs. 23,000 crore has been identified for 2025-26 to 
2030-31. PPP and captive operators are projected to handle nearly 80 per cent of total cargo 
at Major Ports by 2030.

1.4.3 The Ministry, in its written submission to the Committee's questionnaire, states that 
32 National Waterways are operational as on 30th January 2026, with a combined length of 
5,155 km, and that cargo movement reached 146 million tonnes in 2024-25, reflecting a 
compound annual growth rate of 21 per cent since 2013-14. During 2025-26, 160.86 million 
tonnes had been recorded up to December 2025, and 180.1 million tonnes up to January 
2026 as per the Ministry's depositional notes. The Ministry's focus areas document records a 
target of 200 million tonnes for the financial year 2025-26 and 500 MMT by 2047. Coal 
leads commodity share at 20 per cent, followed by iron ore fines, fly ash, sand, and coke. The 
Ministry's depositional notes record passenger traffic of 7.76 crore in 2024-25 and over 9 
crore in the current year, while the Ministry's focus areas document records passenger traffic 
of 7.64 crore for 2024-25 and over 7 crore up to December 2025. The Committee note the 
variance in the passenger traffic figures for 2024-25 and recommend that the Ministry 
furnish the reconciled figure. The newly-built Farakka lock has reduced vessel transit time 
from 2 hours to 23 minutes. The modernisation of the older lock is targeted for completion 
by July 2026. 
1.4.4 The Committee note that the Global Maritime India Summit 2025, held in October 
2025 in Mumbai with the participation of 93 countries, resulted in over 600 MoUs valued at 
approximately Rs. 12 lakh crore. The Special Secretary submitted during oral evidence that 
the National Monetisation Pipeline 2.0, launched in February 2026, assigns a monetisation 
target of Rs. 2,63,700 crore for the maritime sector for the period 2025-26 to 2029-30. The 
Committee note that under NMP 1.0, the Ministry achieved monetisation of Rs. 21,699 crore 
against a target of Rs. 12,828 crore. The Committee observes that NMP 2.0 represents a 
significantly larger commitment and will require sustained monitoring.

CHAPTER 3 
Scheme-wise Scrutiny of Major Programmes 
3.1 Sagarmala Programme 
3.1.1 The Ministry's depositional notes record that the Sagarmala Programme provides 
financial assistance across five pillars: port modernisation, port connectivity, port-led 
industrialisation, coastal community development, and coastal shipping and IWT. The 
Ministry, in its written submission to the Committee's questionnaire, further states that 
Sagarmala 2.0 is under formulation with six pillars, adding ship repair and recycling, and 
island development to the original framework. The Annual Report records that 277 of 839 
projects have been completed (33 per cent), 209 are under implementation, and 353 remain 
at the planning stage. The allocation for 2026-27 is Rs. 616.88 crore. Under Sagarmala, 128 
projects costing Rs. 8,912 crore have been sanctioned, with GBS support. Thirty-seven 
fishing harbour projects worth Rs. 6,583 crore are underway, of which 12 (Rs. 1,078 crore) 
are complete. The Special Secretary submitted during oral evidence that 78 Sagarmala 
scheme-funded projects valued at Rs. 5,355 crore have been completed, and 50 projects 
worth approximately Rs. 3,500 crore are under implementation. The Committee note the 
distinction between the broader Sagarmala programme (839 projects across multiple 
ministries and funding sources) and the projects directly funded through the Ministry's 
Sagarmala scheme allocation, and recommend that future documentation clarify this 
distinction to facilitate accurate monitoring. 
3.1.2 RoPax and passenger ferry services developed under Sagarmala have demonstrated 
measurable impact. The Annual Report records that the Mumbai-Mandwa route reduced 
travel time from 3 hours to 45 minutes, the Ghogha-Hazira route from 9 hours to 
approximately 4 hours, and the Raigad-Ratnagiri distance was shortened by 60 km. These 
services have enabled the movement of over 1 lakh cargo trucks, saving more than 2 crore 
litres of fuel. 
3.1.3 The NMHC at Lothal, approved by the Cabinet on 9th October 2024, has been 
earmarked Rs. 100.88 crore for 2026-27. Five fishing harbours adjacent to Major Ports, 
Kochi, Chennai, Visakhapatnam, Paradip, and Mallet Bunder, have been sanctioned for 
modernisation. 
3.2 Shipbuilding: SBFAS, SbDS, and MDF 
3.2.1 The Ministry, in its written submission to the Committee's questionnaire, provides a 
detailed exposition of the four-pillar strategy approved by Cabinet on 24th September 2025 
to enhance shipbuilding competitiveness. The written submission states that under SBFAS, 
for normal vessels, assistance is 15 per cent up to Rs. 100 crore and 20 per cent on the 
portion above Rs. 100 crore; for specialised, hybrid, and green vessels, the incentive is 15 per 

 
7 
 
cent on the first Rs. 100 crore and 25 per cent on the remaining value. The Maritime 
Development Fund provides Rs. 25,000 crore through a blended-finance structure 
comprising the Maritime Investment Fund (Rs. 20,000 crore for equity support) and the 
Interest Incentivization Fund (Rs. 5,000 crore for reducing borrowing costs). The India Ship 
Technology Centre, with a proposed outlay of Rs. 305 crore under the Indian Maritime 
University, is to serve as the apex body for indigenous design and technological capability. 
The Ministry's depositional notes further record that the total SBFAS corpus up to 2036 is 
Rs. 24,736 crore; that the shipbreaking credit note provides 40 per cent of fair scrap value; 
and that the National Shipbuilding Mission is the designated institutional body to 
coordinate implementation. 
3.2.2 SbDS, with Rs. 19,989 crore, comprises: greenfield cluster development (Rs. 9,930 
crore through 50:50 Centre-State SPVs with 100 per cent capital support for common 
maritime infrastructure); brownfield expansion (Rs. 8,261 crore at 25 per cent capital 
assistance); and shipbuilding risk coverage (Rs. 1,443 crore for pre-shipment, post-
shipment, and vendor default insurance). The Ministry submitted that the scheme intends to 
promote shipbuilding clusters as part of the long-term vision and that government 
assistance is critical given the scale of the required capacity expansion. 
3.2.3 MDF, with Rs. 25,000 crore, provides blended finance through the Maritime 
Investment Fund (Rs. 20,000 crore for equity support at 49 per cent Government equity) 
and the Interest Incentivisation Fund (Rs. 5,000 crore for up to 3 per cent interest subsidy 
through SMFCL). The Committee note that SMFCL has been registered as an NBFC (RBI 
licence 19th June 2025) with an approved borrowing limit of Rs. 25,000 crore and has 
sanctioned Rs. 4,198.10 crore. 
3.2.4 The Annual Report records that Kolkata-based shipyards, GRSE and Hooghly-CSL,

Detailed Observations and Recommendations 
The Committee, having scrutinised the Demands for Grants (2026-27), the Ministry's 
written submission to the Committee's questionnaire, the Ministry's depositional notes, the 
Annual Report (2025-26), the Annual Action Plan, the Output-Outcome Monitoring 
Framework, and the oral evidence of the representatives of the Ministry, place on record the 
following observations and recommendations. 
6.1 Financial Management and Budgetary Discipline 
6.1.1 Observation 1 — Overall Budgetary Architecture and Absorptive Capacity: 
The Committee scrutinised the Detailed Demands for Grants and note that the net allocation 
of          Rs. 5,164.80 crore represents a 78 per cent increase over the RE of Rs. 2,899.11 crore 
for 2025-26. Rs. 1,765 crore (34 per cent of net GBS) is directed towards SBFAS, SbDS, and 
MDF, schemes approved by Cabinet on 24th September 2025, with operational guidelines 
released only on 26th December 2025. The combined corpus of these three schemes over the 
decade to 2036 is approximately Rs. 69,725 crore. The Ministry's written submission 
discloses that actual expenditure in 2025-26 stood at Rs. 2,329.86 crore as on 31st January 
2026 against  BE of Rs. 3,470.58 crore. The Committee further note from the Output-
Outcome Monitoring Framework that the OOMF designates 'Target not amenable' for 
virtually all quantitative indicators under SbDS and SBFAS, raising the question of how 
absorption will be monitored in the absence of measurable output benchmarks. 
6.1.2 Recommendation: He Committee recommends that the Ministry develop a phased 
disbursement plan for the first year of SBFAS, SbDS, and MDF operations, with quarterly 
milestones tied to specific operational readiness benchmarks including scheme portal 
operationalisation, application pipeline development, appraisal infrastructure, and fund 
release triggers. An inter-divisional Task Force may be constituted to monitor the roll-out of 
these schemes and identify absorptive capacity gaps in real time rather than at year-end. 
6.1.3 Observation 2 — Realism of Budget Estimates and Expenditure Pattern: As 
examined from the documents received from the Ministry, the depository statement for 
2025–26 reveals that the 2025-26 BE was contracted by 16.5 per cent at RE, with only 71 per 
cent of RE expended by 31st December 2025 (updated to 80.4 per cent as at 31st January 
2026). In the two preceding years, the BE was revised upward by 7.9 per cent and 20.2 per 
cent respectively. In Sagarmala, Actuals of Rs. 658.29 crore fell short of RE by Rs. 50.71 
crore. Such oscillation between over-estimation and under-utilisation points to systemic 
deficiencies in the Ministry's internal budget estimation methodology. The Committee 
further note that the Ministry furnished no analysis of the reasons for variation in its 
statement, with multiple entries showing, suggesting inadequate quality control in the 
preparation of budgetary documentation itself. 

 
16 
 
6.1.4 Recommendation: He Committee recommends that the Ministry institute a 
structured monthly expenditure tracking mechanism for each major head, consonant with 
the Modified Cash Management System of the Department of Expenditure. The internal 
estimation process may be redesigned as a bottom-up exercise drawing on implementing 
agencies' quarterly fund absorption plans, rather than a top-down normative allocation 
model, so as to produce estimates that are realistic at inception rather than requiring 
substantial mid-year correction. 
6.1.5 Observation 3 — Revenue-Capital Composition and Subsidy-to-Asset 
Conversion: The Committee note that the Revenue Section constitutes 77.5 per cent of the 
gross Demand, a structural feature driven by the subsidy character of SBFAS. The Ministry's 
written submission furnished that SBFAS provides financial assistance of 15-25 per cent per 
vessel to make construction viable. The Ministry's depositional notes further record that the 
shipbreaking credit note provides 40 per cent of fair scrap value. The Committee observes 
that the predominance of revenue expenditure in a sector requiring massive infrastructure 
creation warrants systematic tracking of whether each tranche of revenue subsidy is 
generating verifiable capital-asset creation in the form of delivered gross tonnage. 
6.1.6 Recommendation: He Committeeis of theconsidered view that the Ministry may 
wish to develop a Subsidy-to-Asset Conversion Index for the SBFAS programme, tracking 
the relationship between each tranche of financial assistance disbursed and the 
corresponding shipbuilding capacity commissioned, measured in gross tonnage. Such an 
index, published annually alongside the OOMF, would strengthen expenditure-outcome 
accountability for the single largest revenue head in the Demand and provide the Committee 
with a structured basis for future scrutiny. 
6.1.7 Observation 4 — IEBR Monitoring and Port Authority Financial Health: 
The IEBR of Rs. 12,702.94 crore is approximately 2.5 times the GBS. The Ports and 
Lighthouses sector relies on IEBR of Rs. 11,944.07 crore against GBS of Rs. 841.74 crore. 
While this leveraging model reduces the fiscal burden on the Consolidated Fund, the 
Committee observes that it creates a structural gap in parliamentary oversight, since the 
IEBR does not form part of the Demand and is not subjected to the same degree of scrutiny 
as GBS allocations. 
6.1.8 Recommendation: He Committeeis of theconsidered view that the Ministry may 
wish to include a comprehensive annexure on the IEBR of Major Port Authorities in future 
Demands for Grants documentation, furnishing project-wise allocations, expenditure, and 
outcome indicators, so as to provide Parliament with a complete picture of public and quasi-
public investment in maritime infrastructure. 
6.1.9 Observation 5 — Settlement of Audit Observations: Fifteen CAG paras were 
settled and 14 remain pending as on 31st January 2026. All 10 PAC paras were settled. The 
continued pendency of 14 CAG paras warrants sustained institutional attention, particularly 
given the transformative scale of new schemes being operationalised. 

 
17 
 
6.1.10 Recommendation: He Committee recommends that the Ministry constitute a 
dedicated Audit Para Settlement Cell with monthly progress reviews, establishing a 
structured workflow for each pending para with clear internal accountability assigned to the 
concerned Division. 
6.2 Port Infrastructure, Connectivity and Capacity Utilisation 
6.2.1 Observation 6 — Port Capacity Utilisation and the Capacity-Throughput 
Disparity: The Committee scrutinised the Ministry's written submission and the Annual 
Report and note that 145 out of 283 berths at Major Ports are fully mechanised, handling 
approximately 78 per cent of total cargo, with a mechanisation target of 93 per cent by 2030. 
The written submission discloses that cargo handling capacity at Major Ports has increased 
from 800.52 MTPA in 2013-14 to 1,681 MTPA in 2024-25 (an increase of 110 per cent) and 
berth productivity has risen from 12,468 tonnes in 2013-14 to 18,304 tonnes in 2024-25 (47 
per cent increase). Total national port handling capacity, including other-than-major ports, 
has risen from 1,400 MTPA to 2,771 MTPA. Notwithstanding these gains, aggregate cargo 
throughput of 854.86 MMT at Major Ports in 2024-25, when juxtaposed against 57 PPP 
projects contributing approximately 660 MTPA of additional capacity, indicates that a 
significant proportion of installed capacity remains dormant. The Chairman of Paradip Port 
Authority confirmed during oral evidence that capacity utilisation at Major Ports is 
approximately 50-60 per cent. This structural under-utilisation represents both an 
opportunity cost and a challenge to the investment rationale underlying further large-scale 
capacity additions such as Vadhvan (Rs. 76,220 crore) and ICTP Great Nicobar (Rs. 99,000 
crore). 
6.2.2 Recommendation: He Committee recommends that the Ministry commission a 
comprehensive Port Capacity Utilisation Audit across all 12 Major Ports, disaggregated by 
berth type, cargo category, and connectivity modality, to identify the specific bottlenecks 
that account for the under-utilisation. Such an audit, if integrated with the Port Master 
Planning process, would enable targeted interventions that improve throughput on existing 
infrastructure before committing to further capacity creation. 
6.2.3 Observation 7 — Last-mile Road and Rail Connectivity and Logistics Cost 
Reduction: The Committee noted from the Annual Report that the Sagarmala Port 
Connectivity pillar encompasses last-mile rail and road enhancements for faster evacuation. 
The Haridaspur-Paradip Railway Company Ltd., an SPV, is operational and yielding 
dividends. IPRCL provides rail evacuation at several Major Ports. The Committee also 
observed that a consolidated assessment of port-wise connectivity status, classifying each 
Major Port's road and rail linkage as adequate, under construction, or deficient, was not 
placed before the Committee. During oral evidence, the Ministry was unable to state the 
number of Major Ports with 100 per cent modular ship-to-rail transfer connectivity and 
committed to furnish the details in writing. The absence of such an assessment impedes a 
systematic evaluation of whether the single largest structural impediment to port utilisation, 
namely evacuation infrastructure, is being addressed with adequate priority and 

 
18 
 
coordination with the Ministry of Road Transport and Highways and the Ministry of 
Railways. 
6.2.4 Recommendation: He Committee recommends that the Ministry should prepare a 
comprehensive Port Connectivity Scorecard for each Major Port, benchmarking road (lane 
count, NHAI status), rail (last-mile connectivity, DFC linkage), and waterway access against 
a defined adequacy standard. The Ministry may further wish to explore modular ship-to-rail 
transfer facilities at high-volume ports as a mechanism for reducing container dwell time 
and transshipment costs, directly addressing the logistics cost imperative. 
6.2.5 Observation 8 — JNPA-Dedicated Freight Corridor Connectivity: The 
Committee noted from the oral evidence that JNPA, India's largest container port handling 
approximately 10 million TEUs, has not yet been connected to the Dedicated Freight 
Corridor. The Ministry's Additional Secretary submitted that JNPA has completed its yard 
infrastructure but that the Railway portion has been delayed due to land issues, with a 
revised timeline of April 2026. A Member noted that the DFC was connected to Mundra in 
Gujarat, contributing to Mundra's rising market share relative to JNPA. The Committee 
observes that the absence of DFC connectivity to JNPA over a prolonged period has had 
significant implications for the port's competitiveness and for logistics costs on the western 
corridor. 
6.2.6 Recommendation: He Committee recommends that the Ministry pursue the JNPA-
DFC connection as a matter of the highest priority and furnish a status report to the 
Committee. The Committee further recommend that the Ministry undertake a logistic cost-
impact assessment of the DFC connectivity delay on JNPA's competitive position vis-a-vis 
other western coast ports. 
6.2.7 Observation 9 — Vessel Turnaround Time and Operational Efficiency: The 
Ministry, in its written submission, records that average turnaround time at Major Ports has 
improved from 93.6 hours in 2013-14 to 49.5 hours in 2024-25, a reduction of 47 per cent. 
The Committee note from the oral evidence that the Special Secretary cited an average of 
28.5 hours, which appears to use a different measurement methodology. Nevertheless, the 
Annual Report discloses that turnaround times continue to vary widely across ports, from 
approximately 26.35 hours at JNPA to over 81.73 hours at SMP Kolkata, and that the 
container vessel turnaround time was 41.76 hours. SMP Kolkata has commenced night 
navigation in the upper Hooghly to reduce turnaround and pre-berthing delays. The 
Committee observes that turnaround time is a composite indicator encompassing berth 
availability, crane productivity, documentation clearance, pilotage, and evacuation 
infrastructure, and that the wide variance across ports suggests that best practices are not 
being systematically disseminated. 
6.2.8 Recommendation: He Committee recommends that the Ministry establish Port 
Turnaround Time Reduction Targets for each Major Port, benchmarked against 
international comparators, with specific interventions mapped to each component of the 
turnaround cycle. The Ministry may further wish to standardise the measurement 
methodology for turnaround time across all reporting platforms. The ONOP initiative, by 

 
19 
 
standardising documentation and reducing transaction volumes, provides a strong platform; 
turnaround time tracking may be integrated into the ONOP digital architecture so as to 
generate real-time port performance dashboards. 
6.2.9 Observation 10 — Berth Mechanisation and PPP Award Rates: The 
Committee note that 49 PPP projects worth Rs. 51,093 crore have been approved since 2019-
20, of which only 25 projects valued at Rs. 13,355 crore (26 per cent of investment value) 
have been awarded. The 24 unawarded projects, representing Rs. 37,738 crore, constitute a 
significant pipeline blockage. The Committee observes that where berth mechanisation is the 
binding constraint on throughput, a finding consistent with the wide turnaround time 
variance noted in Observation 9, the pace of PPP award directly constrains the capacity 
utilisation improvement. 
6.2.10 Recommendation: He Committeeis of theconsidered view that the Ministry may 
wish to conduct a diagnostic review of the 24 approved-but-unawarded PPP projects, 
identifying patterns in bidding failures, investor reluctance, or concession design 
shortcomings. A Berth Modernisation Programme that sequences PPP awards by 
productivity impact, prioritising ports where mechanisation would yield the greatest 
improvement in berth-day output, would constitute a structured response. 
6.2.11 Observation 11 — Sagarmala Programme Implementation Pace: As 
examined from the ministries documents, 839 Sagarmala projects with estimated 
investment of Rs. 5.79 lakh crore, 277 (33 per cent) have been completed after 
approximately a decade. The 353 projects (Rs. 2.75 lakh crore) still at the planning stage

6.3 Shipbuilding, Ship Repair, Recycling, and Dredging 
6.3.1 Observation 13 — Shipbuilding Capacity Gap and Indian Ownership 
Deficit: The Committee scrutinised the Ministry's depositional notes and noted that India's 
annual shipbuilding output of 0.1 million GT constitutes less than 1 per cent of global 
capacity. Only 5 per cent of India's EXIM cargo is carried by Indian-owned or Indian-flagged 
vessels, and Indian-built ships account for merely 7.63 per cent of Indian-owned ships. 
Against comparators such as China (33 million GT), Korea (25 million GT), and Japan (16 
million GT), India's position is marginal. The Ministry's written submission confirms that 
Indian shipyards face a 25-30 per cent cost disadvantage due to higher financing costs, 
limited economies of scale, and gaps in advanced technology. The employment multiplier of 
6.4 and the investment multiplier of 1.82 underscore the sector's economic significance, yet 
the forty-five-fold expansion required to achieve the MAKV 2047 target of 4.5 million GT 
represents a challenge of unprecedented magnitude. 
6.3.2 Recommendation: He Committee recommends that the Ministry may develop a 
transparent order-flow projection framework under SBFAS that provides a region-wise and 
capacity-wise assessment of shipbuilding opportunities, including the anticipated share of 
eastern India's shipyards (GRSE, Hooghly-CSL) and the southern facilities (CSL, Goa 
Shipyard). The National Shipbuilding Mission, once constituted, may design a dedicated 
MSME Vendor Development Programme to map supply chain ecosystems around major 
yards, identify gaps, and create a searchable digital registry of qualified vendors to facilitate 
local sourcing and employment generation. 
6.3.3 Observation 14 — National Shipbuilding Mission: Constitution and 
Mandate: The NSbM, designated as the institutional response to coordinate SBFAS 
implementation, has not yet been formally constituted despite Rs. 515 crore having been

all indicators. The Ministry's written submission records that the India Ship Technology 
Centre is to be established under IMU with a proposed financial outlay of Rs. 305 crore to 
serve as the apex body for indigenous design capabilities, shared ship-design tools, 
specialised software, high-end computing, and R&D support. ISTC is similarly at the 
conceptual stage. The Committee observes that the absence of the nodal coordinating body 
creates a governance vacuum at precisely the moment when the three largest new schemes 
in the Demand are seeking to commence operations. 

 
21 
 
6.3.4 Recommendation: He Committee recommends that the Ministry expedite the

6.3.5 Observation 15 — Container Manufacturing Scheme and Coastal Cargo

6.8 Maritime Resilience and Trade Diversification 
6.8.1 Observation 38 — Maritime Chokepoint Vulnerability and India’s Trade 
Resilience Architecture: The Committee note that the escalation of security risks in the 
Red Sea, Gulf of Aden, and Strait of Hormuz since 28 February 2026 has tested the 
resilience of India’s maritime trade and energy supply architecture. India, as the world’s 
third-largest crude oil importer with an import dependency of approximately 88 per cent, is 
among the nations most exposed to chokepoint disruptions. A substantial proportion of 
India’s crude oil, LPG, and LNG imports originate from Gulf suppliers whose shipments 
transit the Strait of Hormuz. The disruption has resulted in vessel rerouting via the Cape of 
Good Hope, adding 10 to 14 days to transit times, elevating freight and insurance costs, and 
placing pressure on export competitiveness, particularly for low-margin merchandise. The 
Committee observe, however, that India’s proactive diversification strategy has yielded 
demonstrable results: approximately 70 per cent of crude imports are now routed from 
sources outside the Strait of Hormuz, up from 55 per cent previously, and India now imports 
crude from approximately 40 countries. Domestic refineries continue to operate at high 
utilisation levels. The Directorate General of Shipping has established a 24-hour control 
room to monitor Indian-flagged vessels operating in the Persian Gulf region. India’s 
combined petroleum storage capacity, including Strategic Petroleum Reserves and 
commercial stocks held by Oil Marketing Companies, provides a buffer of approximately 74 
days. While these measures demonstrate institutional preparedness, he Committeeis of 
theconsidered view that the structural exposure of India’s maritime trade to chokepoint risks 
warrants a systematic, forward-looking framework that converts crisis-response measures 
into permanent resilience architecture. 
6.8.2 Recommendation: He Committee recommends that the Ministry of Ports, Shipping 
and Waterways, in coordination with the Ministry of Commerce and Industry, the Ministry 
of Petroleum and Natural Gas, the Ministry of External Affairs, and the Directorate General 
of Shipping, develop and institutionalise a Maritime Resilience and Trade Diversification 
Framework (MRTDF) as a permanent component of India’s maritime policy architecture. 
The Committee note with appreciation the Government’s decisive measures in diversifying 
crude sourcing and securing alternative supply chains. Building upon these gains, the 
Framework may encompass the following pillars: 
(a) Targeted Cost Relief and Trade Facilitation: He Committee recommends 
calibrated and time-bound support measures to shield exporters from transient cost 
escalation. These may include partial underwriting of war-risk premiums for Indian-flagged 
vessels through a dedicated Maritime Risk Pool operated by SMFCL; extension of export 

 
30 
 
obligation compliance timelines for EPCG and Advance Authorisation holders affected by 
rerouting delays; facilitation of enhanced working capital limits through coordination with 
trade finance institutions; and a structured relief framework for port-related charges on 
delayed cargo, including demurrage waivers and container detention fee adjustments at 
Major Ports. 
(b) Structural Rerouting and Alternative Maritime Corridors: He Committee 
recommends that the Ministry facilitate a structured alternate routing preparedness protocol 
for Indian shipping operators and port authorities. This may include pre-negotiated berthing 
and bunkering arrangements at key transshipment hubs including Colombo, Singapore, and 
Cape Town; bilateral maritime logistics agreements with strategic partner nations to ensure 
priority access during disruptions; and capacity assessment of India’s own transshipment-
capable ports, particularly Vizhinjam (under commissioning), Vadhvan, and ICTP Great 
Nicobar, to reduce dependence on foreign transshipment hubs in the medium term. The 
Committee further note that multimodal alternatives such as the International North-South 
Transport Corridor (INSTC) and the India-Middle East-Europe Economic Corridor (IMEC) 
could partly de-risk maritime chokepoint concentration, and recommend that the Ministry 
coordinate with the Ministry of External Affairs on the expeditious operationalisation of 
these corridors. 
(c) Energy Supply Resilience: He Committee recommends that the Ministry, in 
coordination with the Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas, support the strengthening of 
India’s energy supply resilience. The approved Phase-II expansion of Strategic Petroleum 
Reserves at Chandikhol (Odisha) and Padur (Karnataka) under Public-Private Partnership 
merits accelerated execution. The Committee further recommend that port infrastructure at 
Major Ports be assessed and, where necessary, augmented to handle increased volumes 
arising from rerouted supply chains. India’s ongoing diversification of crude sourcing to 
approximately 40 countries represents a significant strategic gain that merits continued 
policy support. Logistical preparedness for extended maritime routes, including pre-
positioning of bunkering capacity at Indian ports, may be developed as a standing 
contingency measure. 
(d) Maritime Coordination and Risk Management Mechanism: He Committee 
recommends the establishment of a coordinated mechanism under the proposed Directorate 
General of Maritime Affairs for real-time monitoring of maritime risks, integrating vessel 
tracking, coordination with the Indian Navy’s Information Fusion Centre for the Indian 
Ocean Region (IFC-IOR), and advisory dissemination to shipowners and port authorities. 
The Committee further recommend that the Ministry examine the feasibility of a dedicated 
Marine War Risk Insurance Pool for Indian-flagged vessels. Given that only 5 per cent of 
India’s EXIM cargo is currently carried on Indian-flagged ships, the Committee observes 
that expanding the Indian-flag fleet through the SBFAS and MDF programmes acquires 
additional strategic significance in the context of chokepoint vulnerability, as dependence on 
foreign-flag tonnage during maritime disruptions exposes Indian trade to the routing 
decisions and insurance constraints of foreign operators. 

 
31 
 
Expected outcomes: The proposed Framework is expected to ensure continuity of 
essential trade flows during maritime disruptions, moderate cost pressures on exporters, 
strengthen energy supply resilience through expanded petroleum reserves and diversified 
sourcing, develop India’s own transshipment capacity, and embed maritime chokepoint risk 
management as a permanent institutional capability within the Ministry’s operational 
architecture.
