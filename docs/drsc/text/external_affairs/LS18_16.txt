authorized by the Committee to submit the Report on their behalf, present this

PART-I 
NARRATION ANALYSIS 
 
I. Introduction 
 
1. India-Sri Lanka relations may be explained as a relationship of 
civilizational contiguity, geographic proximity and contemporary strategic 
relevance. India is Sri Lanka’s closest neighbor and the relationship 
between the two countries is more than 2,500 years old. The enduring 
relationship between the two countries is underpinned by shared 
civilizational (Ramayana, Buddhism), linguistic (Tamil and Pali) and people-
to-people (cultural, trade and tourism) ties that span centuries.  
 
2. India-Sri Lanka bilateral relations have undergone a qualitative 
change in the recent past, particularly with the unconditional assistance 
offered by India during Sri Lanka’s economic and financial crisis of 2022. 
India’s credit assistance of over USD 4 billion and proactive intervention in 
IMF in the context of Sri Lanka’s economic crisis and sovereign default has 
had a positive impact on our bilateral relationship. India also reinforced its 
role as the First Responder to crises through the extension of immediate 
and wide-ranging assistance through Operation Sagar Bandhu as well as 
the comprehensive relief package of USD 450 million in the aftermath of 
Cyclone Ditwah in 2025. Our broad-based engagement transcends political 
affiliations in Sri Lanka and comprises multifaceted development, economic 
and defence partnership.  
 
3. The inherent affinity in the relationship naturally translates into 
avenues for modern connectivity: physical, digital, energy, economic and 
financial, as well as people-to-people linkages. The principal challenge 
remains management of sensitive issues such as that of the fishermen, 
Tamil aspirations and external strategic footprints through sustained, high-
level, institutionally anchored engagement. 
 
 
 
 

2 
 
II. India’s ‘Neighbourhood First’ Policy  
 
4. The ‘Neighbourhood First’ policy, which guides the management of 
India’s relations with countries in its immediate neighbourhood, focuses on 
creating mutually beneficial, people-oriented, regional frameworks for 
stability and prosperity, including through the building of physical, digital 
and people to people connectivity. Sri Lanka occupies a central place in 
India’s ‘Neighbourhood First’ policy and Vision MAHASAGAR (Mutual and 
Holistic Advancement for Security and Growth Across Regions). 
 
5. Regarding various initiatives taken by India to improve relationship 
with Sri Lanka  and plan to further improve bilateral relations with Sri Lanka 
under this policy, the Ministry in a written reply stated as under: 
 
“Under this approach, India has been assisting neighbouring 
countries, including Sri Lanka, on development of large scale 
infrastructure projects as well as community assets, the augmentation 
of capabilities and extension of financial, humanitarian and livelihood 
assistance as well as prompt crisis response.The majority of our 
initiatives and programmes in Sri Lanka, including those in the realms 
of connectivity across the spectrum, education, health, housing, 
livelihood, human resource development, culture and economic 
engagement are undertaken in order to enhance the quality of life at 
the individual level while our emphasis on investment-led and grant-
oriented cooperation is aimed at aiding Sri Lanka's economic 
recovery in a sustainable manner. The India-Sri Lanka Joint Vision 
Statement of July 2023 titled “Promoting Connectivity, Catalysing 
Prosperity” outlined the connectivity-driven framework agreed upon 
by the leaders in order to impart long-term direction and significant 
momentum to bilateral cooperation for growth and prosperity in both 
countries and in the wider region through enhancement of maritime, 
air, energy, trade, digital and people-to-people connectivity. The Joint 
Statement titled “Fostering Partnerships for a Shared Future” 
launched during the State Visit of President Anura Kumara 
Disanayaka to India in December 2024 also emphasized frequent 
political exchanges, enhanced development partnership in 

3 
 
accordance with the needs and priorities of the Government of Sri 
Lanka, expansion of capacity building initiatives, strategic shift from 
debt-driven models towards investment led partnerships, harness 
economic complementarity through enhanced connectivity, ensuring 
energy security, support for Sri Lanka’s efforts towards digitization, 
promote innovation and technology, enhanced strategic and defence 
cooperation in recognition of shared security interests and common 
regional security challenges.” 
 
III. Mutual and Holistic Advancement for Security and Growth 
Actions Regions 
 
6. India has fostered strong bilateral and multilateral partnerships with 
key partners and like-minded countries in the Indo-Pacific and beyond to 
ensure Security and Growth for all in the Region (SAGAR). Expanding on 
this strategic vision, Hon’ble Prime Minister outlined the MAHASAGAR i.e. 
"Mutual and Holistic Advancement for Security and Growth Across 
Regions” in March 2025 in Mauritius. While SAGAR focuses on security 
and growth within the Indian Ocean Region (IOR), MAHASAGAR expands 
this vision to cover the broader Global South, encompassing the ideas of 
mutual growth, development and capacity building for sustainable growth 
and mutual security for a shared future. 
 
7. When the Committee enquired about how the MAHASAGAR doctrine 
has been of mutually beneficial to both the countries and the plans of the 
Ministry to improve bilateral relationship with Sri Lanka under this doctrine, 
the Ministry in a written reply submitted as under:  
 
“In respect of Sri Lanka, the Vision’s practical content lies in maritime 
security, disaster response, resilient infrastructure, trade and 
investment-led development, capacity building and cooperative 
security. In line with the same, India launched ‘Operation Sagar 
Bandhu’ to assist Sri Lanka in its rescue and relief efforts in the 
immediate aftermath of ‘Cyclone Ditwah’ in December 2025. Further, 
India announced a USD 450 million comprehensive reconstruction 
and rehabilitation package for the benefit of Sri Lanka focusing on 

4 
 
restoring connectivity, and enhancing capabilities in the disaster 
preparedness, health and education, agriculture and housing 
domains.” 
 
IV. Political exchanges 
 
8. India-Sri Lanka political relations are marked by regular high-level 
exchanges, which is reflective of the strong ties between the two countries 
and the importance placed by the leadership on the relationship. Prime 
Minister of India has visited Sri Lanka four times since 2015, with the last 
visit in April 2025. External Affairs Minister Dr. S. Jaishankar too has visited 
Sri Lanka on multiple occasions since 2019. The current Sri Lankan 
President Anura Kumara Disanayaka (AKD) chose India for his first foreign 
visit in December 2024 and again in February 2026 to attend the AI Impact 
Summit. Indian Prime Minister was the first foreign leader to be received by 
President Disanayaka in April 2025. Several Sri Lankan Ministers have 
visited India on multiple occasions for bilateral and multilateral matters. 
Around 65 Sri Lankan Parliamentarians have visited India since 2025 on 
familiarization visits organized by Parliamentary Research and Training 
Institute for Democracies (PRIDE). These exchanges have sustained the 
momentum of our bilateral ties and have served to advance projects in 
priority areas identified during high-level visits. The details of high-level 
visits and MoUs signed since 2019 are at Annexure I and II respectively. 
 
V. Trade and Commerce

with merchandise trade amounting to USD 7.18 billion in FY 2025-26, with 
India’s exports at USD 5.52 billion and Sri Lanka’s exports at USD 1.62 
billion. India has also resumed negotiations with Sri Lanka for finalization of 
the Economic and Technology Cooperation Agreement (ETCA) covering 
both goods and services, with the latest (14 th) round of talks held in July 
2024.

11. Bilateral trade remains structurally asymmetrical in India's favour, 
largely because Sri Lanka imports petroleum products, vehicles, 
machinery, textiles and other intermediate / final goods from India, while Sri 

6 
 
Lanka's export basket to India is narrower and concentrated in items such 
as ships/boats, agricultural products, spices, residues/waste from food 
industries and selected industrial goods.  
 
12. Joint Secretary, Ministry of External Affairs during the sitting of the 
Committee on 4th August 2026 submitted as under: 
 
“Also, to address the point that we need to help Sri Lanka boost its 
exports, there is an idea that we should look at areas where there is 
complementarity. Frankly, items such as spices, tea, apparel and 
coffee are being exported by both India and Sri Lanka. If we can work 
together to find some synergy and bring our value chains and supply 
chains together, there is scope for greater cooperation. Our imports 
from Sri Lanka essentially comprise primary products, mainly fruits, 
nuts, coffee, tea and apparel, as well as some components of ships 
and boats. But primarily, the composition consists of these products. 
That is where they are dependent on exports to us, and that is why 
their exports to us are only $1.6 billion. However, that has actually 
increased mani-fold in the last few years, and there is an appreciation 
on the Sri Lankan side that we have allowed, helped and facilitated 
this.” 
 
13. To address the trade imbalance, efforts are underway to expand Sri 
Lankan export capacity, improve utilisation of the India-Sri Lanka Free 
Trade Agreement (ISFTA), address rules-of-origin and non-tariff issues, and 
conclude a modernised framework such as Economic and Technology 
Cooperation Agreement (ETCA) covering services, trade facilitation and 
investment. The existing ISFTA is goods-focused; ETCA would add 
contemporary disciplines and thereby deepen India's role as Sri Lanka's 
primary economic partner while giving Sri Lankan services and firms greater 
access to opportunities and markets in India. 
 
14. So far, 14 rounds of negotiations have been held on the ETCA. 
These discussions also serve to provide an opportunity to take up 
implementation related issues with respect to the India Sri Lanka Free 
Trade Agreement and address concerns of Indian exporters. It is 

7 
 
understood that the current Government of Sri Lanka is currently 
undertaking a review of all its FTAs and is yet to appoint a chief negotiator 
for the ETCA. 
 
15. Prominent commodities of exports from India and Sri Lanka include 
certain products such as spices, tea, coffee, apparel etc. Other products 
include gems &jewelry, rubber, coconut-based products etc. While no 
formal mechanism exists to promote joint exports of such products, on-
going initiatives to increase connectivity and improve mutual market access 
are expected to integrate and make more efficient the supply chains 
between the countries, making cooperation for export of these items a 
natural progression. 
 
16.  To support the economic partnership, there has been an emphasis 
placed on enhancing multi-modal connectivity, including by resumption of 
air link between Chennai and Jaffna (December 2022), commencement of 
ferry services between Nagapattinam and Kankesanthurai (October 2023), 
fintech connectivity through launch of UPI services (February 2024) as well 
as through the development of allied sectors such as tourism by developing 
Ramayana trial, as well as Buddhist pilgrimage trail. 
 
17. A non-official witness during the sitting of the Committee on 30 th April 
2026 submitted as under: 
 
“On the economic cooperation side, India was, I believe, among the 
first countries with which Sri Lanka signed a Free Trade Agreement. 
Similarly, for India as well, one of its early FTAs was with Sri Lanka in 
1998, which came into effect in 2000.  It is, of course, less than 
perfect, as we have learned over time, especially after the South 
Asian Free Trade Area came into effect in 2006.  However, the Sri 
Lankan business community has certain grievances.  Their complaint 
is that products of significant importance to them are either not 
covered under the FTA or are subject to quotas.  For example, spices 
– particularly pepper – were brought under such restrictions after the 
agreement came into effect, and this remains a sore point for them.”

VI. Foreign Direct Investment 
 
18. India is one of the largest sources of both Foreign Direct Investment 
(FDI) into Sri Lanka (USD 2.94 billion till 2025) as well as tourist traffic 
(accounting for about 1/4th of tourists) into Sri Lanka. We have recently 
moved to a grant assistance-based and investment-led collaboration model 
to reduce Sri Lanka’s debt burden and to increase economic inter-linkages 
with India. In this spirit, we have committed to support modernization of the 
Kankesanthurai (KKS) Port in Northern Sri Lanka through a grant of USD 
61.5 million; extended grant assistance of USD 20 million to cover for 
payments for 07 completed Line of Credit projects; and announced gifting 
of 22 diesel locomotives to Sri Lankan Railways at their request. 
 
19. To a query of the Committee on how India’s FDI helped Sri Lanka in 
strengthening its economy, the following reply has been furnished by the 
Ministry:  
 
“As per the most recent data, cumulative FDI from India to Sri Lanka 
was estimated at 2.79 USD Bn in 2025.Country-wise cumulative 
investment data as of December 2025 is as follows:

20. Indian investments in Sri Lanka cover a variety of sectors such as 
ports, hospitality, manufacturing, shipbuilding etc. In the recent past, 
delegations of NASSCOM, CII etc have visited Sri Lanka to explore 
opportunities for investment. Recently, the Government of Sri Lanka has 
also sought Expressions of Interest for investment through PPP in sectors 

9 
 
such as refinery, minerals and airport. Indian investments are being 
encouraged by the Mission in these sectors. 
 
21. These investments in critical sectors such as infrastructure and 
hospitality have been major source of employment while augmenting Sri 
Lanka’s capacity and productivity in strategic areas. The Government of 
India's role is facilitative: de-risking through inter-governmental 
understandings, Mission-led business outreach, and ensuring projects are 
consistent with Sri Lanka's priorities and India's security sensitivities. 
 
22. The policy decision to move to an investment-led collaboration model 
to reduce Sri Lanka’s debt burden and to increase economic inter-linkages 
with India is also designed to make the Sri Lankan economy more robust 
and its recovery sustainable over the long-run. This can already be seen to 
have effect as the Sri Lankan economy moves from recovery to stability 
and is the path to growth. 
 
VII. Private sector investments 
 
23. India’s large private sector played an important role in promoting 
investment in Sri Lanka. Recent notable private investments by Indian 
companies in Sri Lanka include: 
 
 Acquisition of Colombo Dockyard PLC by Mazagon Dock 
Shipbuilders Ltd. (MDL) (Port Sector): Colombo Dockyard PLC is Sri 
Lanka’s largest shipbuilding and ship-repair company. MDL has 
acquired 51% stake in Colombo Dockyard PLC in 2026. The value of 
investment is approx USD 26.8 million. 
 
 ITC Ratnadipa (Hospitality Sector): The project is considered one of 
the largest Indian hospitality investments ever made in Sri Lanka. The 
total value of investment for this project is estimated as USD 522 
million, which was completed in 2024. 
 
 CEAT Camso Deal (Automobile Sector): CEAT Limited finalized its 
acquisition of Michelin's Camso Off-Highway construction equipment 

10 
 
tire and track business on September 1, 2025, for roughly $225 
million. The deal includes acquiring two manufacturing facilities in Sri 
Lanka—a tire factory in Midigama and a casting plant in Kotugoda—
and provides global ownership of the Camso brand. 
 
 Colombo West Container Terminal: The West Container Terminal 
(WCT) at the Colombo Port is being developed by the Colombo West 
International Container Terminal (Private) Limited (CWIT) which has 
51% ownership by Adani Ports, 34% by John Keells Holdings PLC 
(JKH) with remaining 15% is held by the Sri Lanka Ports Authority 
(SLPA).The overall cost of the project is expected to be around US$ 
750 million with a debt to equity mix of about 70:30.

IX. Connectivity Projects 
 
(i) Maritime Connectivity 
 
27. The passenger ferry service between Nagapattinam in India and 
Kankesanthurai (KKS) near Jaffna in the Northern Province of Sri Lanka, 
which was earlier launched in October 2023, has resumed since 16 August, 
2024. This is significant because it restores a people-centric sea link after a 
long gap and reduces travel friction for pilgrims, tourists, families, students 
and small traders. Government of India has been extending Viability Gap 
Funding (VGF) since the start of the service. While the service is 
temporarily discontinued during adverse weather conditions of the North 
East Monsoon months, occupancy and passenger interest in respect of the 
service has been steadily increasing since inception.  
 
28. In terms of the proposed Rameshwaram–Talaimannar ferry service, 
necessary infrastructure at both ends is required for commencement of 
service in this sector. This route has high symbolic and economic value 
because it would link historically connected coastal communities and 
potentially integrate pilgrimage and tourism circuits. On the Sri Lankan 
side, the Sri Lanka Ports Authority (SLPA) is working towards developing

Observations/Recommendations 
 
1.   India is Sri Lanka’s closest neighbour and the relationship 
between the two countries is more than 2,500 years old, underpinned 
by shared civilizational (Ramayana, Buddhism), linguistic (Tamil and 
Pali), and people-to-people (cultural, trade and tourism) ties.  Sri Lanka 
also occupies a central place in India’s ‘Neighbourhood First’ policy 
and MAHASAGAR (Mutual and Holistic Advancement for Security and 
Growth Across Regions) doctrine.  While Neighbourhood First Policy 
guides the management of India’s relations with countries in its 
immediate neighbourhood, MAHASAGAR expands this vision to cover 
the broader Global South, encompassing the ideas of mutual growth, 
development and capacity building for sustainable growth and mutual 
security for a shared future.  The Committee are glad to note that India 
has traditionally played the role of a Net Security Provider and a first 
responder in times of economic crisis and environmental disasters in 
Sri Lanka.The Committee have selected the subject ‘India-Sri Lanka 
Relations and the way ahead’, to understand the importance of 
maintaining close ties between the two countries shaped by shared 
civilizational, cultural, and historical relationship and the strategic 
relevance of Sri Lanka for a country like India. 
The Committee note that strong policy frameworks have been 
laid down by the Government of India to promote a mutually beneficial, 
people-oriented regional framework for stability and prosperity, 
maritime security, disaster response, resilient infrastructure, etc. in the 
region.  Under these policy initiatives, the Committee are glad to note 
that India-Sri Lanka bilateral relations have undergone a qualitative 

51 
 
change in the recent past, particularly with the unconditional 
assistance offered by India during Sri Lanka’s economic and financial 
crisis of 2022, extension of immediate and wide-ranging assistance 
through operation Sagar Bandhu as well as the comprehensive relief 
package of USD 450 million in the aftermath of cyclone Ditwah in 2025.  
While appreciating the Government for taking various path breaking 
measures which have also generated tremendous goodwill for India, 
the effectiveness of ‘Neighbourhood First’ Policy and MAHASAGAR 
doctrine to address legacy issues between India and Sri Lanka, such as 
implementation of 13 th Amendment, increasing footprints of third 
countries, delay in the implementation of various projects, fear of 
domination by India, fishermen issues around Katchatheevu, 
repatriation of refugees, increasing trade imbalances, etc. remains to 
be seen. 
The Committee desire that for effective implementation of these 
grand policy initiatives, strong and vibrant institutional framework 
should also exist, both at governmental and non-governmental levels, 
to promote greater cooperation and dialogue between the two 
countries.  In this regard, the Committee recommend that the existing 
institutional frameworks, such as India-Sri Lanka Joint Commission, 
Foreign Office Consultations, Annual Defence Dialogue, Colombo 
Security conclave, Joint Working Group on Fisheries, etc. should meet 
more regularly, with clearly defined agenda, so as to bring better 
understanding and promote India-Sri Lanka relation in the spirit and 
the vision envisaged by India’s Neighbourhood First Policy and 
MAHASAGAR doctrine and  ensure time bound implementation of the 
outcomes. The Committee also recommend the Ministry to make efforts 
to successfully leverage existing regional platforms, such as Indian 

52 
 
Ocean Rim Association (IORA), of which India is currently the chair, 
and Bay of Bengal Initiative for Multi-Sectoral Technical and Economic 
cooperation (BIMSTEC) to strengthen cooperation between the two

challenges of terrorism, drug and human trafficking, cybercrimes, etc.

Trade and Commerce 
3. The Committee note that India has been Sri Lanka’s largest 
trading partner. The total bilateral trade between India and Sri Lanka 
has reached USD 7.18 billion in Financial Year 2025-26.  India’s export 
stood at USD 5.52 billion, while Sri Lanka’s export to India was USD 
1.66 billion.  Bilateral trade remains structurally asymmetrical in India’s 
favour, largely because Sri Lanka imports petroleum products, 
vehicles, machinery, textiles and other intermediate/final goods from 
India, while Sri Lanka’s export basket to India is narrower and 
concentrated on items, such as ships/boats, agricultural products, 
spices, residues/waste from food industries, etc.  The Committee are of 
the view that this trade imbalance need to be addressed at the earliest 
by increasing Sri Lankan exports to India and other countries.  
 In this regard, the Committee urge the Ministry to take 
necessary measures to remove all the primary impediments to India-Sri 
Lanka trade, including addressing rules of origin and non-tariff barriers

efforts to improve its economy which will prove to be mutually 
beneficial.

of Sri Lanka Defence Forces, various training programmes are being 
provided by India to Sri Lankan civil servants and professionals, such 
as training of 1500 Sri Lankan civil services officers over a period of 
five years (300 per year) as per MoU signed during the State visit of Sri 
Lankan President to India in December 2024, customized training slots 
for 700 Sri Lankan professionals on an annual basis as announced by 
Prime Minister of India during his State visit to Sri Lanka in April, 2025.  
These training programmes are being carried out as per schedule.  Sri 
Lanka is also allocated 402 training slots annually under the ITEC 
programme.  The Committee also note that the Government of India is 
also providing fully funded ICCR scholarship annually to more than 200 
Sri Lankan students.  Besides scholarship in India, Government of 
India also provides financial assistance to meritorious Sri Lanka 
students at University of Jaffna and Eastern University to foster 
goodwill among the Sri Lankan Student Community.  
From the above observations, the Committee note that India is 
actively participating in the capacity and human resources 

88 
 
development in Sri Lanka, so as to bring about rapid and 
transformational change in the governance of the country and better 
delivery of welfare services to the people. The Committee desire that 
the Ministry may continue providing training to Sri Lankan Civil 
servants and professionals in India even after the completion of the 
current programmes, based on the request received from Sri Lankan 
Government.  The Committee are convinced that providing much 
needed training programme ranging across public administration, IT, 
banking and finance to emerging technologies in premier Indian 
Institutes will certainly help in capacity building of Sri Lanka officials 
and professionals and will have far reaching impact in improving the 
governance in the country.  
 
Strengthening SME Cooperation through the Disha Mentorship 
Programme 
41. The Committee note that Small and Medium Enterprises are an 
important component of Sri Lanka’s economy and that stronger 
linkages between India and Sri Lankan business can contribute to 
deeper bilateral economic integration. In this context, the SME Disha 
mentorship programme provides an avenue for Sri Lankan SMEs to 
benefit from Indian expertise and experience through mentorship and 
capacity-building. The programme was specifically raised during the 
Committee’s sitting held on 4 August 2026. Recognizing the potential 
of such initiatives to strengthen entrepreneurial capacity and business-
to-business ties, the Committee recommend that the Ministry of 
External Affairs, in coordination with relevant stakeholders, facilitate 
and expand such mentorship initiatives to enable Sri Lankan SMEs to 
benefit from Indian expertise, strengthen entrepreneurial capacity and 

89 
 
promote greater business-to- business engagement between the two 
countries. 
 
42. The Committee note that tremendous response has been 
received from the Sri Lankan side for participation in ITEC courses, 
which indicates the relevance of various courses offered under ITEC 
training programmes and the importance attached by Sri Lankan 
government to these courses.  During 2025-26, out of 2472 applications 
received, only 714 participants successfully attended and completed 
various ITEC programmes in India.  Considering that ITEC is an 
important pillar of India-Sri Lanka development cooperation, the 
Committee recommend the Ministry to increase the training slots 
annually allocated to Sri Lanka to enhance professional capacity 
building.

3. Thereafter, Ambassador (Retd.) Y.K. Sinha briefed the Committee covering three broad 
areas of importance in the bilateral relations between India and Sri Lanka viz. political relations, 
economic cooperation and connectivity issues.  With regard to the political relations between the 
two countries, the major issueshighlighted were related tononimplementation of 13 th 
Amendment due to such factors as largely unitary system of governance in Sri Lanka, Sri 
Lankan Supreme Court’s ruling against unification of Northern and Eastern provinces and Sri 
Lankan Government’sreluctance for grant of police power to the provinces. However, he pointed 
out that with the National People’s Party (NPP) in power there is an opportunity to resolve these 
issues through dialogue and outreach.  On economic relations, he highlighted the issues of 
signing of Free Trade Agreement between the two countries, grievances of Sri Lankan business 
community, concerns of Sri Lankan trade unions regarding non tariff barriers, etc.On 
connectivity, he underscored the importance of physical connectivity, digital, and economic grid 
connectivity between the two countries for promotion of trade, commerce, investment and 
movement of professionals and pilgrims. 
 
4. Shri Gopal Baglay, Amb (Retd) briefed the Committee on the recent economic and
