REPORT 
Financial accessibility in higher education in India plays a pivotal role in bridging socio-
economic gaps and enabling millions of students to pursue advanced studies, despite rising costs of 
tuition, accommodation, and related expenses. Due to increasing young population and rapidly 
evolving economy, financial accessibility through educational loans in higher education is essential for 
bridging socio-economic gaps and fostering equitable opportunities, enabling credit-constrained 
students to pursue advanced studies without upfront financial burdens that could otherwise deter 
enrollment or lead to dropouts. These loans serve as a critical alternative to traditional Government 
funding, which has seen shifts toward cost-sharing models, allowing individuals from diverse 
backgrounds to invest in human resource development and contribute to national growth by building a 
skilled workforce. Justification for financial accessibility in higher education and cheaper education 
loans lies in their role as game-changers that promote financial independence, offer benefits like lower 
interest rates, no collateral requirements for meritorious applicants, and the building of credit history, 
ultimately unlocking potential for personal and career advancement while aligning with policies aimed 
at making higher education inclusive and sustainable. 
1.2 With a burgeoning youth population and increasing demand for skilled professionals, the 
Government has prioritized initiatives to make higher education affordable and inclusive. However, 
challenges such as high interest rates on loans, collateral requirements, and limited awareness persist, 
particularly for students from low-income families. To address these, the Department of Higher 
Education, Ministry of Education, along with the Reserve Bank of India (RBI), has taken efforts to 
implement various schemes that provide subsidies, streamlined loan processes, and digital platforms to 
facilitate access to funding.  
1.3 The Department of Higher Education administers key programs like the Central Sector Interest 
Subsidy (CSIS) Scheme, introduced in 2009, which offers full interest subsidy during the moratorium 
period on education loans up to Rs 7.5 lakh for students from economically weaker sections pursuing 
technical or professional courses. More recently, the Pradhan Mantri Vidyalaxmi (PM-Vidyalaxmi) 
Scheme, approved in November 2024, provides collateral-free and guarantor-free loans to meritorious 
students admitted to top institutions through competitive exams or merit-based processes, covering up 
to the full cost of education. These schemes are complemented by the Vidya Lakshmi Portal, a unified 
online platform where students can apply for loans from multiple banks and track applications 
seamlessly. 
1.4 The RBI, in collaboration with the Indian Banks' Association (IBA), oversees the Model 
Education Loan Scheme (MELS), revised in 2022, which standardizes loan provisions across 
scheduled commercial banks for higher studies in India and abroad. RBI's regulations ensure priority 
sector lending for education loans, mandating banks to process applications without undue delays and 
offering moratorium periods of up to one year post-course completion. These frameworks collectively 
enhance financial inclusion by reducing barriers and promoting equitable access to higher education 
opportunities. 
1.5 Despite advancements in educational financing, significant gaps persist in financial 
accessibility for higher education in India, particularly through education loans. High interest rates, 
ranging from 7.5% to 15%, inflate the overall cost of education, deterring many students from 
economically weaker sections and exacerbating repayment burdens, especially when graduates face 

2 
 
unemployment or low-paying jobs. Collateral requirements for loans exceeding Rs 4 lakh as per 
guidelines of RBI issued in April, 2010, further restrict access, as many families lack assets to pledge, 
while procedural hurdles in banks, including discrimination based on gender, region, or discipline, 
compound these issues. Lack of awareness about loan schemes is rampant, particularly in rural areas 
where least number of students are informed, leading to underutilization and perpetuating socio-
economic inequalities. Regional disparities are evident, with Southern States dominating loan 
disbursements, while Northern States such as Uttar Pradesh and Bihar lag behind, highlighting uneven 
distribution and outreach. 
1.6 Additional shortcomings include high default rates and the application process remains 
daunting, with complex documentation and indifferent attitudes from private banks contributing to 
inconsistent growth in loan accounts. Moreover, these financial pressures often lead to mental health 
issues and talent drain, as students grapple with debt stress without adequate support for employability 
enhancement or loan forgiveness programs. Addressing these requires collaborative reforms, including 
interest subsidies, improved financial literacy, and targeted outreach to make education loans more 
inclusive and sustainable. 
1.7 In view of the above, the Committee heard the views of Secretary, Department of Higher 
Education, Ministry of Education, Secretary, Department of Financial Service, Ministry of Finance, 
Government of India and representatives of Reserve Bank of India on 8th September, 2025 to review 
the Schemes for Education Loan and Financial Accessibility in Higher Education in India. The 
Committee also heard the representatives of State Bank India, Indian Bank, Union Bank of India, 
Canara Bank, UCO Bank, Punjab National Bank and Punjab and Sind Bank during its study visits to 
Bengaluru, Guwahati, Shimla.

2. Views of the Department of Higher Education, Ministry of Education on the Schemes for 
Education Loan and Financial Accessibility in Higher Education in India 
  
2.1 During the meeting of the Committee held on 08th September, 2025, Secretary, Department of Higher 
Education and representatives of the Department informed the Committee regarding the details of the schemes 
being run  related to Education Loan and Interest Subvention by the  Department of Higher Education, 
Ministry of Education which are as under:- 
 
(i) Pradhan Mantri Vidyalaxmi (PM Vidyalaxmi) 
(ii) Pradhan Mantri Uchchatar Shiksha Protsahan Central Sector Interest Subsidy (PM-USP 
CSIS) 
(iii) Pradhan Mantri Uchchatar Shiksha Protsahan Credit Guarantee Fund Scheme for Education 
Loans (PM-USP CGFSEL) 
 
2.2 Pradhan Mantri Vidyalaxmi (PM Vidyalaxmi) 
 
2.2.1 The Pradhan Mantri Vidya Laxmi (PM Vidyalaxmi), a new central sector scheme has 
been launched on 6th November 2024. The main objective of the scheme is to ensure that no 
student is denied the opportunity to pursue higher education due to financial constraints. Under the 
scheme, collateral-free and guarantor-free education loans are provided to students of all the states 
and union territories who get merit-based admission in Quality Higher Education Institutions 
(QHEIs) and desirous of availing education loan to pursue higher education from these QHEIs.  
2.2.2 The NIRF 2024 ranking has been considered to prepare the list of QHEIs for academic year 
2024-25 based on the following criteria: 
 Top 100 ranked Higher Education Institutions (HEIs) in the overall, category-specific or 
domain-specific rankings in latest list of NIRF published by the Ministry of Education; 
plus 
 Top 200 ranked HEIs under the governance of state/UT governments in the latest list of 
NIRF published by the Ministry of Education; plus 
 all remaining HEIs under the governance of Government of India. 
2.2.3 Further, for students with annual family income up to ₹8 lakhs, the PM-Vidyalaxmi scheme 
provides 3% interest subvention on loans up to ₹ 10 lakhs. Up to one lakh fresh students not getting 
any other scholarship or interest subvention on education loan will get this benefit. Interest subvention 
benefit is provided during moratorium period, i.e., course period plus one year. Total outlay kept for 
interest subvention from 2024-25 to 2030-2031 is ₹3,600 crore.The interest on the loans will become 
due one year from date of disbursement of loans.

2.4 Pradhan Mantri Uchchatar Shiksha Protsahan Credit Guarantee Fund Scheme for Education 
Loans (PM-USP CGFSEL) 
 
2.4.1 The Department has informed the Committee that under the Pradhan Mantri Uchchatar Shiksha 
Protsahan Credit Guarantee Fund Scheme for Education Loans (PM-USP CGFSEL) of the Central 
Government, launched in the year 2015, credit guarantee is provided for education loans sanctioned up 
to ₹ 7.5 lakhs. The guarantee cover is up to 75% of outstanding default. Under the model education 
loan scheme of the Indian Banks’ Association, the repayment period for education loan is up to 15 
years after moratorium period (course year plus one year). The credit guarantee covers the entire period 
of repayment.Number of credit guarantees issued till date is around 12.3 Lakhs.

3. Views of the Department of Financial Services (DFS), Ministry of Finance on the Schemes for 
Education Loan and Financial Accessibility in Higher Education in India:- 
3.1 The Committee during its meeting held on 8th September, 2025 heard the views of Secretary, 
Department of Financial Services (DFS), Ministry of Finance. The Department has informed the 
Committee that Government has launched various schemes and is dedicated towards making education 
loans easily available to students desirous of pursuing education in India and abroad which are given as 
under:- 
(i)  Model Education Loan for pursuing higher education in India and abroad. 
(ii) Central Sector Interest Subsidy Scheme (CSIS). 
(iii) Credit Guarantee Fund Scheme for Education Loans (CGFSEL). 
(iv) Pradhan Mantri Vidyalaxmi (PM-Vidyalaxmi) Scheme. 
 
3.1 Model Education Loan for pursuing higher education in India and abroad 
3.1.1 Department has further informed that for financing higher education for credit constrained 
students in India, Indian Banks Association (IBA) has devised “Model Education Loan Scheme for 
pursuing Higher Education in India and Abroad” in the year 2001. Since then, the scheme had been 
modified from time to time based on the experience gained and feedbacks received during its 
implementation over the years. The scheme was last amended on 21.3.2024. The main features of the 
scheme are as below: 
(a) This Scheme provides need-based finance. 
(b) No collateral security/third-party guarantee is required for loans amount up to Rs. 7.50 
lakhs (in case of loans that are eligible for CSIS and/or Credit Guarantee coverage 
extended under the `Credit Guarantee Fund Scheme for Education Loan' (CGFSEL). 
(c) No Margin for loan up to Rs 4 lakh. 
(d) Moratorium period is allowed upto study period plus one year in all cases. 
(e) Repayment period (after moratorium) is available upto 15 years for all loans. 
 
3.2 Central Sector Interest Subsidy Scheme (CSIS)  
3.2.1 Department of Financial Services, Ministry of Finance has informed the Committee that the 
Ministry of Education has launched CSIS Scheme in 2009 to ensure that no student is denied the 
opportunity to pursue higher education in professional courses because of his/her financial condition. 
The features of the scheme are as under: 
i. This scheme provides full interest subsidy during the moratorium period on loan availed under 
Model Education Loan Scheme of Indian Banks’ Association (IBA) for pursuing 
technical/professional courses in India.  
ii. Students whose annual gross parental/ family income is up to Rs.4.5 lakh are eligible under the 
scheme.  
iii. Interest Subsidy under CSIS scheme is admissible only once either for undergraduate or post 
graduate or integrated course.  

14 
 
iv. Under the scheme, interest subsidy on education loan is provided for a maximum amount of Rs. 
10 lakhs. 
3.2.2 As per the scheme guidelines applicable w.e.f. 01.04.2022, no collateral security or third-party 
guarantee is required for Education Loan sanctioned up to Rs.7.50 lakh. The lending institution is to 
ensure that this part of loan is covered for guarantee under Credit Guarantee Fund Scheme for 
Education Loan (CGFSEL). 
3.3 Credit Guarantee Fund Scheme for Education Loans (CGFSEL) 
3.3.1 As informed by DFS, Credit Guarantee Fund Scheme for Education Loans (CGFSEL) was 
launched in September 2015 to provide guarantee cover for loans up to Rs.7.5 lakh sanctioned by 
Public Sector Banks, Private Sector Banks, Foreign Banks and 27 RRBs to Indian borrowers. Interest 
rate on such loans capped at 2% over the Base rate/MCLR.  
3.3.2 Under the scheme, Guarantee Cover is 75% of amount in default. Annual Guarantee Fee is 
0.5% p.a. of outstanding amount. Total corpus approved is Rs.3,500 crore.  
3.4  Pradhan Mantri Vidyalaxmi (PM-Vidyalaxmi) Scheme 
3.4.1 The Committee was informed that the Union Cabinet on 06.11.2024 has approved PM 
Vidyalaxmi scheme under the administrative control of Department of Higher Education (DHE), which 
enables loans through banks to meritorious students so that financial constraints do not prevent any 
youth of India from pursuing quality higher education. A mission mode mechanism will facilitate and 
enable education loans to meritorious students who get admission in the top 860 Quality Higher 
Educational Institutions in the country, which translates into a coverage to approximately 22 lakh 
students every year. The scheme enables meritorious students of these QHEIs to take collateral free, 
guarantor free education loans through a simple, transparent, student-friendly and entirely digital 
application process. A dedicated PM-Vidyalaxmi Portal is developed and maintained by Canara Bank 
(under the administrative control of DHE) to ensure overall implementation and monitoring of the 
scheme. The portal has the following features: 
 
(a) A unified portal that allows student to apply for education loan digitally. 
(b) PM-Vidyalaxmi portal allows student to upload all the required documents for sanction 
of loan. 
(c) A simple 2-page application format for applying education loan 
(d) Facility for students to apply to multiple banks through common application for 
education loans; 
(e) Facility for students to track the status of submitted application; 
(f) Facility for banks to download various reports for effective monitoring of the scheme. 
 
3.4.2 The scheme also provides for 3% interest subvention on loans up to Rs. 10 lakh to a maximum 
of one lakh needy students in a year, where annual family income is less than Rs. 8 lakhs. This implies 
that, students who are not availing the benefits under CSIS or any other Central /State Government 
Scholarship or any other interest subvention scheme or Fee reimbursement shall be eligible for availing 
the benefitof 3% interest subvention under PM Vidyalaxmi scheme.

3.4.3 Apart from PM Vidyalaxmi scheme, all Scheduled Commercial Banks have been advised by 
Reserve Bank of India to adopt Model Education Loan Scheme (MELS), formulated by IBA. Those 
students who are not covered under the extant PM-Vidyalaxmi scheme may apply under the MELS 
through PM-Vidyalaxmi Portal.  
3.4.4 With respect to the requirement of credit score from students/guardians, the IBA’s MELS 
provides the following: 
(a) the student borrower may not have a credit history and as such he is assumed to be

(b) In case of an adverse credit history of student, banks at their discretion may frame suitable

(c) Rejection of loan application, if any, shall be done with the concurrence of the next higher

Lenders have been advised that first time borrowers’ loan applications should not be rejected just

4.4 RBI's instructions on Education Loans- Initially guided by its circular dated April 28, 2001, 
Scheduled Commercial Banks were advised to adopt the Model Education Loan Scheme (MELS) 
formulated by the Indian Banks’ Association (IBA). Over the years, this model has been periodically 
revised, with the most recent update being MELS 2022. Further banks have been advised not to 
demand collateral security for educational loans upto ₹4lakh, as per RBI guidelines issued in April

society. The MELS 2022 of the IBA covers features such as eligibility, courses covered, quantum of 
finance, margin, security, rate of interest etc.

4.5 Credit Autonomy and Lending Policies- Credit-related matters have been largely deregulated. 
Regulated Entities (REs), including banks and NBFCs, operate under Board-approved credit policies 
that comply with overarching regulatory frameworks. RBI does not micro manage individual lending 
decisions but sets out guiding principles. As such, credit decisions are taken based on the RE’s 
commercial judgment and within the framework of agreed contractual terms between the borrower and 
lender. 
4.6 Role and Regulation of Credit Information Companies (CICs)- The Credit Information 
Companies (Regulation) Act, 2005, along with related rules and regulations from 2006, provides the 
legal foundation for the regulation of CICs. RBI’s updated Master Directions on Credit Information 
Reporting (issued January 6, 2025) aim to streamline the reporting and sharing of credit information. 
These directions enforce data standardization, safeguard borrower privacy, and facilitate effective 
grievance redressal. CICs are now required to update credit information fortnightly, provide one free 
full credit report per year to consumers, and offer mechanisms for data correction. Importantly, the RBI 
has also brought CICs under the Reserve Bank-Integrated Ombudsman Scheme, 2021, thereby 
enhancing accountability. 
4.7 Credit Scoring and Loan Sanctions- The Reserve Bank of India has not issued any guidelines/ 
instructions to its REs, such as banks and NBFCs, to sanction or reject a loan based on a certain credit 
score. To ensure that credit decisions of credit institutions (CIs) such as banks are based on credit 
information available in the system, the RBI has mandated that CIs include in their loan policies/credit 
appraisal processes suitable provisions for obtaining Credit Information Reports(CIRs) from one or 
more CICs. Lenders rely on various factors to arrive at a credit decision, and the information sourced 
from CICs forms only one of these factors. Information on past repayment history, including delayed 
repayments, Loans settled, restructured, written-off, etc., in the CIRs, is essential for assessing the 
credit worthiness of a borrower and enabling lenders to make informed credit decisions. As part of 
Best Practices for Credit Institutions, CIs have been advised that first-time borrowers’ loan applications 
should not be rejected solely because they have no credit history. Credit scoring models used by CICs 
are proprietary and based on their respective experience in the business of credit information. CICs 
generate a borrower’s credit score considering multiple factors, including but not limited to recency 
and frequency of delinquency, debt level, payment history, etc. 
4.8 Measures for credit access and borrower protection- To enhance accessibility to credit and 
improve borrower protection, the Reserve Bank of India has introduced a series of regulatory and 
supervisory measures that address systemic gaps in credit information, transparency, and fair lending 
practices. 
4.9 Credit Information Reporting and Grievance Redressal- As part of reforms in credit information 
systems, Credit Institutions (CIs) and Credit Information Companies (CICs) have been directed to 
update credit data on a fortnightly basis, ensuring that borrower profiles are current and accurate. To 
promote consistency and ease of interpretation, all CICs are required to calibrate credit scores 
uniformly on a scale of 300 to 900, facilitating better comparison and comprehension across 
institutions. Borrowers are now entitled to receive, on request and after proper authentication, one free 

20 
 
full credit report (including credit score) annually. Additionally, both CIs and CICs must have systems 
to process requests for rectification of inaccurate data. To strengthen consumer protection, RBI has 
instituted an Internal Ombudsman mechanism for CICs and extended the RBI Integrated Ombudsman 
Scheme (RBIOS), 2021 to cover grievances related to CICs. Furthermore, CICs and CIs are mandated 
to provide SMS/email alerts for credit report accessor when defaults are reported, and must conduct 
root cause analysis of complaints. A compensation framework has also been introduced to ensure 
timely resolution of complaints; if a complainant's issue is not addressed within 30 calendar days, 
compensation becomes applicable. 
4.10 Transparency through Key Facts Statement (KFS)- To empower borrowers with clear and 
complete information, RBI has mandated the issuance of a Key Facts Statement (KFS) for all retail 
and MSME term loans. As per RBI circular dated April 15, 2024 
(DOR.STR.REC.13/13.03.00/2024-25), the KFS must be provided in a language understood by the 
borrower, explained in person or digitally, and acknowledged in writing. It must include the Annual 
Percentage Rate (APR) —which aggregates all applicable charges — and a detailed amortization 
schedule. The KFS must also specify the validity period of the offer (minimum three working days 
for loans above 7 days in tenure, and one day for shorter-term loans). Charges incurred on behalf of 
third-party service providers (e.g., insurance or legal fees) must be transparently disclosed and 
supported by receipts. Importantly, no fees or charges beyond what is listed in the KFS can be 
levied at any stage without explicit borrower consent. The KFS is also required to be part of the 
loan agreement as a summary box. 
 
4.11 Fair Lending Practices- RBI continues to enforce a strong frame work of fair practices for 
both banks and Non-Banking Financial Companies (NBFCs). Under the Fair Practices Code (para 
2.5 of the Master Circular dated July 1, 2015), banks must disclose upfront all material terms 
including prepayment options and charges, penalties for delays, switching costs between floating 
and fixed interest, and the presence of interest reset clauses. This information must also be made 
available on the bank’s website. Moreover, for all loan categories, including credit cards, banks are 
instructed to communicate, in writing and within stipulated timeframes, the specific reasons for 
rejection of any application. These fair practice guidelines ensure that no borrower is denied credit 
arbitrarily or misled by opaque terms. Similar obligations have been extended to NBFCs, as 
outlined in Chapter VII of the Master Direction – Non-Banking Financial Company (Scale Based 
Regulation), 2023, issued on October 19, 2023. 
 
4.12 The representatives of RBI have also informed the Committee that credit-related matters are 
largely deregulated, with banks and NBFCs guided by their Board-approved policies within the RBI’s 
overarching regulatory framework. Lenders are not required to base credit decisions solely on credit 
scores, and first-time borrowers cannot be denied credit for lacking a credit history. While the RBI 
does not intervene in individual lending decisions, it seeks to balance prudential regulation with 
borrower protection through measures such as the Key Facts Statement, regulation of Credit 
Information Companies and strengthened grievance redressal systems.

5. OBSERVATIONS/RECOMMENDATIONS OF THE COMMITTEE 
5.1 The Committee notes that between 2014 and 2025, the number of active student loans 
fell from 23.36 lakh to 20.63 lakh in 2025. However, the total credit amount has increased steeply 
from ₹52,327 crore in 2014 to ₹1,37,474 crore in 2025, suggesting much higher borrowing per 
student due to rise in cost of higher education during recent years. The Committee expresses its 
concerns over these figures since it suggests that the accessibility of educational loans is declining 
over time, even as educational costs have risen rapidly. The Committee, therefore, recommends 
the Department of Higher Education and Department of Financial Services should take sincere 
efforts to ensure educational loans to maximum number of students of the country and all 
families Below Poverty Line (BPL) should be accorded priority in sanctioning of educational 
loans for higher education. 
5.2 The Committee observes that the Department of Higher Education has taken various 
initiatives for publicity of PM Vidyalaxmi scheme through print media, radio advertisements, 
arrangement of camps in institutions and through social media platforms. However, the 
Committee notes that majority of students from rural background, disadvantageous sections of 
the society and remote areas are perhaps not aware of the schemes being run by Government for 
education loans. The Committee also notes that Department is putting stalls and standees in 
places like IITs, IIMs, NITs and IIITsetc. The Committee feels that the Department should 
maximize its publicity among students in High Schools, Kendriya Vidyalayas, Jawahar 
Navodaya Vidyalaya, State run schools targeting students of Class 11th and 12th, so that, 
awareness can be spread among them about the schemes for education loans. Also, the 
advertisement for awareness campaign in all vernacular languages and 22 Scheduled Languages 
recognized by the Constitution. The Committee further recommends that Department of Higher 
Education, DFS, RBI, NABARD, Banks/Financial Institutions and State Governments concerned 
should work in closer coordination for awareness campaign for education loan and every 
educational institution should have education loan facilitation centres and proper counselors to 
guide students how to approach and avail facilities for education loan in an easy manner, in the 
country, particularly in rural districts to ensure loan schemes are accessible to students lacking 
internet access or formal educational support. 
5.3 The Committee takes note of the publicity of education loans schemes through social 
media i.e. Facebook, X(Twitter), Instagram. However, the Committee is constrained to note that 
reach of the publicity campaign is very low among majority of students who need education loan 
for pursuing their higher education. The Committee, therefore, recommends that the 
Department should maximize its social media publicity campaign through platforms which are 
popular among students through shorts, reels etc. 
5.4 The Committee observes that under PM Vidyalaxmi scheme the total number of 
applications for education loan received during 25.02.2025 to 31.08.2025, is 55,887 and the 
amount sanctioned is Rs. 4,427 crores. However, the number of education loan applications 
sanctioned is 30,442, number of loans disbursed is 21,967 and an amount of Rs. 688.27 crores 
only have been disbursed. These figures reflect the fact that around 15% amount has been 
disbursed against the sanctioned amount of Rs. 4,427 crores of education loan under PM 
Vidyalaxmi scheme. The Committee, therefore, recommends the Department of Financial 
Services to ensure that sanctioned loans should not be curtailed or rejected. Further, the 
financial institutions and banks should take sincere efforts to maximize the number of 
applications sanctioned against the loan applications received. 

22 
 
5.5 The Committee observes from the data given at TABLE-3 above, that several banks have 
not sanctioned even a single application for education loan under PM Vidyalaxmi scheme during 
25.02.2025 to 31.08.2025, which shows the callous approach of banks towards poor students and 
applications for education loan under PM Vidyalaxmi received there from. The Committee, 
therefore, recommends the Department of Higher Education, the Department of Financial 
Services and the Reserve Bank of India to issue strict guidelines for sanctioning, rejection and 
pendency of application for education loan under PM Vidyalaxmi and other schemes being run 
by the Government. The Committee further recommends that Department of Higher Education, 
Department of Financial Services, RBI and NABARD should issue uniform policy/guidelines in 
consultation with IBA for public sector, private, Regional Rural Banks, NBFCs etc. 
5.6 The Committee further observes from the data given at TABLE-2 above, that some 
applications for education loan under PM-Vidyalaxmi scheme have low rate of sanctioning of 
education loan and lower number of applications received in comparison to few other States. The 
Committee, therefore, recommends the Department of Higher Education, Department of 
Financial Services, RBI and NABARD should work in close coordination and there should be 
provision for mandatory district-wise dashboard for monitoring of education loan ensuring 
transparency in its sanctioning and disbursement. Further, all banks should publish district-wise 
and institution-wise data regarding loan sanction, rejection, and disbursal in near real-time. The 
dashboard should also include reasons for rejection and avenues for appeal, with targeted data 
for all States to address regional inequities. 
5.7 The Committee recommends that there should be a nodal officer in each branch of every 
bank who is dedicated to supporting applicants seeking education loans, including technical 
support in applying for these loans. There should be clear identification of these nodal officers in 
every branch, and they should be trained to support applicants. 
5.8 The Committee also recommends that a dedicated student grievance portal, integrated 
with RBI’s ombudsman platform and helpline services, should be created for education loans. 
Further, the complaint resolution timelines must be monitored and compensation frameworks 
enforced if delays exceed stipulated timeframes. 
5.9 The Committee notes that education loan under PM Vidyalaxmi scheme is allowed only 
those students who are pursuing their higher education in 902 Quality Higher Education 
Institutions (QHEIs) while the students from other institutions cannot avail this facility under 
PM Vidyalaxmi scheme. The Committee recommends that PM Vidyalaxmi scheme should be 
extended to students from institutions other than 902 Higher Education Institutions (QHEIs), in 
view of the fact that majority of higher education institutions are not covered under QHEIs. 
5.10 The Committee observes that banks have been advised not to demand collateral security 
for educational loans upto ₹4lakh, as per RBI guidelines issued in April 2010. However,   the 
Committee notes that there is many fold increase in higher education expenses since 2010 and 
ceiling of collateral security free for educational loans upto ₹4lakh by RBI is not relevant in view 
of high inflationary trends in higher educational costs, tuition fees, hostel expenses etc. The 
Committee, therefore, recommends the RBI to review its limit on collateral security free 
educational loans guidelines and revise/align it with creamy layer criteria decided by the 
Government from time to time to accommodate students from SC/ST/OBC/EWS and poorest 
section of the society to avail educational loan without collateral security. 
5.11 The Committee reiterates its observation from its 364th Report that education loans with 
limit of up to ₹20 lakh are being categorised as Priority Sector Lending (PSL) from 04.09.2020. 
However, in view of the increased cost of education and the sanction of loans without collateral 
under the PM- Vidyalaxmi scheme, the Committee reiterates that education loans of up to a limit 

23 
 
of ₹50 lakh may be considered as PSL. The Committee recommends that the Department should 
coordinate with the Ministry of Finance and other stakeholders to achieve the same. 
5.12 The Committee observes from TABLE-9 above that percentage of pending applications 
beyond 10 days under PM Vidyalaxmi scheme is 32% in private banks and 41% in cooperative 
banks and RRBs which shows that private banks, cooperative banks and RRBs are not friendly 
to students who are seeking loans under PM Vidyalaxmi scheme. The Committee, therefore, 
recommends the Department of Financial Services, RBI and NABARD to issue strict instructions 
to private banks, cooperative banks and RRBs to sanction and disburse the education loans to 
students under PM Vidyalaxmi scheme and other schemes promptly and in a reasonably time-
bound manner. Further, the accountability of bank officials who are designated to process the 
education loan application in time-bound manner be defined categorically to avoid any lapse in 
their part. 
5.13 The Committee was informed that the RBI has mandated that Public Sector Banks, 
Private Banks and NBFCs seek Credit Information Reports (CIR) of parents of students who are 
applying for educational loans under various schemes of the Government. The Committee 
observes that the majority of the country’spopulation does not have any credit history or the 
accompanying CIBIL score. The Committee was informed that the RBI’s Master Directions 
advise lenders to not reject first time borrowers' loan applications just because they have no 
credit history. However, the Committee observes that this requirement for a CIR/CIBIL score 
nevertheless poses a hurdle for this section of society given the discretion available to the bank 
officials. The Committee, therefore, recommends that categorical mandatory guidelines should 
be issued by Department of Financial Services and RBI to all Banks and Financial Institutions to 
exempt CIBIL score/CIR for families which are getting free rations on their ration cards. The 
Departments can develop alternate evaluation criteria such as parental occupation, income 
sources, or school certification to assess repayment capacity. 
5.14 The Committee also recommends that the Department of Financial Services and RBI 
should conduct periodic audits by an independent agency to ensure that credit assessment 
models are not discriminating against economically weaker or first-time borrowers, especially in 
rural belts. 
5.15 The Committee notes that the Credit Guarantee Fund Scheme for Education Loans 
(CGFSEL) introduced in September 2015 provides guarantee cover of 75% for Education loans 
with limit of up to ₹7.5 lakh. However, due to rise in education fee inflation, the resultant 
increased cost of education and the requirement for collateral free loans,, the Committee 
recommends the guarantee cover be enhanced to ₹20 lakh under Credit Guarantee Fund Scheme 
for Education Loans (CGFSEL). 
5.16 The committee observes that Moratorium period under various education loan scheme 
being run by the Government is allowed upto study period plus one year in all cases. However, 
the Committee notes that students after completing higher education are usually not getting 
employment within one year after the study period.   In view of the difficulties that graduates 
often face in getting jobs, the Committee also recommends that the moratorium period for the 
repayment of student loans should be extended to two years after course completion instead of 
the present duration of study period plus one year. This will give graduates more time to secure 
stable employment opportunities before having to pay back their student loans. The Committee 
also recommends that flexible repayment facility linked to Post-Graduation Employment should 
be introduced and Institutions and lenders should collaborate to create repayment models that 
allow income-based installments, deferments, or restructuring in case of unemployment or 
underemployment, particularly benefiting students from disadvantaged backgrounds. 

24 
 
5.17 The Committee further observes that there is a repayment period of about 15 years post 
moratorium for educational loans and today, there are large number of students who are left 
without any stable jobs, which would risk default of repayments of education loans. The 
Committee, therefore, recommends that the Department of Higher Education should consider on 
urgent basis the introduction of income contingent repayment models to ensure that there is no 
increase in the number of Non Performing Assets (NPAs) in the banks. 
5.18 The committee observes that in a number of cases education loans applications of students 
from rural/remote areas are perhaps either rejected or Branch Managers do not receive these 
applications inspite of RBI's Master direction dated 6th January, 2025 which provides that there 
should be no prohibition or rejection of the first time borrowers because they don’t have any 
credit history. The Committee, therefore, recommends the Department of Higher Education, 
Department of Financial Services and RBI to develop mechanism against undue rejection or non 
receipt of education loan application from disadvantaged groups of the society at Branch level by 
Banks and strict guidelines against this practice should be issued at the earliest. Further, there 
should be robust mechanism for monitoring of implementation of RBI's Master direction dated 
6th January, 2025. 
5.19 The Committee observes that under Credit Guarantee Fund Scheme for Education Loan 
(CGFSEL) there is sanctioned corpus of around Rs. 3,500 crores which provides guarantee to 
lenders for educational loans upto Rs. 7.5lakh covering 75% of the amount in default. The 
Committee also observes that there is rapid increase in demands for educational loans in the 
country. The Committee, therefore, recommends the Department of Higher Education to 
augment the corpus of Rs. 3,500 crores, so that, the scheme does not face exhaustion of funds and 
none of students should left behind to access the education loans. 
5.20 The Committee further notes that under the PM Vidyalaxmi scheme, there is 3% 
subvention on loans up to Rs. 10 lakh and there is also a limit to the number of beneficiaries, 
which is around one lakh with an income ceiling of upto Rs.8 lakhs. Hence, the Committee 
recommends the Department of Higher Education to revise the limit of number of beneficiaries 
in view of vast population of the country and to ensure that no eligible student is left out the 
education system. 
5.21 The Committee emphasizes that in a welfare State, education loans should be highly 
subsidized and it cannot be treated as a commercial venture given its criticality to national 
building. The Committee also notes that the current policy of discretion to commercial banks in 
the disbursal of education loans has impacted the ability of students to seek education loans. The 
Committee strongly recommends that the Department of Higher Education, Department of 
Financial Services, and the RBI to work together to develop a uniform policy for education loans 
across all banks and determine a uniform reasonable rate of interest that should be applied for 
all education loans 
5.22 The Committee strongly believes that income criterion for student loans can be arbitrary, 
difficult to implement, and pose hurdles for students in accessing credit. Rather than income-
based criterion to avail of the Ministry’s education loan schemes of CSIS and PM-Vidyalaxmi, 
the Committee recommends that ration-card of the parents be considered as the primary criteria 
of eligibility for student loans. Priority should be given to those individuals and families holding 
the ration card and those getting free rations on ration cards, in the disbursal of education loans, 
with at least 20% of loans reserved for this segment and with the Government standing as the 
guarantor for these loans. 
5.23 The Committee has previously recommended that the Department work with banks and 
other agencies to track the prevalence of education loans not just in the states where they are 

25 
 
disbursed, but in the state of origin of the beneficiary as well. In its study visit to Ladakh, the 
Committee’s conviction in the need for the same has only strengthened since in many remote 
regions, students leave their home region for higher education. Accurate collection of data is 
necessary for evaluating the prevalence and accessibility of student loans.
