Report on the Food Safety and Standards (Labelling and Display)
Regulations, 2020 and its amendments

1. Background

The Food Safety and Standards Bill, 2005 aimed to consolidate various
laws related to food safety and establish a unified regulatory framework in
India. Its primary objective was to ensure the availability of safe and healthy
food for human consumption by regulating and supervising food safety at all
stages of the food chain. Prior to this, there were multiple laws and regulations
governing food safety in India, leading to confusion and inconsistencies. The
Bill aimed to streamline these laws into a single, comprehensive piece of
legislation. The Food Safety and Standards Bill, 2005 was passed by Lok Sabha
on 26" July 2006 and by Rajya Sabha on 2™ August, 2006 as the Food Safety
and Standards Act, 2006. The Act was given assent by the President of India on
23" August, 2006. The Act established the Food Safety and Standards
Authority of India (FSSAI) as the apex body for food safety in India. This
centralized body is responsible for setting standards, regulating food
businesses, and ensuring compliance with the Act. The Act covers the entire
food chain, from manufacturing and storage to distribution, sale, and import of
food articles. This holistic approach ensures that food safety is addressed at
every stage of the process. The ultimate goal of the Act is to protect and
promote public health by ensuring that food available to consumers is safe,
nutritious, and free from contaminants and adulterants.

2. Section 92 (2) (k) of the Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006 empowers
FSSAI to make regulations regarding the manner of marking and labelling of
foods. Accordingly, the Food Safety and Standards (Labelling and Display)
Regulations, 2020, notified on 17 November, 2020, prescribe the labelling
requirements of pre-packaged foods and display of essential information on
premises where food is manufactured, processed, served and stored with the aim
to enhance consumer information and safety regarding pre-packaged food in
India. These Regulations separate packaging, labelling, and advertising
requirements, and mandate specific information on food labels, including
nutritional information, allergen declarations, and manufacturer details. The
Regulations were laid on the Table of Lok Sabha on 19th March, 2021 and on
the Table of Rajya Sabha on 23rd March, 2021.

2.2 Chapter 3 mandates display of information in food service establishments

declarations on the label due to their potential health implications. These

3. Examination of the Regulations and interaction with the concerned
Ministry and Stakeholders

3. The Committee on Subordinate Legislation decided to examine the Food
Safety and Standards (Labelling and Display) Regulations, 2020 and subsequent
amendments, as the Committee felt that the Regulations are essential due to its
importance to public health, consumer rights and transparency in food labelling,
especially in the context of rising health concerns like obesity, lifestyle diseases and
food adulteration. The Committee highlighted the need to understand the scientific
basis, regulatory processes and implementation challenges.


3.1 During the course of its examination of the aforesaid Regulations, the
Committee held three meetings on 2 May, 2025, 18" June, 2025 and 24th
September, 2025. In these meetings, the Committee held detailed deliberations and
heard the views and submissions of the Secretaries, Ministry of Health and Family
Welfare, Ministry of Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises, FSSAI, National
Institute of Nutrition (NIN) and ICMR and other Stakeholders/Associations. The
Committee also sought information through Questionnaires from the Ministry and
other organizations. The following paras summarize the views, submissions and the
deliberations in these meetings and the information shared with the Committee.

3.2 In the meeting of the Committee held on 2" May, 2025, the representatives of
the Ministry of Health & Family Welfare and Food Safety and Standards Authority of
India (FSSAJI) were heard. In his presentation, CEO, FSSAI gave a comprehensive
overview of food labelling regulations in India, tracing their evolution from the
Agricultural Produce (Grading and Marking) Act of 1937 (AGMARK) to the Food
Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI) regulations of 2020. He mentioned
that initially, labeling was optional under AGMARK and the Prevention of Food
Adulteration Act (PFA) of 1954 introduced mandatory labeling requirements such as
product name, ingredients, net weight, and prohibitions on false claims. Subsequent
legislation, including the Infant Milk Substitutes Act of 1992 and the Legal
Metrology Act of 2009, further refined labelling provisions, addressing specific
products like genetically modified foods and infant milk substitutes. The Food Safety
and Standards Act (FSSA) of 2006 consolidated these efforts, leading to the
comprehensive Packaging and Labelling Regulations of 2011 encompassing
packaging, labeling, advertisement and claims. Later, in 2018, food safety standards
and packaging regulations were made separate, and advertising and claims
regulations were made separate. In 2020, labelling and display regulations were
notified.

3.3 CEO, FSSAI submitted that the Food Safety & Standards (Labelling and
Display) Regulations, 2020 introduced significant updates, mandating nutritional
information based on Recommended Dietary Allowance (RDA) per 100 grams,
allergen declarations, and specific logos for fortified, vegan, and Ayurvedic foods.
Also Front-of-pack labeling became mandatory for multi-source edible oils, pan
masala warnings, and vegetarian/non-vegetarian declarations. Amendments in 2021
and 2022 reduced label size requirements, exempted certain products from allergen
declarations, and standardized labelling for breads like whole wheat and multigrain. It
was submitted that the regulations align with global standards like the Codex
Alimentarius and include India-specific requirements, such as vegan and Ayurvedic
logos. It was informed that E-commerce and food service establishments with over 10
outlets must also comply with labelling requirements, including calorific value
disclosures.

3.4 CEO, FSSAI further submitted that enforcement of the Regulations is robust,
with penalties under the parent Act ranging from Rs. 5-10 lakhs for civil offences like
misbranding to imprisonment for criminal violations causing injury or death. It was

7


informed that in 2023-24, over 4 lakh food samples were tested, identifying 33,000
non-conforming cases, with 29,000 penalties imposed. Also, the Advertisement
Monitoring Committee addressed 577 misleading claims, closing 280 cases after
corrections. He further informed that a draft Front-of-Pack Nutrition Labelling
(FOPNL) Regulation, notified in 2022, proposes a star-rating system (0.5 to 5 stars)
based on positive and negative nutrients, harmonized with international practices and
over 14,000 stakeholder comments are under review, with ongoing consultations to
finalize amendments, reflecting a scientific and inclusive approach to improving food
labelling standards in India.

3.5 During the meeting, the Chairman of the Committee emphasized the need for
FSSAI to align with the Prime Minister’s anti-obesity campaign, citing Singapore’s
simple A-D nutri-code as a model for clear, consumer-friendly labelling to combat
obesity. He urged FSSAI to enhance its institutional capacity and update Regulations
in response to a Supreme Court directive to finalize Front-of-Pack Nutrition Labelling
(FOPNL) within three months. He also sought information on the coordination
between FSSAI and the Ministry of Consumer Affairs on misleading advertisements.
CEO, FSSAI acknowledged the Supreme Court’s directive on FOPNL, noting that a
2022 draft notification received over 14,000 stakeholder comments, and an Expert
Committee is reviewing them. He confirmed FSSAI’s commitment to stakeholder
consultations for adopting a simpler nutri-code like Singapore’s A-D system. On
misleading advertisements, he clarified that FSSAI regulates food-related claims,
issuing show-cause notices and penalties while coordinating with the Consumer
Affairs Ministry for broader issues. He also clarified that alcohol falls under FSSAI’s
labelling purview, with warning sizes increased to 50% of the label. For ice cream, he
noted that vegetable oil-based frozen desserts are permitted but must be clearly
labelled as non-milk products, similar to paneer analogues and vanaspati.

3.6 During this meeting, Members had also raised a range of critical queries and
concerns regarding the implementation and electiveness of food labelling regulations.
Concerns were voiced about the unclear display of manufacturing and expiry dates on
food products, with suggestions to prevent companies from manipulating dates to
boost sales, FSSAI’s approach to updating the National Health Rating in line with
evolving WHO guidelines, and how it balances input from medical experts and
industry stakeholders and the mechanism for random post-market inspections and
jurisdiction over counterfeit products like fake rice and milk powder. Challenges in
the grassroots implementation of labelling rules, particularly confusion around
drinking water safety and hotel food adulteration and the scientific basis for star
ratings were also mentioned.

३.7. In response to the Members' queries, CEO, FSSAI informed the measures taken
by FSSAI to address various concerns submitting that on the issue of illegibly small
expiry dates, major manufacturers already withdrew products well before expiration,
that FSSAI engages smaller producers through platforms like FICCI and state-level
meetings to enforce similar practices. On the Health Star Rating system, it was
submitted that it is based on ICMR and National Institute of Nutrition guidelines,

8


with inputs from international data and stakeholder consultations aimed at balancing
public health and industry views. Regarding enforcement, it was mentioned that each
Food Safety Officer is mandated to conduct 25 sample checks monthly, with over
four lakh samples tested in 2023—24 across 247 labs, and all data is being uploaded to
the INFoLNET portal. He also highlighted awareness initiatives including Eat Right
Melas, Eat Right Schools, and certified campuses. Addressing online labelling
exemptions, he clarified that these apply only to e-commerce listings, not physical
products, which must be comply fully. It was also informed that labeling is permitted
in English, Hindi, or regional languages and underlined the agency’s guidelines
promoting healthy diets in schools, including bans on high-fat/salt/sugar foods.

3.8 During the 20% meeting of the Committee held on 18" June, 2025, Secretary,
Ministry of Health and Family Welfare mentioned that the Food Safety and Standards
(Labelling and Display) Regulations, 2020, consolidated previous packaging and
labelling rules and have been amended three times since their notification. Further
amendments, including front-of-pack labeling (FOPL), are under consideration, with
some aspects being sub judice. It was mentioned that obesity is a public health
concern and a growing issue, being addressed through inter-ministerial collaboration
like promoting physical activity via the 'Fit India' campaign, incorporating yoga in
Ayushman Arogya mandirs, promoting millets (low glycemic index), the 'Eat Right
Movement,' and 'Eat Right Campuses' to encourage healthy eating. It was further
highlighted that the Ministry of Education has introduced health and fitness chapters
in the 2023 National Curriculum Framework. Also, mobile teams screen children for
height and weight, identifying approximately 1,00,000 obese children among 57
million screened in government schools since January 2025, around 1.1 million
teachers were trained on obesity aspects, and 3.3 million adolescents have received
counseling, with 6,50,000 health promotion sessions conducted. FSSAI Campaign
named 'Aaj Se Thoda Kam' which promotes reduced consumption of unhealthy
ingredients, with videos available in multiple languages to raise awareness was also
highlighted.

3.9 During the meeting, CEO, FSSAI in his presentation informed that the 2021
amendment made declarations mandatory for additives like aspartame, specifying
that it is unsuitable for pregnant women and children. Further the 2022 Amendments
exempted products with less than 0.5% saturated or trans fats from declaration and
introduced new requirements for nutritional information per 100g/100ml and per
serving size, FSSAI logo, manufacturing/packaging/expiry dates, and allergen
declarations (e.g., milk, prawns, peanuts, gluten). These amendments also introduced
logos for Ayurveda, vegan, fortified, and vegetarian/non-vegetarian foods,
harmonized with Codex Alimentarius Standards. He further mentioned about the
mandatory labelling requirements like Product name, ingredients list, manufacturer
details, nutritional information, FSSAI license number, allergen declarations, batch
number, net weight, and country of origin for imported foods etc and other specific
declarations for multi-source edible oils, milk powders artificial sweeteners and
Statutory warnings (e.g., for pan masala, supari) which must cover 50% of the pack.

9


3.10 On the aspect of Front-of-Pack Labelling (FOPL), it was conveyed that a draft
for FOPL was notified in 2022, proposing a star- rating system based on energy, total
sugars, salt, and saturated fats and an Expert Committee is reviewing 14,000
stakeholder suggestions, with consultations held in Mumbai, Hyderabad, Kolkata,
and Delhi. It was further informed that the Hon’ble Supreme Court has directed the
Expert Committee to submit a report within three months to finalize FOPL
amendments. He stated that the process was on for involving the stakeholder
consultations, review by the Scientific Committee, and re- notification as a draft by
the Ministry. He also highlighted the various concerns raised by 80% of MSMEs and
1,34,000 Self-Help Groups, fearing adverse impacts on traditional sweets (e.g.,
rasgulla, gulab jamun) due to low star ratings etc.

3.11 Further in his submissions, CEO, FSSAI mentioned about the enforcement and
violations statistics, exemptions from nutritional labelling for single-ingredient,
unprocessed, or minimally processed foods (e.g., packaged drinking water, herbs,
spices, salts, sweeteners) and small packets (<30 sq cm), misleading claims with
terms like "natural," "authentic," or "pure" being restricted unless part of a brand
name, school regulations that schools must promote safe, balanced diets per NIN’s
‘Dietary Guidelines for Indians.' And various awareness programs like the 'Eat Right
Pro India Programme' which conducts village-level awareness campaigns, including
400+ 'Eat Right Melas' and school engagements to promote healthy eating.

3.12 Director, ICMR-National Institute of Nutrition, in her submission mentioned
about the role of NIN in setting Recommended Dietary Allowances (RDAs) for
energy, protein, iron, vitamins, and minerals for different population groups (e.g.,
adults, children, pregnant women) stating that RDAs are revised every 2-3 years
based on scientific data. She further stated that the Indian food composition table
provides data on 151 nutrients across 500+ commonly consumed foods, used globally
in South Asia and supports nutritional labelling by specifying RDA percentages per
serving/100g. She also highlighted Obesity interventions by NIN through promotion
of 'My Plate for the Day' (2000-calorie balanced diet) to address cereal-heavy diets
and low fruit/vegetable consumption. She also mentioned about the Nutrify India
Now App mentioning that it is a searchable app providing nutritional data for food
grains and recipes, though variations in household recipes pose challenges.

3.13 During its third meeting held on the 24th September, 2025, the Committee
heard the representatives of the Ministry of Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises
(MSME) and Food Industry Associations. The Secretary, Ministry of MSME, made a
presentation outlining the definition, role, and contribution of MSMEs to economic
growth, particularly in the food processing sector. He also highlighted challenges
faced by MSMEs, such as financial constraints and procedural complexities, and
suggested ways to facilitate better implementation of the Labelling and Display
Regulations, 2020. He emphasised that the Ministry serves as the nodal agency for
MSMEs, dealing with size-based classifications and normative aspects. The Secretary
reiterated that MSMEs are key engines of economic growth, contributing
significantly to GDP, exports, employment, empowerment, and environmental

10


sustainability. He informed the Committee that the Government is actively exploring
mechanisms to simplify compliance frameworks and reduce the compliance burden
without compromising basic standards. He suggested that FSSAI could adopt a more
scale-sensitive and capacity-based approach in this regard. He further proposed that,
similar to income tax and GST thresholds, FSSAI could consider introducing
threshold-based licensing requirements, grounded in sound empirical and public
health criteria.
3.14 Further, highlighting the need for strengthening common infrastructure—such
as cold chains, testing laboratories, and packaging units—the Secretary underlined
that access to affordable credit remains critical for MSME capacity building. He also
noted that digital onboarding for MSMEs onto e-marketplaces could enhance their
competitiveness while naturally improving standards of hygiene, packaging, and
marketing. On research and development, he observed that small industries lack
resources to undertake R&D independently, and suggested that targeted support
mechanisms be developed to promote innovation and quality enhancement in the

MSME food sector. The Secretary stressed the importance of capacity enhancement
and State Government participation, as industry is a State subject. He recommended
that stakeholder consultations by regulatory bodies such as BIS, FSSAI, and QCI be
made more inclusive, sector-specific, and grounded in the realities of MSMEs.

3.15 The Committee then interacted with representatives of the food industry.
Welcoming them, the Chairman highlighted the Hon’ble Prime Minister’s vision of
an obesity-free India and the need to encourage healthier consumer choices. He
expressed concern over increasing trends of obesity and sought the industry’s
perspective on the proposed labelling regulations and their implications.

३.16 The representative of CIFTI informed the Committee that CIFTI facilitates the
food sector by addressing quality concerns and interpreting food laws. He clarified
the distinction between “expiry” and “best before” dates, suggesting that “expiry/use
by” should be mandatory only for perishable and high-risk foods, while “best before”
should apply to others. He recommended a holistic review of Schedule II to remove
redundant declarations, the use of QR codes to reduce label clutter, and a science-
based revision of definitions related to “unsafe food.”

३.17 The representative of the Indian Dairy Association (IDA) stated that while the
current regulations suit the dairy industry, the proposed star-rating system could
cause consumer confusion. He noted anomalies in the algorithm, where nutritious
products like paneer received the same or lower ratings than less healthy items. IDA
suggested expert review of the algorithm, default five-star ratings for natural dairy
products, and exemption of natural saturated fats and sodium from negative
highlighting.

3.18 The All-India Food Processors’ Association (AIFPA) expressed concern that
the proposed star-rating system could harm traditional food industries, confuse
consumers, and negatively affect farmers and the economy. They argued that such a
system could reinforce misconceptions about processed foods and damage India’s
traditional food heritage.

3.19 The Indian Food & Beverage Association (IFBA) recommended that front-of-
pack labelling focus on providing balanced nutritional information to help consumers
make informed choices. They emphasised the importance of robust enforcement,
consumer education, and policy integration, and called for harmonisation across
different regulatory authorities to establish a “One Nation, One Law” framework.

3.20 The Forum of Indian Food Importers (FIFI) opposed the star-rating system,
stating that it fails to capture the nutritional value of products comprehensively. They
also recommended that annual labelling changes coincide with the start of the
financial year (11 April) rather than July, to align with business cycles and allow
adequate time for compliance.

4. The Committee, based on the inputs it received from Members and various
stakeholders, also sought, through Questionnaires and other written submissions,
responses to the various points in respect of the regulations. The Ministry and FSSAI
duly responded to the same and based on the same the following submissions have
been prepared:

4.1 In their submissions the ICMR-—National Institute of Nutrition (NIN),
Hyderabad has stated that it has played a central role in shaping India’s Food Safety
and Standards (Labelling & Display) Regulations framed by the Food Safety and
Standards Authority of India (FSSAI). Its inputs have ensured that food labelling
standards in India are scientifically robust, nutritionally relevant, and culturally
appropriate. The Institute submits that ICMR-NIN defines and periodically revises
Recommended Dietary Allowances (RDA) for the Indian population based on age,
gender, life stage, and activity level. Further the 2020 Nutrient Requirement Report
forms the basis for mandatory nutrition labelling under Regulation 5(3), covering
macronutrients (proteins, carbohydrates, fats) and micronutrients (vitamins and
minerals). Also, FSSAI’s labelling norms (e.g., 2000 kcal diet reference values) are
derived from NIN’s RDA framework. Besides this, the Tolerable Upper Limits
(TULs) defined by NIN are used to ensure safe fortification levels and prevent
overconsumption.

4.1.1 It has also been submitted that NIN’s scientific work supports labelling of
fortified foods, advising that fortification should not exceed one RDA/day and it led
the development of Double Fortified Salt (Iron + Iodine) standards. Further, the
Indian Food Composition Tables (IFCT) developed by NIN provide nutrient data for

12


528 Indian foods across 151 nutrients—forming the backbone for nutrient profiling
and back-of-pack labelling.

4.1.2 NIN has also stated that in their research it has been revealed that there is low
public understanding of nutrition labels as most consumers check expiry dates but not
nutrition details, and find the information too technical. These findings are part of
input towards FSSAI’s push for simplified, symbol-based front-of-pack labels
(FoPNL). Also NIN has actively promoted consumer awareness, through efforts like
“Read B4 U Eat” campaigns, inclusion of /abe/ reading in the Dietary Guidelines for
Indians, 2024, and “Let’s Fix Our Food” initiative developing school-based label
reading kits.

4.1.3 Further NIN scientists serve on FSSAI’s scientific panels and expert
committees on food labelling, claims, and advertising and NIN continues to guide
Front-of-Pack Nutrition Labelling (FOPNL) design—encouraging interpretive tools
such as star ratings, colour codes, and warning labels. NIN also submits that,
recognizing rising obesity and diet-related NCDs, NIN advocates strong, mandatory
FOPNL systems supported by limits on sugar, salt, and fat, and restrictions on
marketing unhealthy foods to children and in a study, NIN findings included that
most participants read labels, but mainly check expiry/manufacturing dates, Symbol-
based formats had high acceptance, even among illiterate participants, Warning
Labels effectively deterred unhealthy food choices, Nutri-Score was most preferred—
its colour-coded summary format was easy to understand, Warning Labels and Nutri-
Star strongly influenced perception and reduced preference for unhealthy foods and
summary indicators (Nutri-Score, HSR) help promote healthier options; warning
labels discourage unhealthy ones. All formats improved consumer understanding, but
a hybrid model combining summary indicators and warning symbols is best suited for
India. NIN recommended this approach for the Indian Nutrition Rating (INR) model
being developed by FSSAI.

4.1.4 It has also been submitted that India faces rising rates of overweight, obesity,
and NCDs, driven by increased consumption of ultra-processed foods (UPFs)}—now
accounting for nearly 10% of household food expenditure. NIN stresses that effective
food labelling, supported by national dietary guidelines, is crucial to promote
healthier food environments and align with the Prime Minister’s call to reduce sugar
and fat intake.

4.2 The Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI) in its written
submissions to various queries raised by various stakeholders submitted detailed
clarifications on the provisions of the FSS (Labelling & Display) Regulations, 2020,
stating that the regulations strike a careful balance between consumer right-to-know,
scientific accuracy, cultural sensitivities, and practical compliance feasibility for
Food Business Operators (FBOs).

4.2.1 In their submissions, FSSAI stated that the name of the food must always
indicate its true nature. Coined/fanciful/brand names are permitted provided they are
13


accompanied by a descriptive name reflecting the actual food. When adjectives such
as “natural”, “pure”, “authentic” etc. are used in brand names, a prominent disclaimer
(“This is only a brand name or trademark and does not represent its true nature”) is
mandatory. Besides this, FSSAI logo and licence number, vegetarian/non-vegetarian
symbol, and product name are required on the front-of-pack even on small packages
(<100 cm?) with contrasting colours mandated for legibility. Veg/non-veg declaration
on the principal display panel reflects India’s unique cultural and religious dietary
sensitivities. Also, products with shelf life >3 months may declare expiry/use-
by/best-before in “Month Year” format (e.g, MAY 2025) instead of full
DD/MM/YY, while DD/MM/YY remains optional and short shelf-life items (<3
months) require full date.

4.2.2 In respect of flavours, colours and additives, it has been submitted that
flavouring substances must be declared by class name (Natural, Nature-Identical,
Artificial) and processing aids and carry-over additives that no longer perform a
technological function in the final product need not be declared. Also, nutritional
labelling exemptions and fortification single-ingredient and minimally processed
foods (including most raw agricultural commodities), alcoholic beverages, spices,
herbs, water, etc., are exempted from nutritional information unless a nutrition/health
claim is made. Also, naturally occurring trans fats (milk fat, animal fat) are exempted
from trans-fat declaration and % RDA calculation.

4.2.3 In respect of the query as to whether uniform labelling rules place a
disproportionate compliance burden on Micro and Small Enterprises (MSEs)
compared to larger businesses, and whether these regulations should be adjusted or
scaled according to enterprise size and capacity to ease compliance for smaller
businesses, the Ministry states that labelling requirements under the Food Safety and
Standards (Labelling and Display) Regulations, 2020 apply uniformly to all food
businesses, regardless of size, because the information is essential for consumer
awareness. While certain exemptions exist under Regulation 8—for example, for
small packages, reused bottles, and perishable foods—there is no differentiated
regime for Micro and Small Enterprises (MSEs). To ease compliance, FSSAI
conducts capacity-building programmes and has trained over 28 lakh food handlers,
most of whom are from the MSME sector, to help them meet regulatory
requirements. Further the Ministry has conveyed that the MSME Ministry
participates in regulatory decision-making bodies such as the Food Authority and the
Central Advisory Committee and through these platforms, the Ministry of MSME
raises implementation concerns and discusses issues affecting the MSE sector.

4.2.4 On the question of whether Micro and Small Enterprises (MSEs) should
receive longer transition periods to comply with new labelling requirements—
especially when changes involve significant costs such as replacing pre-printed
packaging—and how regulators can help them manage financial and logistical
challenges from sudden labelling changes, the Ministry states that FSSAI already
provides transitional periods for all Food Business Operators, with July 1 designated

as the uniform enforcement date for labelling amendments and a minimum 365-day
14


implementation window. Concerns of MSMEs are discussed through the Ministry’s
participation in the Food Authority and Central Advisory Committee. FSSAI also
undertakes stakeholder consultations, awareness programmes, and extensive
capacity-building training—particularly involving MSMEs—to help them
understand and comply with new labelling regulations and manage related
challenges.

4.2.5 In respect of queries regarding limitations in the current Front-of-Pack
Nutrition Labelling (FOPNL) system, which relies on fixed thresholds and a
complex algorithm that may create anomalies and whether any revision to adopt
clearer, more transparent, and context-specific approaches such as per-serving
labelling is under consideration, the Ministry has informed that the matter is
currently sub judice.

4.2.6 On the question as to whether “Best Before” is mandatory for non-perishable
foods, when “Use By/Expiry” should be applied, and whether regulations should be
amended so that “Expiry/Use By” is reserved only for short-shelf-life or high-risk
foods, the Ministry has submitted that under the FSS (Labelling and Display)
Regulations, 2020, “Expiry/Use By” is mandatory for all food products, while “Best
Before” may only be provided as optional additional information. For products with
a shelf life of seven days or less, “Expiry/Use By” must be declared even if the date
of manufacture is omitted. Hence, no change to mandate “Best Before” in place of
“Expiry/Use By” is indicated.

4.2.7 As regards how FSS regulations can be strengthened to expand mandatory
disclosures on key nutrients, allergens, and additives, the Ministry has submitted
that current Labelling and Display Regulations already require detailed nutritional
information, including per-serve contributions to recommended dietary allowances
for added sugar, saturated fat, trans fat, and sodium, as well as mandatory
declarations for food additives and allergens. Also a draft amendment for 2025
further proposes highlighting the per-serve percentage contribution to RDA for
added sugar, saturated fat, and sodium in bold, larger font to enhance consumer
clarity.

4.2.8 In response to a query as to how food labelling can be simplified—through
standardised font sizes, placement, symbols or colour codes, less technical language,
and potential digitisation—to improve readability and compliance, especially for
MSMEs, the Ministry states that current regulations already specify mandatory
labelling requirements and allow the use of barcodes/QR codes for additional
information. It also highlights an FSSAI consumer-awareness initiative, “Har Label
Kuch Kehta Hai,” aimed at improving label literacy.

4.2.9 On the aspect of FSS regulations ensuring long-term effectiveness by
integrating strong enforcement, consumer education, and alignment with national
health and nutrition policies rather than functioning as standalone rules, the Ministry

15


has submitted that FSSAI regulations are aligned with national dietary guidelines
developed by ICMR-NIN, which also participates in FSSAI’s scientific panels. To
promote behavioural change, FSSAI conducts extensive nationwide awareness
initiatives—such as “Aaj Se Thoda Kam,” “Har Label Kuch Kehta Hai,” “Eat Right
India,” and “Stop Obesity’—ausing mass media, social media, and public outreach
channels. Additionally, 305 Mobile Food Testing Labs operate across districts,
providing testing, training, and education, especially in remote areas. These
combined efforts support enforcement, consumer literacy, and alignment with
broader health objectives.

4.2.10 On the question of need to identify regulatory safeguards needed to prevent
plant-based products from misusing dairy-specific terms like “milk,” “cheese,” or
“butter,” to protect both consumer clarity and the integrity of traditional dairy items,
the Ministry responds that existing FSS regulations already prohibit misleading
labelling and contain specific provisions on the use of dairy terms. FSSAI has also
issued FAQs, advisories, and notices, released awareness videos, monitored labels
and online listings through its Advertisement Monitoring Cell, and conducted
stakeholder consultations to prevent misuse of dairy terminology.

4.2.11 On the aspect of better harmonizing regulations with export-promotion
policies to reduce regulatory conflicts, ease compliance for exporters, and align
domestic standards with international trade requirements while maintaining public
health protections, the Ministry submits that the FSS Act mandates FSSAI to
promote consistency between domestic and international food standards, particularly
through harmonization with Codex norms wherever feasible. However, exporters
ultimately must comply with the importing country’s standards, which vary, and
export facilitation does not fall within the direct mandate of the FSS Act.

4.2.12 On the issue of as to how FSS regulations can be better aligned with national
dietary guidelines so that labelling policies, nutrient-profiling models, and food-
classification systems promote public-health goals and culturally appropriate diets,
rather than relying only on individual nutrient thresholds, it has been submitted that
national dietary guidelines developed by ICMR-NIN form a scientific foundation
for FSSAI standards and policies and FSS regulations are dynamic and periodically
reviewed by Scientific Panels—including ICMR-NIN representatives—to ensure
alignment with evolving nutritional science. Thus, mandatory labelling requirements
under the 2020 Regulations are intended to help consumers make informed choices.

4.2.13 Further FSSAI/ Ministry submits that the Regulations provide robust
consumer protection through clear, prominent, and culturally relevant mandatory
declarations while incorporating proportionate exemptions, flexible formats, and
digital options that significantly reduce the compliance burden on FBOs,
particularly for single-ingredient, minimally processed, small-pack, and bulk non-
retail products. The framework aligns with global best practices (Codex, WHO)
while addressing India-specific public health and socio-cultural priorities.

5. Observations And Recommendations: After detailed deliberations amongst
the Committee based on inputs from the concerned administrative Ministry, i.e. The
Ministry of Health and Family Welfare as well as the Ministry of Micro, Small and
Medium Enterprises and the various stakeholders, including food industry
representatives, the observations/recommendations of the Committee are outlined in
the succeeding paragraphs: -

5.1 The Committee appreciates the ongoing efforts of the Government of India in
strengthening food safety and regulatory frameworks, particularly in the area of
food labelling. While acknowledging the progress made, the Committee notes that
the existing labelling system still requires further enhancement to effectively
safeguard consumers from rising public-health concerns such as obesity, diabetes,
and hypertension. The Committee observes that current labels are often difficult
for the average consumer to interpret and may not sufficiently guide healthier
purchasing decisions. Drawing upon international best practices and
recognizing the unique nutritional challenges faced in the country, the
Committee underscores the need for continued improvement in the labelling
regime and recommends that future measures focus on improving clarity,
readability, and consumer understanding of nutritional information so as to
support national efforts in preventing non-communicable diseases and
promoting informed, healthy choices among citizens aligning with the Hon’ble
Prime Minister’s objectives towards reducing lifestyle-related diseases and
combating obesity through improved public awareness and healthier dietary
practices.

5.2 The Committee recognises the need for an effective Front-of-Pack labelling
system to address the growing incidence of diet-related non-communicable diseases
such as obesity, diabetes, hypertension, and cardiovascular disorders. It notes that
warning labels significantly enhance consumers’ ability to make quick and informed
choices, particularly for those with low literacy levels. While the Committee
acknowledges the potential benefits of interpretive systems such as Nutri-Score or
Health Star Ratings in the long run, it is mindful of stakeholder submissions,
including those from the food industry, regarding the time and operational
adjustments required for such a transition.

5.3. The Committee observes that existing back-of-pack nutrition tables are
frequently printed in very small fonts, making them difficult or impossible for many
consumers to read. It is also understood that placing nutrition information on the
front of the package increases the likelihood of consumers noticing and using the
information, significantly enhancing engagement. Also, the Committee is of the
view that clear, prominent, and easy-to-interpret nutrition information empowers
consumers to make healthier dietary decisions and therefore, supports national
efforts to curb non-communicable diseases. Therefore, the Committee is of the view

17


that simplified Nutrition Information panels on the front of the pack may be
promoted in a gradual manner so as not to overburden industry during the transition
to the Front of Pack warning label system. Accordingly, the Committee
recommends that FSSAI encourage the adoption of standardized Front-of-
Pack Nutrition Information (FOPNI) panels in a simplified and easily readable
tabular format.The Committee is of the view that introduction of FOP
Nutrition Information panels shall improve transparency, strengthen consumer
protection, and promote healthier food choices across the population. The
Committee further recommends that the nutrition information be presented in
a simplified, standardized, and easily readable tabular format and should
include key nutrients—energy, total sugar, added sugar, salt/sodium, total fat,
saturated fat, and trans-fat—presented per 100 g/ml and per serving, with a
prescribed minimum font size to ensure legibility, particularly for the elderly
and individuals with low vision.

5.4 The Committee observes that a substantial number of Indian consumers are
not proficient in English, limiting access to critical food information when labels are
predominantly in English and thus there is a requirement of providing such
information multilingually so that it reaches the consumer in one’s own language.
The Committee also observes that QR codes offer a cost-effective and scalable
solution to provide multilingual information without requiring multiple printed
labels. Therefore, the Committee recommends the adoption of QR-based
multilingual food labelling to ensure transparency, inclusivity, and consumer
protection across India’s diverse population. Further the Committee
recommends the inclusion of such a QR code on the front panel of all pre-
packaged foods, linking to a mobile-friendly page that provides complete label
information—including ingredients, nutritional facts, allergen warnings, date
marking, and FSSAI licence number—in all 22 Scheduled languages plus
English. The QR page should also incorporate text-to-speech functionality to
assist consumers with visual impairments or literacy challenges.

5.5 The Committee notes that Regulation 5(1)(a) mandates the use of the food
name specified under the Food Safety and Standards Regulations to establish its
identity, while 5(1)(b) allows common, usual, or descriptive names in the absence of
a specified name. The Committee observes that ambiguous or misleading names
(e.g., "natural" or "pure" without certification) can confuse consumers and
undermine trust, especially in e-commerce. Therefore, the Committee
recommends amending Regulation 5(1) to require the name of food to be
displayed in bold, larger font on the Front of Pack (FOP), aligned with
nutritional claims and ingredient lists to prevent inconsistencies. The
Committee further recommends that FSSAI should prohibit fanciful or trade
names that imply unsubstantiated health benefits unless certified. Further to
enhance enforcement, FSSAI should develop guidelines with illustrative
examples (e.g., "fortified wheat flour" must match fortification declarations),
conduct annual audits, and impose tiered penalties for violations.

18


5.6 The Committee notes that Regulation 5(3)(c) exempts certain foods from
mandatory nutritional labelling, including unprocessed single-ingredient products,
herbs/spices, tabletop sweeteners, alcoholic beverages, chewing/bubble gum , and
others . This aims to reduce burden on minimal-risk items but raises concerns for
health-impacting categories. The Committee finds it alarming that sweeteners ,
alcoholic beveragesand chewing gums are exempted, given their links to obesity,
diabetes, and hidden calories. Also, sweeteners lack child warnings despite prior
restrictions, alcohols contribute to Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) without
calorie disclosure, and gums often contain artificial additives. The Committee
recommends that 5(3)(c) be suitably amended to remove or limit these
exemptions.The Committee further suggests that exemptions should apply
strictly to raw, unprocessed items. In this regard, the Committee suggests that
FSSAI may consider introducing a “Voluntary Nutritional Information
Framework” for products currently exempted from mandatory nutrition
labelling and this framework should allow manufacturers to opt-in to display
simplified nutrition information per 100 g/ml or per serving, using clear
formats such as Front-of-Pack labels or QR codes linking to full data.The
Committee is of the view that this voluntary but structured approach will
expand access to meaningful nutrition information for exempted products,
support informed consumer choices, and respect traditional and small-scale
producers by avoiding undue regulatory burden.

5.7. The Committee notes that Regulation 5(7) requires every food package to
display the FSSAI logo and license number in a contrasting color to the background ,
but lacks specifications on minimum font size, especially for small packs or
crowns/closures, which can hinder visibility and consumer trust in traceability. The
Committee recommends amending Regulation 5(7) to mandate a minimum font
size of 1 mm for the logo and license number, scalable based on pack size (e.g.,
2 mm for packs >100 cm?). Also, Colors must ensure high contrast (e.g., black
on white), with multi-color options permitted if visibility is maintained.

5.8 The Committee notes that Regulation 5(14) requires allergen declarations
(e.g., "Contains sulphite") for concentrations >10 mg/kg (as sulphur dioxide), based
on international risk assessments . The Committee notes that this is crucial for
sensitive groups, including children and asthmatics. The Committee expresses its
concern about health risks from excessive sulphur dioxide (e.g., respiratory
issues) and recommends strengthening Regulation 5(14) by expanding the
allergen list to include sesame and celery , mandating bold declarations near
ingredient lists, and requiring cross-contamination warnings. The Committee
further states that, for sulphites, enforcing routine testing and setting category-
specific higher thresholds would be helpful. The Committee further suggests
that manufacturers should minimize sulphite levels through alternative
preservatives, with FSSAI providing technical guidance and incentives aligned
with ICMR-NIN data on vulnerable populations.

19


5.9 The Committee notes that Regulation 8(5) allows exemptions for brand owner
addresses and license numbers if provided via Barcode/Global Trade Identification
Number (GTIN), including QR codes, for non-retail containers. Noting that this
facilitates digital traceability in B2B supply chains, the Committee observes the
potential for QR codes to deliver real-time, detailed information (e.g., ingredients,
veg/non-veg), enhancing transparency, brand engagement, and efficiency in e-
commerce. The Committee recommends making QR codes for all non-retail
containers, with links to verifiable data on FSSAI portals. The Committee
further recommends that to address implementation challenges, subsidies for
MSMEs to adopt digital labelling and integrate with traceability systems under
FSS Act, 2006 can be provided.

5.10 The Committee recommends that the Food Safety and Standards
Authority of India (75841), in consultation with ICMR-National Institute of
Nutrition (NIN), should institutionalize a mechanism for periodic review and
updating of the nutritional benchmarks—such as Recommended Dietary
Allowances (RDA) and Tolerable Upper Limits (TULs)—used in the Food
Safety and Standards (Labelling & Display) Regulations. Such reviews should
align with the latest scientific evidence and national dietary guidelines issued by
ICMR-NIN to ensure that nutrition labelling remains scientifically sound,
relevant to Indian dietary patterns, and consistent with public health
objectives.

5.11 Recognizing the limited public understanding of nutrition labels, the
Committee recommends that FSSAI, in collaboration with ICMR—-NIN and
educational institutions, intensify nationwide consumer awareness campaigns
on food label reading and healthy food choices. Successful models such as
“Read B4 U Eat” and the school-based label reading kits developed under the
“Let’s Fix Our Food” initiative should be expanded and institutionalized as
part of nutrition education programmes in schools and public health
campaigns to promote behavioral change at the community level.

5.12 The Committee acknowledges the steps taken by FSSAI to curb the misuse of
dairy terms but notes that plant-based analogues continue to proliferate, increasing
the potential for consumer confusion. The Committee recommends that FSSAI
strengthen enforcement by establishing clearer, more explicit labelling criteria
distinguishing dairy products from plant-based alternatives, including
mandatory qualifiers (e.g., “plant-based” displayed prominently). The
Committee further recommends regular market audits, enhanced penalties for
repeated violations, and wider public education to ensure consumers clearly
understand product categories. Additionally, the Committee suggests periodic
review of dairy-term regulations in light of emerging food technologies and
evolving market practices to maintain the integrity of traditional dairy
terminology.

20


5.13 The Committee notes the efforts made by FSSAI to harmonize domestic
regulations with Codex standards but observes that exporters continue to face
operational challenges due to gaps between domestic standards and diverse
international requirements. The Committee recommends that the Government
establish a coordinated mechanism between FSSAI, export-promotion bodies,
and relevant ministries to proactively map international standards, identify
areas of divergence, and streamline domestic compliance pathways for
exporters without diluting public-health safeguards. The Committee further
recommends creating sector-specific guidance documents and a dedicated help-
desk to support exporters in navigating differing overseas standards. Regular
consultations with industry and periodic review of harmonization efforts
should also be undertaken to ensure India’s food regulatory framework
remains globally competitive and scientifically robust.

5.14 The Committee appreciates the existing linkage between ICMR-NIN dietary
guidelines and FSSAI’s regulatory framework but notes that further alignment is
necessary to strengthen public-health outcomes. The Committee recommends that
FSSAI adopt a more holistic, food-based approach—beyond individual
nutrient thresholds—by integrating dietary-pattern guidance, culturally
relevant food classifications, and clearer front-of-pack communication
reflecting national dietary goals. The Committee also advises regular,
structured coordination between FSSAI and ICMR-NIN to ensure timely
incorporation of updated dietary science into labelling and nutrient-profiling
policies. Additionally, the Committee encourages development of consumer-
friendly tools and educational materials that reinforce balanced and culturally
appropriate dietary choices.

3. Following this, in his presentation, CEO FSSAI presented a comprehensive overview
of food labelling regulations in India, tracing their evolution from the Agricultural Produce
(Grading and Marking) Act of 1937 (AGMARK) to the Food Safety and Standards Authority
of India (FSSAI) Regulations of 2020. He mentioned that initially, labeling was optional
under acts like AGMARK and the Prevention of Food Adulteration Act (PFA) of 1954,
which introduced mandatory labeling requirements such as product name, ingredients, net
weight, and prohibitions on false claims. Subsequent legislation, including the Infant Milk
Substitutes Act of 1992 and the Legal Metrology Act of 2009, further refined labelling
provisions, addressing specific products like genetically modified foods and infant milk
substitutes. The Food Safety and Standards Act (FSSA) of 2006 consolidated these efforts,
leading to the comprehensive Packaging and Labelling Regulations of 2011, which were later
bifurcated into separate regulations for packaging, labelling, and advertising in 2018 and
2020.

4. He stated that the Food Safety & Standards (Labelling and Display) Regulations, 2020
introduced significant updates, mandating nutritional information based on Recommended
Dietary Allowance (RDA) per 100 grams, allergen declarations, and specific logos for
fortified, vegan, and Ayurvedic foods. Also Front-of-pack labeling became mandatory for
multi-source edible oils, pan masala warnings, and vegetarian/non-vegetarian declarations.
Amendments in 2021 and 2022 reduced label size requirements, exempted certain products
from allergen declarations, and standardized labelling for breads like whole wheat and
multigrain. It was submitted that the regulations align with global standards like the Codex
Alimentarius and include India-specific requirements, such as vegan and Ayurvedic logos. It
was informed that E-commerce and food service establishments with over 10 outlets must
also comply with labelling requirements, including calorific value disclosures.

5. CEO, FSSAI submitted that enforcement of the Regulations is robust, with penalties
under the FSSA ranging from Rs. 5-10 lakhs for civil offences like misbranding to
imprisonment for criminal violations causing injury or death. It was informed that in 2023-
24, over 4 lakh food samples were tested, identifying 33,000 non-conforming cases, with
29,000 penalties imposed. Also, the Advertisement Monitoring Committee addressed 577
misleading claims, closing 280 cases after corrections. He further informed that a draft Front-
of-Pack Nutrition Labelling (FOPNL) regulation, notified in 2022, proposes a star-rating
system (0.5 to 5 stars) based on positive and negative nutrients, harmonized with
international practices and over 14,000 stakeholder comments are under review, with ongoing
consultations to finalize amendments, reflecting a scientific and inclusive approach to
improving food labelling standards in India.
