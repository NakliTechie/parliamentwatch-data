CHAPTER I: BUDGETARY ANALYSIS 
A. Overview of Demands for Grants (2026-27) 
1.1 The Ministry of Tourism is the nodal agency for the formulation of national policies and 
programmes for the development and promotion of tourism. It coordinates with Central 
Ministries, State/UT Governments, and the private sector to develop infrastructure, regulate 
service standards, and build human resource capacity. 
1.2 For the financial year 2026-27, the Ministry has been allocated ₹2,438.40 crore under 
Demand No. 99. Revenue expenditure accounts for ₹2,393.10 crore (98.14%) and Capital 
expenditure for ₹45.30 crore (1.86%). The allocation is approximately 86% higher than the 
Revised Estimate (RE) 2025-26 of ₹1,310.30 crore.

1.4 The Committee notes that no funds have been provided for international and domestic 
marketing and promotional activities under the Demands for Grants for 2026-27. The entire 
₹3.50 crore under the Overseas Promotion and Publicity head is allocated towards India’s 
mandatory contribution to international organisations such as the United Nations World Tourism 
Organisation (UNWTO). The Ministry informed the Committee that a request has been made to 
the Ministry of Finance for an allocation of ₹265 crore towards promotional activities. The 
Committee observes that the absence of any budgetary provision for the promotion of 
Indian tourism constitutes a serious deficiency in the budget formulation process. The 
Committee recommends that the Ministry take immediate steps to secure the required 
allocation through appropriate budgetary means, including a Supplementary Demand, 
and ensure that a structured multi-year promotional budget is established from the next 
financial year onwards. 
1.4A The Committee is of the view that the structural weakness in overseas promotion calls for 
institutional reform of a more fundamental character than an annual budgetary supplement alone 

2 
 
can provide. Three measures merit consideration in this regard. First, the setting-up of an India 
Tourism Promotion Board, a dedicated body bringing together the Government, State tourism 
departments, the hospitality industry, civil aviation stakeholders, and the private sector, would 
provide a stable institutional platform for coordinated, professionally managed destination 
marketing. Comparable tourism promotion bodies in competing destinations, including the 
Tourism Authority of Thailand, the Singapore Tourism Board, and VisitBritain, have 
demonstrated the effectiveness of a dedicated promotional institution operating at arm’s length 
from the administrative Ministry. Second, the Overseas Promotion and Publicity (OPP) 
budget may be ring-fenced as a committed, multi-year line in the Ministry’s budgetary 
framework, with a minimum floor linked to a defined percentage of total tourism infrastructure 
outlay, so as to ensure that promotional investment is sustained regardless of annual budgetary 
compression. Third, the appointment of designated Tourism Officers within Indian Embassies 
and High Commissions in key source markets would materially strengthen India’s on-ground 
promotional presence, facilitate engagement with local travel trade associations, and enable 
timely intelligence on source-market trends to inform the Ministry’s promotional strategy.

1.6 The Committee notes with concern the exceptionally low fund utilisation during 2024-25, 
where the Ministry could spend only ₹164.07 crore against a Budget Estimate of ₹2,479.62 
crore, representing a utilisation rate of 6.6 per cent. Even measured against the Revised 
Estimate, utilisation stood at only 19.3 per cent. The data reveals a recurring pattern over three 

3 
 
consecutive years wherein Budget Estimates are substantially reduced at the Revised Estimate 
stage and actual expenditure falls further still. 
1.7 During the evidence, Members questioned the basis for recommending increased funding 
when the Ministry has consistently failed to utilise the allocations already provided. 
1.8 The Ministry attributed the underutilisation to the following factors: (a) non-submission of 
Utilisation Certificates (UCs), No-Objection Certificates (NOCs), and closure documents by 
State Governments and Implementing Agencies; (b) delays in finalisation of Requests for 
Proposal (RFPs) for engaging Tourism Experience Development and Management Agencies 
(TEDMAs) and the preparation of Detailed Project Reports (DPRs); (c) the transition from the 
TSA-2 to the TSA-1 model, requiring all State Governments to open accounts with the Reserve 
Bank of India; and (d) Central Nodal Agency (CNA) threshold constraints restricting fresh 
releases when the CNA balance exceeds 6.25% of the BE. 
1.9 The Committee is not satisfied with the explanations offered by the Ministry. The same 
reasons have been cited in successive years to explain the same failures. The transition to the 
TSA-1 model, while a legitimate one-time disruption, does not account for a pattern of 
underutilisation that predates the transition. The Committee further notes the Ministry’s 
tendency toward concentration of expenditure in the final quarter, which raises concerns 
regarding both financial accountability and the quality of outcomes. 
1.10 The Committee further notes that under the Swadesh Darshan scheme, actual expenditure 
in 2024-25 was ₹59.25 crore against a Budget Estimate of ₹1,750.00 crore, representing a 
utilisation rate of 3.4 per cent. 
1.11 The Committee recommends that the Ministry implement a mandatory quarterly 
expenditure schedule targeting a minimum of 20 per cent utilisation in Q1, 25 per cent in 
Q2, and 30 per cent in Q3, to prevent end-year concentration. A digitalisedUtilisation 
Certificate submission portal with defined timelines and automatic escalation to State 
Chief Secretaries upon breach must be established within three months. The Ministry may 
submit to the Committee a quarterly report on fund utilisation measured against pre-
defined quarterly expenditure targets, so as to enable timely parliamentary scrutiny of 
absorption progress throughout the financial year. 
C. Shortfall in Projected Demand for 2025-26 
1.12 The Ministry informed the Committee that it had projected a demand of ₹2,656.44 crore for 
2025-26 but received an allocation of ₹2,541.06 crore, resulting in a shortfall of ₹115.38 crore. 
The PRASHAD scheme was the only component that received an excess allocation of ₹40.00 
crore. 
1.13 The Committee observes that while the Ministry has highlighted shortfalls in allocation 
from the Ministry of Finance, its inability to utilise even the reduced allocations weakens the 
basis for seeking higher provisions. The Committee is of the view that the credibility of 
future demands is contingent upon demonstrating sustained improvement in absorption 
capacity. The Committee recommends that any future projections to the Ministry of 
Finance be accompanied by a detailed expenditure performance statement for the 
preceding three years, along with specific measures proposed to improve utilisation.

CHAPTER II: SCHEME-WISE ANALYSIS 
A. Swadesh Darshan Scheme (SD 1.0, SD 2.0, and CBDD) 
2.1 Under Swadesh Darshan 1.0, the Ministry sanctioned 76 projects worth ₹5,290.33 crore, of 
which 75 have been physically completed. Under Swadesh Darshan 2.0, 53 projects have been 
sanctioned for ₹2,208.31 crore. Additionally, 38 projects worth ₹697.94 crore have been 
sanctioned under the Challenge Based Destination Development (CBDD) sub-scheme across 
four themes: Spiritual Tourism, Culture and Heritage, Vibrant Villages, and Eco-Tourism and 
Amrit Dharohar. The cumulative sanctioned amount under Swadesh Darshan since 2023-24 
stands at ₹2,906.25 crore. 
2.2 The Committee notes with concern the physical progress of projects under SD 2.0 and 
CBDD. Out of 91 active projects, the position as furnished by the Ministry is as follows:

implementation status report for all Swadesh Darshan and CBDD projects, to be placed on 
the Ministry’s website and furnished to the Committee. The Ministry may consider 
instituting a formal mechanism for linking future project sanctions to the implementing 
State’s past performance on physical progress, fund utilisation, operations, and

maintenance. The Ministry may also furnish to the Committee, within three months, a 
detailed assessment of the reasons for non-completion of each of the 77 projects currently 
below 25 per cent progress, along with a revised timeline for completion. 
B. PRASHAD Scheme 
2.8 Under the PRASHAD scheme, 54 projects aggregating ₹1,726.74 crore have been 
sanctioned, of which 33 have been completed. Nine new projects worth ₹172.27 crore were 
sanctioned in 2024-25. 
2.9 During the evidence, the Committee examined two specific cases: 
(a) Annavaram Temple Town, Andhra Pradesh (₹25.33 crore): Delayed due to late 
tendering. Work orders were submitted by the State Government in August 2025. First 
instalment of ₹6.74 crore was released on 1 October 2025. Physical progress reported at 28.36 
per cent. 
(b) Bom Jesus Basilica, Goa (₹16.46 crore): Stalled at approximately 24 per cent after the 
Archaeological Survey of India (ASI) sealed the project site following the discovery of ancient 
remains. The State Government took four months to submit a report despite repeated reminders 
and has sought deviations including dropping the Parking Lot and Heritage Interpretation Centre 
components. The deviation proposal, initially submitted in September 2025 and revised in 
January 2026, is under examination. No additional funds were released during 2025-26. 
2.10 The Committee notes that the Ministry has acknowledged that PRASHAD projects face 
more delays than Swadesh Darshan projects due to the brownfield nature of work, involvement 
of temple administrations, vested interests, and encroachment. The Ministry has stated that clear 
land titles will be emphasised as a condition for sanctioning new projects in 2026-27 through 
suitable provisions in the new Guidelines being formulated. 
2.11 The Committee recommends that for all new PRASHAD project sanctions in 2026-27, 
clear land titles and inter-agency clearances (including from ASI where applicable) be 
enforced as non-negotiable prerequisites at the sanction stage itself. The Ministry may 
ensure that pre-sanction due diligence includes mandatory site surveys and written 
confirmation from all concerned agencies. The Committee further recommends that the 
Ministry formulate a standard operating procedure for deviation requests, with a defined 
timeline of not more than 30 days from receipt of the first proposal to final decision, given 
that a longer period would effectively consume a full quarter of the project monitoring 
cycle and delay corrective action beyond acceptable limits. 
C. Pradhan Mantri Janjatiya Unnat Gram Abhiyan (PM-JUGA) 
2.12 The PM-JUGA sub-scheme under Swadesh Darshan was designed to develop 1,000 tribal 
homestays. Against this target, the Ministry received 17 proposals covering 94 clusters by the 
extended deadline of 1 September 2025. Five proposals from Andhra Pradesh, Ladakh, Madhya 
Pradesh, Mizoram, and Uttarakhand were approved by the Central Sanctioning and Monitoring 
Committee (CSMC) on 12 November 2025, covering 9 clusters and 365 tribal homestays, at a 
total cost of ₹17.52 crore. 

6 
 
2.13 Members noted during the evidence that the financial provision of ₹5 lakh for construction 
of two rooms and ₹3 lakh for renovation is inadequate, particularly in hilly and remote areas 
where transportation costs alone can reach ₹80,000 to ₹1 lakh. The Ministry stated that several 
States are supplementing with an additional ₹3-4 lakh from their own budgets. The Committee is 
of the view that the Ministry may consider designating a fund coordinating agency for PM-
JUGA, with a clearly defined mandate to liaise between the Ministry, implementing States, and 
the Department of Expenditure, so as to prevent project sanctions and fund flows from being 
obstructed by inter-agency procedural delays, particularly given the time-sensitive nature of the 
current Finance Commission cycle. 
2.14 The Committee further notes that the Ministry has sought the concurrence of the 
Department of Expenditure (DoE) for sanctioning the 5 approved projects, but this concurrence 
remains pending. As the current year is the final year of the 15th Finance Commission cycle, 
any further delay may result in the lapse of these sanctions. 
2.15 The Committee recommends that the Ministry treat the pending DoE concurrence as 
a matter of utmost urgency. The Committee further recommends that the per-unit 
financial allocation under PM-JUGA be reviewed and revised upward in consultation with 
the Department of Expenditure, taking into account actual construction costs in hilly and 
remote terrains. For the 16th Finance Commission cycle, the scheme guidelines must be 
redesigned with realistic cost norms and a simplified application process to achieve wider 
uptake. 
D. Assistance to Central Agencies 
2.16 Under this component, 66 projects worth ₹948.80 crore have been sanctioned to central 
agencies. Of these, 43 projects (₹520 crore) have been completed. 
2.17 Members inquired about coordination with the ASI regarding site development plans and 
tourism promotion. The Committee notes that the Committee’s earlier Report on “Holistic 
Development of India’s Tourism Sector through Formal Skill Development, University-Level 
Courses and Professional Training” had specifically recommended that the ASI partner with the 
proposed National Tourism Knowledge Grid for the training of site guides. 
2.18 The Committee recommends that inter-agency coordination between the Ministry of 
Tourism and the ASI be formalised through a standing mechanism, to ensure that site 
development, heritage interpretation, and tourism promotion are planned in an integrated 
manner. The Committee reiterates its earlier recommendation that the ASI serve as the 
custodian of factual accuracy while the proposed National Tourism Knowledge Grid 
institutions handle content delivery and pedagogical aspects for site guides. 
E. Special Assistance to States for Capital Investment (SASCI) 
2.19 Through 50-year interest-free loans from the Department of Finance, 42 projects worth 
₹3,795 crore have been sanctioned across 25 States. 
2.20 The Committee recommends that the Ministry furnish a detailed state-wise status 
report of all SASCI projects, including physical and financial progress, to the Committee 
within three months.

CHAPTER III: TOURISM PERFORMANCE 
3.1 Provisional statistics for 2025 show that Domestic Tourist Visits increased by 40.2 per cent, 
from 294.82 crore in 2024 to 413.28 crore in 2025, largely attributable to the Kumbh Mela. 
3.2 Foreign Tourist Arrivals (FTAs) declined by 9.4 per cent, from 1.00 crore in 2024 to 0.90 
crore in 2025. Foreign Exchange Earnings (FEE) declined by 6.62 per cent to ₹2,73,638 crore. 
The Ministry attributed this decline primarily to instability in Bangladesh, from where arrivals 
fell by 73.4 per cent (from 17,50,165 in 2024 to 4,66,012 in 2025). Bangladesh historically 
accounts for 17-18 per cent of foreign tourist arrivals. Excluding Bangladesh, FTAs from other 
source markets recorded a positive growth of approximately 4 per cent. 
3.3 During the evidence, Members observed that India’s global tourism share remains below 2 
per cent. India ranks 39th globally despite ranking 6th in natural resources and 9th in cultural 
resources. France receives approximately 100 million foreign tourists annually. India’s Buddhist 
circuit attracts less than 1 per cent of the world’s Buddhist pilgrims, with only 4.4 lakh 
international travellers. 
3.4 The Ministry stated during the evidence that the strengthening domestic market is to some 
extent displacing international tourism. Members found this explanation inadequate, observing 
that both segments should be accommodated simultaneously. 
3.5 Tourism contributes 5.2 per cent to national GDP and provides an estimated 8.46 crore direct 
and indirect jobs, accounting for 13.34 per cent of total national employment. The Committee 
notes that the Ministry has no specific employment projection targets for 2026-27, relying 
instead on retrospective data from the Periodic Labour Force Survey (PLFS). 
3.6 The Committee’s earlier Report on “Holistic Development of India’s Tourism Sector 
through Formal Skill Development, University-Level Courses and Professional Training” had 
recommended that the Ministry adopt a broader set of core indicators beyond foreign tourist 
arrivals, including average length of stay, per-tourist spend, deployment of Certified 
Civilisational Interpreters, an interpretation-focused visitor satisfaction index, and employment 
continuity of trained personnel. 
3.7 The Committee recommends that the Ministry set specific, forward-looking 
employment projection targets for the tourism sector for 2026-27 and subsequent years, in 
addition to retrospective PLFS-based estimates. The Committee reiterates its earlier 
recommendation that the Ministry adopt a broader and more comprehensive set of 
performance indicators as detailed in its Report on skill development, and create a digital 
dashboard for near real-time tracking of these metrics. The Ministry may report on the 
action taken on this recommendation within three months.

CHAPTER IV: PROMOTION AND MARKETING 
A. Overseas Promotion and Publicity 
4.1 The Committee notes that the entire allocation of ₹3.50 crore under the Overseas Promotion 
and Publicity head for 2026-27 is earmarked for India’s mandatory contribution to international 
organisations. No funds have been provided for international or domestic marketing activities. 
4.2 In 2025-26, ₹3.07 crore was initially allocated under OPP, supplemented through 
reappropriation of ₹40 crore from the Domestic Promotion scheme. The Ministry stated that this 
limited funding constrained its participation to only three international travel exhibitions during 
the year. India was absent from major international fairs including IMEX Frankfurt, IBTM 
Barcelona, and AIME Australia, where Indian operators attended at their own expense. 
4.3 The Committee is of the view that the absence of any budgetary provision for 
promotional activities constitutes a serious impediment to India’s competitiveness in global 
tourism markets, particularly at a time when Foreign Tourist Arrivals are declining. The 
Committee recommends that the Ministry draw up a comprehensive three-year 
international promotional calendar covering a minimum of ten major travel fairs 
annually, including the World Travel Market (London), Fitur (Madrid), ITB Berlin, 
IMEX Frankfurt, IBTM Barcelona, AIME Australia, and leading fairs in the Asia-Pacific 
and Middle East regions. The allocation for this purpose must be regularised through 
appropriate budgetary means at the earliest. 
B. Closure of Foreign Tourism Offices and Mission-Level Representation 
4.4 The Committee sought an explanation regarding the closure of all foreign tourism offices. 
Members referred to previous Committee recommendations to attach dedicated tourism officers 
to Indian missions abroad in all countries. 
4.5 The Committee reiterates its recommendation that dedicated tourism representation be 
restored in Indian missions abroad. The Committee recommends that the Ministry 
formulate a phased plan for restoring dedicated tourism officers in at least the top 15 
source market countries. The phased plan should include proposals for staffing, budgetary 
provision, and coordination protocols with the Ministry of External Affairs. 
C. Domestic Promotion 
4.6 The Ministry informed the Committee that domestic promotional activities include Bharat 
Parv, Paryatan Parv, Ek Bharat Shreshtha Bharat events, and the International Tourism Mart 
(ITM) for the North-East. The Dekho Apna Desh initiative, launched in January 2020, promotes 
domestic tourism through webinars, quizzes, seminars, familiarisation tours, and social media 
outreach. 
4.7 The Committee recommends that the Ministry strengthen the Dekho Apna Desh 
initiative with measurable annual targets for reach and engagement, and integrate it with 
the Incredible India Digital Platform for a unified domestic promotional strategy. 
4.8 The Committee would further suggest that the Ministry undertake a systematic stakeholder 
mapping exercise, identifying for each sub-sector of the tourism industry, including heritage 

10 
 
tourism, wildlife tourism, wellness tourism, pilgrimage tourism, adventure tourism, MICE, and 
coastal tourism, the specific source countries from which that sub-sector draws its principal 
inbound visitors. Such a mapping would enable the Ministry to design targeted, sector-specific 
promotional campaigns directed at the countries with the greatest demand potential for each 
segment, rather than relying solely on generic destination-marketing. The Ministry may consider 
publishing this stakeholder and source-market mapping as a reference document to guide both 
its own promotional planning and the investment decisions of private sector tourism operators.

CHAPTER V: INTERNATIONAL COLLABORATION AND INSTITUTIONAL 
COOPERATION 
5.1 The Committee notes that the Ministry’s international engagement currently encompasses 
participation in select international travel fairs, coordination with Indian missions abroad, and 
contributions to international organisations including the UNWTO. The Ministry has stated that 
it participates in events such as the World Travel Market in London and Fitur in Madrid, and 
runs global campaigns under the “Incredible India” brand. 
5.2 The Committee further notes that the Ministry has organisedfamiliarisation tours for foreign 
tour operators and travel media, including Japanese operators in Rajasthan and foreign operators 
in North-Eastern States. However, the Committee observes that the scope and frequency of such 
engagement has been severely curtailed by the reduction in the OPP budget, with the Ministry 
limited to participation in only three international exhibitions in 2025-26. 
5.3 In the area of institutional cooperation, the Committee notes that the Ministry has signed 70 
Memoranda of Understanding between 10 leading hospitality chains in India and 21 Central 
Institutes of Hotel Management, covering student engagement, faculty development, short-term 
tourism skilling, and institutional development. However, the Committee observes that there is 
no comparable framework for institutional collaboration with leading international tourism and 
hospitality institutions, tourism boards of competitor nations, or multilateral bodies beyond the 
mandatory UNWTO contribution. 
5.4 The Committee notes that several competing destinations, including Thailand, Singapore, 
Malaysia, and the UAE, have established structured bilateral tourism cooperation agreements, 
joint marketing campaigns, and institutional partnerships with universities and tourism boards of 
key source markets. India’s absence from such bilateral and multilateral frameworks limits 
knowledge exchange, mutual recognition of qualifications, and joint promotional opportunities. 
5.5 The Committee further notes that the Ministry has proposed the establishment of five 
Regional Medical Hubs, seaplane connectivity through Viability Gap Funding, and Buddhist 
Circuits in the North-East, all of which have a significant international dimension in terms of 
attracting foreign tourists and require sustained collaboration with the Ministry of External 
Affairs, Indian missions abroad, and foreign Governments. 
5.6 The Committee recommends that the Ministry develop a structured international 
collaboration framework encompassing the following elements: (a) bilateral tourism 
cooperation agreements with at least 10 key source market countries, covering joint 
promotion, mutual recognition of tourism qualifications, and exchange of best practices; 
(b) institutional partnerships between IHMs, IITTM, and leading international hospitality 
and tourism institutes for faculty exchange, curriculum benchmarking, and joint research; 
(c) a formal coordination mechanism with the Ministry of External Affairs to leverage 
Indian missions abroad for sustained tourism promotion, event hosting, and B2B 
facilitation; and (d) active engagement with multilateral tourism bodies beyond the 
mandatory UNWTO contribution, including participation in UNWTO capacity-building 
programmes, statistical frameworks, and sustainable tourism initiatives.

CHAPTER VI: SECTORAL TOURISM 
A. Ayurveda and Yoga Tourism 
6.1 The Committee notes the absence of Ayurveda in the Ministry’s tourism schemes and 
presentations, despite its significant potential, particularly in States such as Kerala and Tamil 
Nadu. Members observed that yoga tourism similarly lacks adequate focus despite global 
recognition. 
6.2 During the evidence, Members recommended that the Ministry work with Export Promotion 
Councils and the estimated 500-600 Ayurveda companies and practitioners to develop this 
segment. 
6.3 The Committee recommends that the Ministry formulate a dedicated promotion 
strategy for Ayurveda and yoga tourism in coordination with the Ministry of AYUSH and 
relevant Export Promotion Councils, with measurable targets for international wellness 
tourist arrivals. The strategy should include identification of 10-15 certified Ayurveda and 
yoga tourism destinations, standardisation of service quality, multilingual promotional 
content, and targeted outreach in key source markets with demonstrated demand for 
wellness tourism. 
B. Buddhist Circuit Development 
6.4 The Committee notes that the 2026-27 budget proposals include the development of 
Buddhist Circuits in Arunachal Pradesh, Sikkim, Assam, Manipur, Mizoram, and Tripura. 
Proposed interventions cover preservation of temples and monasteries, establishment of 
interpretation centres, improvement of pilgrim amenities, and strengthening of connectivity. 
6.5 The Committee observes that India attracts less than 1 per cent of the world’s Buddhist 
pilgrims despite being the birthplace of Buddhism and home to its most sacred sites. 
6.6 The Committee recommends that the Ministry prepare a time-bound action plan 
within three months for the Buddhist Tourist Circuit with measurable targets for 
international Buddhist pilgrim arrivals, developed in coordination with the Ministry of 
External Affairs and Indian missions in key Buddhist-majority countries including Japan, 
South Korea, Thailand, Myanmar, Sri Lanka, Vietnam, and Cambodia. The action plan 
should include targeted promotional campaigns, improvement of air and surface 
connectivity to Buddhist sites, dedicated interpretation services at all major Buddhist sites 
with multilingual capability, and institutional partnerships with Buddhist organisations 
and cultural bodies in source countries. 
C. Wildlife Tourism 
6.7 Members noted during the evidence that jungle tourism in India is among the most 
expensive globally, with expenditure of ₹8-10 lakh per visit at destinations such as 
Ranthambore. Members suggested focusing on specific national parks and tiger reserves rather 
than dispersing resources, citing Kaziranga as a potential model. 
 

13 
 
6.8 The Committee recommends that the Ministry develop a targeted strategy for wildlife 
tourism, identifying 10-15 high-potential national parks and tiger reserves for globally 
competitive positioning, in coordination with the Ministry of Environment, Forest and 
Climate Change. The strategy must include carrying capacity assessments, community 
benefit-sharing mechanisms, infrastructure improvement plans, and pricing 
rationalisation measures to enhance accessibility for both domestic and international 
tourists. 
D. Coastal and Blue Economy Tourism 
6.9 The Committee notes that several projects under SD 2.0, CBDD, PRASHAD, SASCI, and 
ACA relate to coastal and water-based tourism. The project “Alappuzha: A Global Water 
Wonderland” in Kerala (₹93.17 crore) is reported at 11.46 per cent physical completion. 
6.10 Cruise terminal projects at Mormugao (₹50.00 crore) and Mumbai (₹37.50 crore), and the 
Ex-INS Guldar Underwater Museum and Submarine Tourism project (₹46.91 crore), are noted 
by the Committee. 
6.11 The Committee recommends that the Ministry ensure close monitoring of all coastal 
and blue economy tourism projects for timely implementation. For cruise tourism, the 
Ministry should coordinate with the Ministry of Ports, Shipping and Waterways to develop 
a comprehensive cruise tourism facilitation framework, including streamlined immigration 
procedures, shore excursion infrastructure, and homeport development at selected ports. 
E. Eco-Tourism and Sustainable Tourism 
6.12 The Committee notes the Ministry’s formulation of National Strategies for Eco-tourism and 
Rural Tourism, and the Travel for LiFEprogramme. The 2026-27 proposals include development 
of Mountain Trails (Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Jammu and Kashmir, Araku Valley, and 
Podhigai Malai), Turtle Trails (Odisha, Karnataka, Kerala), and Bird Watching Trails (Pulikat 
Lake in Andhra Pradesh and Tamil Nadu). 
6.13 When asked about Regenerative Tourism, the Ministry stated that this is a “comparatively 
newer concept” and no activities have been specifically proposed for 2026-27. 
6.14 The Committee recommends that the Ministry commission a study on regenerative 
tourism frameworks suitable for Indian conditions and integrate the findings into the 
design of schemes under the 16th Finance Commission cycle. The study should draw upon 
international best practices and be completed within one year. 
F. Medical Value Tourism 
6.15 The Ministry informed the Committee that the medical tourism sector does not fall directly 
under its mandate. The Ministry’s role is limited to branding and promotion. The 2026-27 
proposals include the establishment of five Regional Medical Hubs in partnership with the 
private sector, combining healthcare delivery, education, research facilities, AYUSH Centres, 
and Medical Value Tourism Facilitation Centres.

6.16 The Committee recommends that an inter-ministerial coordination mechanism for 
medical value tourism be formalised, involving the Ministry of Tourism, Ministry of 
Health and Family Welfare, Ministry of AYUSH, Ministry of Commerce and Industry, 
and the Ministry of Home Affairs. The mechanism should address standardisation of 
pricing transparency for foreign medical tourists, streamlined visa procedures, quality 
accreditation of participating hospitals, and a unified digital onboarding platform. 
G. Hotel Capacity and Pricing 
6.17 The Committee notes the hotel capacity constraints highlighted during the evidence. India 
has approximately 1,80,000 branded hotel rooms and 15,000 unbranded rooms. Industry 
estimates suggest approximately double this capacity is required. Members noted that room 
tariffs reach ₹12-13 lakh during major events and contrasted this with consistently affordable 
pricing in competing destinations such as Bangkok, where rooms remain available at ₹25,000-
45,000. 
6.18 The Ministry informed the Committee of efforts to reduce hotel licensing requirements 
from 50-90 permissions and to recommend the elimination of outdated regulations such as the 
Sarais Act. Tourism has been included for the first time in both central and state-level Ease of 
Doing Business frameworks. The NIDHI+ platform has recorded 55,414 registered 
accommodation units as of 23 January 2026. 
6.19 The Committee recommends that the Ministry coordinate with State Governments to 
address hotel capacity constraints through the following measures: (a) revision of Floor 
Area Ratio (FAR) norms for hotel development in all States with tourism potential; (b) 
expedited rationalisation of licensing requirements, with a target of reducing the number 
of permissions required to below 20 within two years; (c) repeal or replacement of the 
Sarais Act and other outdated regulations; and (d) inclusion of a hotel capacity 
augmentation component in the National Mission for 50 Globally Competitive 
Destinations. The Ministry may consider placing the progress report on each of these 
measures in the public domain.

CHAPTER VII: MANPOWER AND SKILL DEVELOPMENT 
A. Capacity Building for Service Providers 
7.1 Under the CBSP scheme, the Ministry trained 89,801 persons in 2024-25 through 
programmes covering language competency, etiquette, and communication skills. The HSRT 
(Hunar Se Rozgar Tak) courses are NSQF-approved skill development programmes. 
7.2 The Committee notes that 84 per cent of the tourism workforce operates in the informal 
sector without skill certification or training. The tourism and hospitality sector has a projected 
CAGR of 12-13 per cent. 
7.3 A total of ₹60 crore was allocated for skill development in 2025-26: ₹35 crore under 
Assistance to IHMs and ₹25 crore under CBSP. Under the IHM scheme, ₹20.10 crore had been 
utilised as of January 2026 (65 per cent of the RE of ₹31 crore). Under CBSP, the allocation was 
enhanced from ₹25 crore at BE to ₹80 crore at RE. 
7.4 The Committee recalls that in its earlier Report on “Holistic Development of India’s 
Tourism Sector through Formal Skill Development, University-Level Courses and Professional 
Training”, the Committee had observed that India’s challenge is not a shortage of destinations, 
but a shortage of credible interpreters of its civilisation, and had made detailed recommendations 
for addressing this gap. The Committee notes that several of these recommendations are directly 
relevant to the Demands for Grants under examination. 
7.5 The Committee recommends that the Ministry, in implementing the CBSP scheme and 
related training programmes for 2026-27, align its activities with the recommendations 
made in the Committee’s earlier Report, particularly with respect to: (a) the creation of a 
National Tourism Knowledge Grid with the IITTM as the academic anchor; (b) the 
deployment of a mission-mode Recognition of Prior Learning (RPL) initiative to assess, 
train, and certify the informal workforce of guides, priests, and facilitators at the district 
level, as specifically recommended by the Committee; and (c) the incorporation of 
mandatory modules on Indian history, art, culture, geography, and interpretive 
storytelling in all tourism curricula. The Ministry may furnish a detailed action plan for 
implementation of these recommendations within three months. 
B. Paryatan Mitra / Paryatan Didi Initiative 
7.6 The Paryatan Mitra / Paryatan Didi initiative trained 4,382 individuals across 7 pilot 
destinations: Orchha, Gandikota, Bodh Gaya, Aizawl, Jodhpur, Srinagar, and Sri Vijaya Puram. 
The Ministry clarified that this training targets persons already engaged in tourism-related 
occupations and is designed for skill and behaviour modification. The Ministry stated that the 
question of placement rates is therefore not applicable. 
7.7 The Committee recommends that the Ministry expand the Paryatan Mitra / Paryatan 
Didi initiative beyond the current 7 pilot destinations to cover at least 50 destinations in 
2026-27, with a particular focus on the 50 destinations proposed under the National 
Mission. The Ministry should establish a tracking mechanism for measuring the impact of 
training on visitor satisfaction and service quality at trained destinations.

C. Tourist Guide Training 
7.8 The Ministry has announced plans to train 10,000 tourist guides across 20 iconic destinations 
through collaboration with IIMs, using a 12-week hybrid training course. 
7.9 During the evidence, Members questioned the need for IIM-level curriculum for tourist 
guides, when practical language training and cultural knowledge should suffice. Members cited 
examples from Spain, Thailand, and Cambodia, where retired teachers and ordinary citizens 
serve as guides effectively. 
7.10 The Committee recalls its earlier Report, in which it had recommended the notification of a 
new professional category titled “Certified Civilisational Interpreter (CCI)”, distinct from 
general tour guides, trained to provide historically rigorous, evidence-based interpretation 
supported by a tiered certification system linked to academic depth and language proficiency. 
7.11 The Committee recommends that the Ministry present the detailed training plan for 
the proposed 10,000 tourist guides, including budget allocation, pedagogical rationale, and 
justification for the IIM collaboration, before the programme is expanded. The Committee 
further recommends that the Ministry simultaneously advance the notification of the 
“Certified Civilisational Interpreter” cadre as recommended in the Committee’s earlier 
Report, ensuring that this professional category is distinct from general tour guides and is 
supported by a tiered certification system, multilingual capability assessment, and periodic 
renewal requirements. The Committee also reiterates its earlier recommendation that 
targeted incentives, including stipends or higher certification tiers, be provided for 
proficiency in UN and key emerging market languages such as Spanish, French, 
Mandarin, and Arabic. The Committee would further suggest that, in selecting an 
academic collaborator for the tourist guide training programme, the Ministry may 
consider whether a liberal arts curriculum, of the kind offered by institutions such as IIM 
Kozhikode or other reputable institutes with dedicated liberal arts and humanities 
programmes, may be more appropriate than a conventional management curriculum, 
given that the interpretive and communicative skills required of a tourist guide are more 
closely aligned with history, cultural studies, and the humanities than with business 
administration. The Committee would invite the Ministry to clarify whether management 
graduates trained in tourism-specific courses would in practice take up tourist guide 
professions, and to design the training architecture accordingly. 
D. Status of Institutes of Hotel Management 
7.12 Members raised concerns about State IHMs being closed (including in Yamuna Nagar, 
Haryana) without proper tracking of central government investments. 
7.13 The Committee notes that the Ministry has signed 70 Memoranda of Understanding

upgrading the National Council for Hotel Management and Catering Technology (NCHMCT) 
into a National Institute of Hospitality. 
 
7.14 Members specifically noted the absence of hotel management, hospitality, and tourist guide 
training institutions in the North-Eastern region beyond Guwahati.

E. Industry Immersion and Prior Learning Recognition

CHAPTER IX: INSTITUTIONAL AND GOVERNANCE ISSUES 
A. Constitutional Status of Tourism 
9.1 The Committee notes that tourism is neither in the Union List, the State List, nor the 
Concurrent List of the Constitution and is treated as a residual subject. Members observed 
during the evidence that this creates inherent coordination difficulties between the Centre and 
the States. The question raised by Members during the evidence, regarding whether tourism is 
the responsibility of the Centre or the States, reflects a persistent structural ambiguity. 
9.2 The Committee recommends that the Ministry, in consultation with NITI Aayog, 
prepare a comprehensive policy note examining mechanisms to strengthen Centre-State 
coordination for tourism, including the feasibility of including tourism in the Concurrent 
List or establishing a statutory National Tourism Authority with defined roles, 
responsibilities, and enforcement powers. The policy note may be submitted to the 
Committee within one year. 
B. Staffing and Institutional Capacity 
9.3 The Committee notes that the Ministry operates with a vacancy rate of approximately 35 per 
cent, with 359 personnel in position against a sanctioned strength of 552. 
9.4 The Committee recommends the preparation of a time-bound recruitment plan to fill 
all vacant posts within one financial year. The Ministry may furnish the recruitment plan 
to the Committee within three months.

Institute of Hotel Management (Kottayam), blocking of funds due to non-setting up of Food

9.6 The Committee recommends that the Ministry constitute a dedicated Audit Settlement

CHAPTER X: FUTURE PLANS 
A. Scheme Rationalisation for the 16th Finance Commission Cycle 
10.1 The Ministry has proposed consolidating eight current schemes into three integrated 
schemes for the 16th Finance Commission cycle (2026-31): 
(a) National Mission for 50 Globally Competitive Destinations: proposed outlay of ₹25,000 
crore over five years, to be implemented through Destination Management Organisations 
(DMOs) with performance-based incentives and private sector participation. 
(b) Unified Tourism Infrastructure Development Scheme: consolidating Swadesh Darshan, 
PRASHAD, and other assistance programmes. 
(c) Integrated Promotion and Publicity Scheme: merging overseas and domestic promotion with 
a proposed outlay of ₹5,000 crore over five years. 
10.2 The Committee notes that the proposed National Mission implies an annual expenditure 
capacity of approximately ₹5,000 crore, which is roughly double the Ministry’s current demand. 
10.3 The Committee recommends that all EFC notes for new schemes under the 16th 
Finance Commission cycle be accompanied by: (a) detailed implementation roadmaps with 
year-wise milestones; (b) institutional capacity assessments; (c) State-wise absorption plans 
based on past expenditure performance; (d) a phased scaling mechanism linked to 
demonstrated expenditure performance; and (e) a clear human resource plan, addressing 
the current 35 per cent vacancy rate, to ensure adequate supervisory capacity for the 
expanded outlay. The Committee further recommends that the Unified Tourism 
Infrastructure Development Scheme incorporate specific provisions for post-project 
maintenance, asset management, and regular third-party impact assessments. 
B. Key Initiatives Proposed for 2026-27 
10.4 The Ministry has informed the Committee of the following initiatives proposed for 2026-
27: (a) Development of 15 identified archaeological sites, including Lothal, Dholavira, 
Rakhigarhi, Adichanallur, Sarnath, Hastinapur, and Leh Palace. (b) Seaplane connectivity and 
Viability Gap Funding (VGF) for remote and water-linked destinations, with incentives for 
indigenous manufacturing of seaplanes. (c) Reduction of TCS on overseas tour programme 
packages to 2 per cent. (d) Development of Buddhist Circuits in the North-Eastern region. (e) 
Establishment of five Regional Medical Hubs. (f) National Destination Digital Knowledge Grid. 
(g) Divyangjan Kaushal Yojana for the hospitality sector. (h) Development of Eco-Tourism and 
Nature-Based Trails. (i) Development of tourism destinations in Purvodaya States. 
10.5 The Committee notes these proposals and recommends that the Ministry furnish a 
progress report on each initiative, including physical and financial milestones achieved,

C. National Awards for Tourism Professionals 
10.6 The Committee recalls its earlier recommendation for the institution of National Awards 
for “Best Civilisational Interpreter” and “Best Heritage Storyteller”, to be presented annually 
alongside existing National Tourism Awards. 

22 
 
10.7 The Committee reiterates this recommendation and urges the Ministry to institute 
these awards from the year 2026-27, with a view to elevating the professional status and

D. Tourism Satellite Account 
10.8 The Committee notes that a Tourism Satellite Account (TSA) is the internationally 
recognised framework, recommended by the United Nations World Tourism Organisation, for 
measuring the economic contribution of tourism in a manner comparable across countries. India 
has compiled three TSAs to date: the first for 2002-03, the second for 2009-10, and the third for 
2015-16, all prepared by the National Council of Applied Economic Research (NCAER) for the 
Ministry of Tourism. The most recent TSA is now nearly a decade old. The absence of a current 
TSA weakens the evidentiary basis for the Ministry’s resource claims, limits India’s ability to 
benchmark its tourism economy against international comparators, and prevents accurate 
attribution of tourism-related employment, income, and value addition across sub-sectors. The 
Committee recommends that the Ministry take steps, in coordination with the Ministry of 
Statistics and Programme Implementation, to ensure that a fully updated TSA is compiled 
and published at regular intervals not exceeding five years, and that the findings are 
incorporated into the Ministry’s budgetary presentations and policy planning. The 
Ministry may place a note on the status and roadmap for the Tourism Satellite Account 
before the Committee within three months.
