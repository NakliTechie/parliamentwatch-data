Introduction 
1. I, the Chairman, Department -related Parliamentary Standing Committee on Transport, Tourism and Culture, 
having been authorised by the Committee, have the honour  to present on its behalf this Report on the Demands 
for Grants (2026-27) of the Ministry of Civil Aviation (Demand No. 8). The Report has been prepared following 
a structured scrutiny of the Ministry's budgetary proposals for the financial year 2026 -27, in accordance with the 
mandate vested in the Committee under Rule 270 and related provisions of the Rules of Procedure and Conduct 
of Business in the Council of States. 
2. The Committee has examined the Demands for Grants of the Ministry in view of the stra tegic importance of 
the civil aviation sector to India's economic growth, regional connectivity and international competitiveness. 
With a rapidly expanding air traffic base, a scheduled airline fleet of approximately 860 aircraft with an average 
fleet age of 7.3 years, orders for over 1,600 new aircraft, and cumulative airport capacity reaching 574 million 
passengers per annum, the Ministry of Civil Aviation administers policy oversight for one of the world's fastest -
growing aviation markets. India has cros sed the threshold of 350 million annual passengers and is the world's 
third-largest domestic aviation market, with the market projected to grow from USD 15 billion in 2025 to USD 
25 billion by 2030. The quality, pace, and efficiency of the Ministry's regul atory oversight, infrastructure 
investment, and connectivity programmes have a direct bearing on the nation's economic integration, tourism 
potential, and the mobility of its citizens. 
3. The Committee noted, however, that the period under review has been marked by sustained operational 
turbulence in the sector. The Ahmedabad crash of 12 June 2025, in which Air India Flight AI171 crashed 
shortly after take -off killing 260 persons, the air ambulance crash in Chatra, Jharkhand on 23 February 2026 
killing seve n persons, the Baramati chartered plane crash of 28 January 2026, mass flight cancellations in 
December 2025, and persistent flight delays have exposed systemic fragilities that extend well beyond 
individual airline failures. The Committee observed that th is pattern of operational breakdowns, occurring 
against a backdrop of rapid traffic growth, underscores the urgency of the safety, regulatory capacity, and 
human capital concerns detailed in this Report. 
4. The Committee is of the considered view that the future trajectory of India's civil aviation sector will be 
determined not merely by the quantum of traffic growth, but by the rigour with which safety regulation is 
resourced, connectivity schemes are governed, legacy liabilities are wound down, and emergi ng sectors such as 
drones, MRO and sustainable aviation fuels are nurtured. The pace of fleet and traffic growth must not outstrip 
the pace at which safety regulation is resourced, trained personnel are produced, and institutional capacity is 
built. Growth  without governance is not progress; it is risk accumulation. The Committee's scrutiny has 
accordingly been directed not only at the adequacy of financial allocations, but at the alignment between 
budgetary intent and ground -level outcomes, with particular  attention to recurring patterns of under -utilisation, 
the pace of disinvestment of AIAHL subsidiaries, staffing vacancies in safety regulators, and the equity of 
regional distribution of infrastructure investment. 
5. The present Demands for Grants reflect  several significant policy transitions. The proposed Modified UDAN 
scheme to connect 120 new destinations, the extension of customs duty exemptions for MRO components and 
aircraft parts announced in Union Budget 2026 -27, the operationalisation of the PIAO  Act 2025 for aircraft 
leasing at GIFT-IFSC, the advancement of eVTOL regulations for Advanced Air Mobility, and the expansion of 
DigiYatra to 30 airports are among the substantive shifts that have informed the Committee's examination. The 
Committee has sought to assess whether these policy innovations are accompanied by the institutional capacity, 
budgetary provisioning, and monitoring frameworks necessary for effective implementation.  
6. In the course of its examination, the Committee held an oral evidenc e session with the Secretary and senior 
officials of the Ministry of Civil Aviation on 25 February 2026. The Committee scrutinized the following 
documents placed before it: 
(i) Detailed Demands for Grants (Demand No. 8) for the financial year 2026 -27; 
(ii) Annual Report of the Ministry of Civil Aviation (2025-26); 
(iii) Output Outcome Monitoring Framework (OOMF) for the financial year 2026 -27; 
(iv) Statement indicating the Budget Estimates, Revised Estimates and Actuals for the financial years 2023 -24, 
2024-25, 2025-26 (expenditure incurred up to January 2026) and Budget Estimates of 2026-27;

7. The Committee's examination encompassed six principal thematic areas: the overall financial structure of the 
Demands and its alignment with stated policy objectives; scheme -wise scrutiny of major programmes including 
the Regional Connectivity Scheme (UDAN), AIAHL SPV, MRO sector , Drone PLI, and DGCA; a review of 
past demand versus fulfilment across three financial years; cross -cutting tests of budget realism, regional equity, 
efficiency of outcome linkage, market concentration, and institutional governance; a dedicated assessment  of 
aviation safety in light of the events of 2025 -26; and a focused examination of technology adoption, human 
capital development, and sectoral reforms. The Committee's observations and recommendations across these 
areas are detailed in Chapters I through VII of this Report. 
8. This Report synthesises evidence received from the Ministry, the detailed budgetary documentation, the 
Ministry's written replies to its Questionnaire, the oral depositions during the evidence session of 25 February 
2026, and the Co mmittee's own analysis of multi -year financial trends and publicly available regulatory data. It 
places fiscal prudence, outcome accountability, safety oversight, and equitable regional development at the 
centre of its assessment. The Committee's fifty  three recommendations, presented in Chapter VI, are framed in a 
spirit of constructive engagement and are intended to support the Ministry in strengthening the planning, 
execution, and monitoring of its programmes. 
9. The Committee places on record its si ncere appreciation of the assistance rendered by the officers of the

Accident Investigation Bureau, and the Commission of Railway Safety in providing detailed documentation,

depth of this examination.

Executive Summary 
0.1.1 The Department -related Parliamentary Standing Committee on Transport, Tourism and Culture has the 
honour to present this Report on the Demands for Grants (Demand No. 8) of the Ministry of Civil Aviation for 
the financial year 2026 -27. The Committee scrutinized the detailed Demands for Grants, the Annual Report 
(2025-26), the Output-Outcome Monitoring Framework (OOMF) 2026-27, the Questionnaire responses, and the 
detailed explanatory notes furnished by the Ministry. 
0.1.2 The total budgetary allocation sought under Demand No. 8 is Rs. 2,102.87 crore, comprising Rs. 2,057.87 
crore under the Revenue Section and Rs. 45.00 crore under the Capital Section. In addition, Internal and Extra 
Budgetary Resources (I&EBR) of Rs. 4,699.92 crore are available to the Airports Authority of India (AAI). 
Approximately 36 per cent of the total Revenue allocation is earmarked for servicing legacy debt of Air India 
Asset Holding Limited (AIAHL SPV) at Rs. 757.39 crore, while the Regional Connectivity Scheme (UDAN) 
accounts for approximately 26.2 per cent at Rs. 550.00 crore. The discretionary developmental component 
within the Demand is accordingly modest. 
0.1.3 The scrutiny of the Demands for Grants for 2026 -27 reveals that the Ministry's budgetary architecture 
remains dominated by legacy liabilities and transfer payments. The Committee observes a structural tension 
between the Mini stry's expanding policy ambitions and the limited fiscal instruments available within this 
Demand. The bulk of airport infrastructure investment is financed through AAI's Internal and Extra Budgetary 
Resources, which do not form part of this demand. 
0.1.4 The Committee has scrutinized the Ministry's performance against stated OOMF targets. The RCS -UDAN 
has achieved 657 routes, 93 airports and 159 lakh passengers. India's ICAO USOAP Effective Implementation 
score has been raised to 85.65 per cent. The MRO se ctor has grown to 166 facilities, though only 14 per cent of 
total MRO expenditure by Indian carriers is handled domestically. DigiYatra has been operationalised at 30 
airports with over 2 crore enrolments. However, recurring challenges in expenditure util isation persist: AIAHL 
SPV showed only 29.6 per cent utilisation and RCS only 27.3 per cent as of January 2026, raising concerns 
about end-year bunching. 
0.1.5 The Committee observed that the allocation for AIAHL SPV has been reduced from Rs. 945.51 crore (BE 
2025-26) to Rs. 757.39 crore (BE 2026 -27) on account of progressive receipt of monetisation and rental 
proceeds. However, the disinvestment of AIAHL subsidiaries remains at a preliminary stage. The Committee 
observed that the pace of the disinvestment process warrants close monitoring. 
0.1.6 The Committee has also scrutinized staffing vacancies in the safety regulators and notes with concern the 
48.3 per cent vacancy rate in DGCA (787 of 1,630 posts vacant) and the 30.3 per cent vacancy rate in BCAS 
(181 of 598 posts vacant). Given the rapid expansion of the regulated entity universe and the pattern of safety 
incidents and fleet defects documented during 2025 -26, the Committee are of the view that these vacancies 
represent a structural challenge to the regulatory capacity and safety oversight of the sector. 
0.1.7 The Committee has further scrutinized the regional distribution of AAI's infrastructure investment and 
observes a significant imbalance. Of 23 major ongoing airport projects with a combined award  cost exceeding 
Rs. 10,932 crore, only one project is in the North Eastern Region (Imphal, Rs. 421.57 crore), representing 3.9 
per cent of the total. No ongoing project exists in seven of the eight NER states. This is at variance with the 
Ministry's stated priority of NER connectivity. 
0.1.8 The Committee has given particular attention to aviation safety in this Report, in light of the Ahmedabad 
crash of June 2025 (260 fatalities), charter aircraft crashes, a DGCA audit finding recurring technical defects i n 
nearly half of 754 aircraft inspected, and 19 safety violation notices issued by DGCA by late 2025. The 
Committee are of the considered view that the safety architecture of the sector requires a fundamental reset.  
0.1.9 This Report details the Committee' s analysis of the financial structure, scheme -wise performance, past 
demands versus fulfilment, cross -cutting thematic issues, and aviation safety, concluding with fifty  three 
actionable observations and  recommendations in Chapter VI to enhance the efficac y of public expenditure and 
safety oversight in the civil aviation sector.

CHAPTER 1 
Overall Assessment of the Ministry's Demands 
1.1 Role and Mandate of the Ministry 
1.1.1 The Ministry of Civil Aviation is the nodal Ministry for the formulation of national policies and 
programmes for the development and regulation of the Civil Aviation sector in the country. The 
Ministry's jurisdiction encompasses airports, air transport, a ir traffic services and the carriage of 
passengers and goods by air. 
1.1.2 The Ministry informed the Committee that, in addition to its primary policy -formulation 
functions, it supervises implementation by constituent organisations of special programmes of  the 
Government, particularly those intended for weaker sections of society and for promoting balanced 
regional development. 
1.2 Key Policy Priorities for 2026-27 
1.2.1 The Ministry submitted that the focus areas for 2026 -27 include: (a) the Regional Conne ctivity 
Scheme (UDAN), including the proposed modified UDAN scheme to connect 120 new destinations; 
(b) strengthening the regulatory and safety oversight framework through DGCA; (c) promotion of the 
MRO sector through tax rationalisation and customs duty exemptions announced in the Union Budget 
2026-27; (d) advancement of aircraft leasing and financing activities through GIFT -IFSC; (e) 
development of Greenfield airports and expansion of existing airport infrastructure; (f) promotion of 
the drone ecosystem; (g) digital transformation initiatives including DigiYatra and eGCA; and (h) 
steps towards Advanced Air Mobility (AAM) through eVTOL regulations. 
1.2.2 The Committee noted the breadth of the Ministry's stated policy agenda. However, the 
Committee observed that the budgetary instruments available under Demand No. 8 are 
disproportionately consumed by legacy liabilities (AIAHL SPV) and the RCS, leaving limited scope 
for direct fiscal support to several of the new priorities enumerated above. 
1.3 Scale and Composition of the Demand 
1.3.1 The total allocation under Demand No. 8 for 2026 -27 is Rs. 2,102.87 crore. The Revenue 
Section accounts for Rs. 2,057.87 crore and the Capital Section for Rs. 45.00 crore. The composition 
is summarised in Table 1.1 below.

1.3.2 The Committee notes that over 62 per cent of the total Demand is accounted for by AIAHL

investment and developmental activities funded through budgetary support. The Airports Authority of 
India, which undertakes the bulk of airport infrastructure investment, is funded through Internal and 
Extra Budgetary Resources of Rs. 4,699.92 crore, which do not form part of this demand. 
1.4 Growth of the Civil Aviation Sector 
1.4.1 The Ministry submitted that India's civil aviation sector has been on a robust growth trajectory. 
The cumulative airport capacity has risen from 525 MPPA in 2023 -24 to 534 MPPA in 2024 -25 and 
574 MPPA in the first three quarters of 2025 -26. The  scheduled airline fleet has grown from 716 
aircraft to approximately 860 aircraft over the past five years, with an average fleet age of 7.3 years 
compared to the global average of 14.8 years. Indian airlines have placed orders for over 1,600 new 
aircraft to cater to projected demand growth. The number of operational airports has expanded from 
74 in 2014 to approximately 163 by 2025-26, with a long-term target of 350-400 airports by 2047. 
1.4.2 The Committee noted the sectoral growth trajectory and observed that the expansion of the fleet 
and the order book of over 1,600 new aircraft will place substantial additional demands on the 
regulatory, safety and infrastructure capacity of the sector. The Committee observed that the 
alignment between the pace of fle et growth and the pace of regulatory capacity -building merits 
careful monitoring, particularly given the safety events documented during 2025-26. 
1.5 AAI Revenue Buoyancy and Budgetary Paradox 
1.5.1 The Committee observed a striking paradox in the Ministry 's financial architecture. While AAI 
reported revenue of Rs. 20,648.25 crore and profit of Rs. 7,233.28 crore in FY 2024 -25, and Airport 
Lease Revenue surged thirty -six-fold from Rs. 87.25 crore (FY 2020 -21) to Rs. 3,192.75 crore (FY 

3 
 
2024-25), the Ministry's own Demand (No. 8) declined from Rs. 3,113.36 crore (BE 2023 -24) to Rs. 
2,102.87 crore (BE 2026-27), a reduction of 32.5 per cent over three years. The Committee notes that 
this creates a structural disconnect: the entity generating the bulk of sectoral  revenue (AAI) operates 
outside parliamentary budgetary oversight, while the entity exercising budgetary authority (the 
Ministry) administers an increasingly constrained and legacy -dominated Demand. The Committee is 
of the view that this disconnect warrant s institutional correction through enhanced parliamentary 
visibility of AAI's capital investment programme.

informed that as of the date of examination, RCS -UDAN has operationalised 657 routes connecting 
93 airports and has carried 159 lakh passengers since inception.

CHAPTER 5A 
Aviation Safety — A Systemic Assessment 
5A.1 The Committee has examined the state of aviation safety with particular attention in this Report, 
given the gravity of events during the period under review. The Ahmedabad crash of 12 June 2025, in 
which Air In dia Flight AI171, a Boeing 787 -8 Dreamliner, crashed shortly after take -off killing 260 
persons and leaving one survivor, represents the deadliest aviation disaster in India in recent decades. 
The preliminary investigation by AAIB revealed that both engine s abruptly stopped receiving fuel 
within seconds of each other shortly after take-off, with cockpit recordings indicating pilot confusion. 
On 12 February 2026, AAIB stated that media reports suggesting the crash probe had been concluded 
were "incorrect and speculative" and that the investigation remains in progress. 
5A.2 The Committee noted that in the six months preceding July 2025, Air India received nine show -
cause notices for safety violations from DGCA. By late 2025, DGCA had issued 19 safety violation  
notices across operators, citing breaches of Flight Duty Time Limitations, lapses in quality assurance, 
unauthorised cockpit access, and aircraft operated with expired emergency equipment. In February 
2026, DGCA imposed a penalty of approximately Rs. 1 cr ore on Air India for operating an Airbus 
A320 on eight commercial flights without a valid Airworthiness Review Certificate on 24 -25 
November 2025. 
5A.3 The Committee further note that DGCA issued a sweeping safety warning to Non -Scheduled 
Operator Permit holders following the Jharkhand air ambulance crash of February 2026, announcing 
measures including mandatory public disclosure of aircraft age and maintenance history, a safety 
ranking system for NSOPs, licence suspensions of up to five years for FDTL viol ations, and personal 
accountability for senior leadership of operators for systemic non -compliance. DGCA also grounded 
four aircraft operated by one NSOP after discovering multiple violations during a special safety audit. 
5A.4 The Committee observed that while these enforcement actions are welcome, they are reactive in 
character. The pattern of repeated safety violations, recurring technical defects in nearly half the 
audited fleet, and multiple crashes within a single year poi nts to structural inadequacies in the 
preventive safety architecture. The Committee are of the considered view that India's aviation safety 
framework requires a fundamental reset that addresses root causes: regulatory staffing, surveillance 
depth, maintenance oversight, pilot training quality, and crew fatigue management. 
5A.5 Pilot-to-Aircraft Ratio and Crew Buffer Capacity.  The Committee notes that India's pilot-to-
aircraft ratio of approximately 14 is below the global benchmark of 18 -20. Globally, airlines maintain 
20-25 per cent spare crew capacity to absorb operational shocks; Indian carriers operate at near -total 
utilisation, allowing minor disruptions to cascade across networks. Between 2020 and 2024, 
approximately 5,700 Commercial Pilot Licences were issued, against a projected requirement of 7,000 
pilots between 2024 and 2026 and 25,000 -30,000 over the next decade. Air lines have resorted to 
hiring foreign pilots, with 236 temporary licences issued in 2025, a costly and unsustainable approach. 
The Committee observed that the pilot shortage is not merely a quantity problem but a systemic 
structural deficit that constrains operational resilience. 
5A.6 Passenger Rights and Complaint Redressal.  The Committee noted that during oral evidence, 
significant concerns were raised about passenger service quality, particularly on Air India 
international flights. The Ministry informed that a 24/7 Air Service helpline (2463 2987) was 
established. The Committee observed that the establishment of a helpline is necessary but not 
sufficient. India lacks a comprehensive statutory passenger rights framework comparable to those in 

14 
 
other major aviation markets. The Committee are of the view that a formal Passenger Rights Charter, 
backed by an enforceable compensation framework, is overdue for a market carrying over 350 million 
passengers annually.

6.11 Geopolitical Resilience and Inter-ministerial Coordination 
6.11.1 Observation 51 — Impact of War in Gulf on India’s Civil Aviation 
The Committee noted with concern the closure and restriction of airspace across several countries in 
West Asia, beginning 28 February 2026, arising from the escalating Iran –Israel–United States 
conflict, which has posed significant and multi-dimensional challenges to India’s civil aviation sector. 
As roughly half of India’s int ernational passenger traffic transits through Gulf hubs such as Dubai, 
Doha and Abu Dhabi, the disruption led to cancellation of over 2,600 flights by Indian carriers within 
the first week, with 410 flights cancelled on 28 February and 444 on 1 March alone , stranding an 
estimated 60,000 passengers on the first day. Airspace closures by Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, Qatar, Bahrain, 
the UAE and Israel effectively shut down the primary air corridor linking Asia, Europe and Africa. 
Airlines were forced to reroute westbou nd flights via the Indian Ocean and Mediterranean airspace, 
adding 2 to 4 hours of flight time and substantially increasing fuel consumption. Block times on the 
Dubai–Delhi sector rose from 3 hours 30 minutes to nearly 6 hours. The Committee appreciated th e 
Government’s swift and decisive evacuation response: over 2,20,000 Indian nationals were facilitated 
to return from Gulf countries between 1 and 16 March 2026, including 32,107 on Indian carriers in 
the first week alone, with the Ministry of External Aff airs establishing a dedicated Special Control 
Room, 24/7 Embassy helplines, and coordinating land border crossings from Iran into Armenia and 
Azerbaijan. The Committee further observed that the disruption has had cascading consequences 
across three critical dimensions. First, on international route viability: carriers have been compelled to 
suspend, curtail or reroute services, with Air India alone suspending 50 westbound flights on 1 March, 
and Emirates, Qatar Airways and Etihad temporarily halting all ope rations. Second, on Air Turbine 
Fuel supply and pricing: global jet fuel prices surged by 58.4 per cent to USD 157.41 per barrel in the 
week ending 6 March 2026 (IATA/S&P Global Platts), crude oil briefly touched USD 119.50 per 
barrel on 9 March, and ATF i n India was revised upward by Rs. 5,215 per kilolitre effective 1 March 
2026 to Rs. 96,638 per kilolitre, prompting Air India, IndiGo and Akasa Air to impose fuel surcharges 
ranging from Rs. 199 to Rs. 2,300 per sector. Third, on domestic flight frequency:  the redeployment 

27 
 
of aircraft and crew for longer international routings, combined with elevated fuel costs, has reduced 
the fleet and crew available for domestic operations, with the Civil Aviation Minister acknowledging 
that the ATF price impact would be  felt from April 2026 and that the matter requires a multi -
departmental exercise involving the Ministry of Civil Aviation, the Ministry of External Affairs, and 
the Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas. 
6.11.2 The Committee observed with particular concer n that the rapid escalation in international 
crude oil prices arising from the West Asia crisis has placed the Indian airline industry under acute 
financial stress. With ATF priced on the basis of international crude benchmarks and constituting 35 
to 40 per cent of airline operating costs, the burden on domestic carriers has become severe. Airlines 
face a stark choice between incurring unsustainable losses or grounding capacity, both of which 
outcomes are against the larger public interest of maintaining ai r connectivity across the country. The 
Committee noted that between 2022 and 2024, the Government had instituted a mechanism capping 
the floor and ceiling of ATF pricing for domestic airlines, which provided a measure of stability 
during a period of global  price volatility. The Committee is of the considered view that the present 
crisis, which is more severe in its scale and duration than earlier episodes, warrants the revival of a 
similar ATF pricing stabilisation arrangement in the public interest. The Co mmittee accordingly 
recommends that the Ministry of Civil Aviation and the Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas jointly 
and urgently examine the feasibility of reinstating a structured ATF pricing band for domestic airline 
operations for the duration of t he crisis, calibrated to protect both airline viability and passenger 
affordability, while ensuring that Oil Marketing Companies are not subjected to uncompensated 
under-recoveries. 
6.11.3 Recommendation: The Committee recommends that the Ministry of Civil Aviation, building on 
the Government’s demonstrated and commendable crisis response capability including the evacuation 
of over 2,20,000 nationals, develop a comprehensive inter -ministerial Aviation Resilience and 
Diversification Framework (ARDF) in coord ination with the Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas, 
the Ministry of External Affairs, the Ministry of Home Affairs, the Airports Authority of India, and 
the Directorate General of Civil Aviation, structured on three time-bound pillars: 
(a) Accelerate p oint-to-point long -haul services: Incentivise Indian carriers to launch and expand 
direct wide -body services from emerging and secondary hubs (Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Chennai, 
Kolkata, Pune) to Europe and North America within 12 months, progressively reducin g dependence 
on Gulf transit. Fast-track slot allocation, wet-lease approvals, and bilateral air service agreements on 
under-used routes, in close coordination with the Ministry of External Affairs. The Committee notes 
that Turkish Airlines continued operations via Istanbul and European carriers (Air France, Lufthansa, 
British Airways) maintained US–India connectivity by avoiding Gulf airspace entirely, demonstrating 
the viability of non-Gulf routing. 
(b) Identify and secure alternative transit hubs: Conclu de overflight and traffic -right arrangements 
with non -Gulf nodes (Istanbul, Athens, Frankfurt in Europe; Nairobi in Africa; and Singapore in 
South-East Asia) to provide contingency connectivity when Gulf hubs are compromised. Leverage 
existing air -service agreements and, where needed, negotiate temporary contingency clauses under 
ICAO Annex 9. The Committee notes that the Government has already identified ‘safe airports’

6.12 Helicopter Safety Policy  
6.12.1 Observations 52 - Institutionalizing Helicopter Safety 
The Committee observes a systemic gap in safety oversight within the charter sector of the aviation 
industry, transitioning from a "lightly regulated adjunct" to a high -risk sector. Fat al incidents —
specifically the June 15, 2025, Aryan Aviation crash (Bell 407) and the non -fatal February 24, 2026, 
Pawan Hans sea crash -landing—expose a "pressure culture" where the consideration for commercial 
revenue and VIP schedules tends to override "N o-Go" weather decisions during the high -density 
pilgrim rush. Technical evidence points to recurring mechanical instability, including stuck collective 
controls and tail rotor malfunctions, which are lethal in high -altitude or maritime corridors when 
flying single-engine helicopters that lack power redundancy. Furthermore, the absence of localized 
Air Traffic Control (ATC) and 24 -hour weather stations, coupled with chronic Flight Duty Time 
Limitation (FDTL) violations, indicates that the current regulatory framework is inadequate in 
protecting the lives of the crews and passengers operating the 250+ registered units. 
6.12.2 Recommendation: The Ministry of Civil Aviation (MoCA) may consider incorporating a 
dedicated Helicopter Wing within the DGCA, led by tec hnical aviation professionals, to 
institutionalize specialized maintenance audits and ex -military pilot "familiarization" training, since 
the drive to reach a destination or complete a task overrides prudent safety decisions, such as delaying 
or cancelling a flight due to adverse weather, when ex -military pilots fly civilian aircraft. A statutory 
mandate could be considered under the aegis of a high -powered committee to transition all high -risk 
pilgrimage and inter -island routes to twin -engine helicopters o nly, providing a vital safety buffer 
against mid -air technical snags. To manage operational saturation, the DGCA must fast -track the 
deployment of localized ATC systems and automated weather interlocks that prohibit takeoffs during 
"poor visibility" windows. Finally, the regulator must enforce transparency by ranking the 133 Non -
Scheduled Operators (NSOs) on safety performance, auditing ADS -B (Automatic Dependent 
Surveillance Broadcast) and CVR (Cockpit Voice Recorder) data to ensure the 12 -hour rest mandate 
is met, and fixing accountability for any flight launched with known mechanical deficiencies or under 
commercial duress. 
6.13 Aviation Safety Pertaining to VIP and Charter Flights 

29 
 
6.13.1 Observation 53 
The Committee notes with concern that incidents invo lving chartered jets ferrying VIPs point to a 
recurring pattern of operational vulnerabilities. Evidence suggests that adverse weather conditions, 
including poor visibility, have frequently been a contributing factor. The Committee is particularly 
concerned that pilots may, at times, face implicit or explicit pressure to adhere to demanding 
schedules, even when prevailing conditions fall below safe operational thresholds. 
6.13.2 The Committee further observes that the sterile cockpit rule, which prohibits non-essential 
communication during critical phases of flight, is not consistently respected during VIP charter 
operations. This introduces avoidable distraction at moments demanding absolute crew attention. 
6.13.3 The Committee also notes that frequent las t-minute schedule changes impose considerable 
strain on flight crew, compelling already fatigued personnel to extend duty hours beyond safe limits. 
This raises concerns regarding crew alertness and sound decision-making. 
6.13.4 The Committee places on reco rd its appreciation for the DGCA's firm position that no 
commercial or logistical consideration may override any safety parameter. 
6.14 Recommendations 
(a) When visibility falls below prescribed safety minima, diversion to a better -equipped airport 
must be the only acceptable course of action. The sterile cockpit rule must be strictly enforced across 
all charter and VIP operations. 
(b) Flight Duty Time Limitations must be enforced uniformly, without exception, across all 
categories of flights. Pilot trainin g must mandatorily cover weather awareness and sound decision -
making in uncontrolled environments. 
(c) The Committee further emphasises that ex -military pilots transitioning to civilian aviation 
must undergo structured familiarisation training. In civil av iation, delaying or cancelling a flight on 
safety grounds is not a failure of resolve. It is an affirmation of professional responsibility.
