INTRODUCTION 
 The Department of Atomic Energy (DAE), came into being on August 3, 1954, 
and has been engaged in the development of nuclear power technology, application of 
radiation technologies in the fields of agriculture, health care, industry and basic 
research. 
1.2  The Atomic Energy Commission (AEC) is the apex body for formulation of 
policies and direction of the programmes. The Commission implements its policies and 
programmes through the Department of Atomic Energy (DAE). The programmes of 
DAE have been divided into four sectors viz. Research and Development, Industries, 
Minerals and Power Sector. 
1.3  The programmes being followed by DAE, can be briefly summed up as follows: 
 Increasing the share of nuclear power through deployment of indigenous and 
other proven technologies and also developing fast breeder reactors and thorium 
reactors with associated fuel cycle facilities; 
 Building and operation of research reactors for production of radioisotopes; 
developing radiation technology applications in the field of health care, 
agriculture and industry, cancer care, water related technology, waste 
management etc.; 
 Designing and developing advanced technologies such as accelerators, lasers, 
supercomputers, advanced materials and instrumentation and encouraging 
transfer of technology to industry; 
 Providing support to basic research in nuclear energy and related frontier areas 
of science; interaction with universities and academic institutions; supporting 
academic programmes having a bearing on DAE’s activities; and International 
co-operation in related advanced areas of research and contribution to national 
security. 
1.4 DAE’s programmes are multidisciplinary in nature. For their execution, DAE 
has evolved abroad based organizational structure comprising research centres, 
industrial units, public sector undertakings and fully aided institutions, along with 
service organizations. These organizations are engaged in research, technology 
development and commercial operations in the areas of nuclear power and associated 
technologies, radiation technologies and their applications, basic research and academic 
programmes. 
1.5 The Department is also engaged in the development of radiation technologies 
and their applications for better crop varieties, techniques for crops protection, radiation 
based post-harvest technologies, techniques for radio-diagnosis and radiotherapy of 
diseases particularly cancer, technologies for safe drinking water, better environment 
and industrial growth.

1.6 Over the years, the Department has been able to establish a network of 
institutions engaged in R&D as well as industrial activities and acquired expertise in all 
aspects of the nuclear fuel cycle. The first stage of the nuclear power programme is in 
commercial domain and the second stage has been launched with the ongoing 
construction of 500 MWe Prototype Fast Breeder Reactor (PFBR). 
1.7 Nuclear Power Corporation of India Limited (NPCIL) formed in 1987, is a 
Public Sector Enterprise under the administrative control of DAE. NPCIL is responsible 
for siting, design, construction, commissioning and operation of nuclear power reactors. 
Safety is given overriding priority in all facets of nuclear power reactors. At presents, 
NPCIL is operating 22 nuclear power reactors with an installed capacity of 6780 MW.

2.5 The Committee recommends that the DAE should adopt a more robust 
approach to financial planning and forecasting by incorporating detailed risk 
assessments and contingency allocations into the initial budget estimates. The 
Committee notes that the Revised Estimates have consistently exceeded the Budget 
Estimates. The Committee therefore urges the Department to implement regular 
internal and external audits and to ensure transparent reporting of any mid-year 
adjustments, with clear justifications for deviations from the initial allocations. 
Furthermore, it is recommended that standard operating procedures for urgent 
procurement and maintenance should be established to pre-empt emergency 
expenditures, while also integrating anticipated costs for global collaborations and 
new technologies into the budgetary framework.  
2.6 The Committee expresses major concern over the substantial gap between 
the budgetary allocations and actual utilization by the DAE. In 2023-24, the actual 
expenditure of ₹35,885.93 crore was closely aligned with the RE. However, in 
2024-25, actual expenditure stood at ₹29,051.95 crore, a mere 80.34% utilization of 
BE allocations, falling significantly short of both BE and RE by nearly ₹7,500 
crore. The Committee acknowledges that the Ministry of Finance imposed a 
budgetary cut of 13.47% on the proposed RE for 2024-25 which led to reduction in 
the allocation from the projected ₹42,256.77 crore to ₹36,564.24 crore. The 
Department informed the Committee that it has sought additional funds through the 2nd 
batch of Supplementary Demand for Grants (SDG), which, if fully allocated, would 
allow the Department to incur an expenditure of up to ₹37,362 crore during the fiscal 
year. A significant portion of the additional funds requested under the SDG is 
earmarked for critical payments, including ₹1,710.43 crore for the procurement of 
nuclear fuel from M/s TVEL, Russia, ₹595.42 crore towards India's commitments to the 
International Thermonuclear Experimental Reactor (ITER) project, and ₹5,900 crore for 
committed liabilities of various units, aided institutions, and Public Sector Undertakings 
(PSUs) under the capital and revenue sections. 
2.7 The Committee recommends that the Department continue to streamline 
fund disbursal processes to ensure optimal utilization of allocated resources, 
particularly for multi-year atomic energy projects. The Committee also 
recommends that a more robust financial strategy be adopted, incorporating 
contingency planning to mitigate the impact of unexpected budget cuts. 
Additionally, the Committee urges the Ministry of Finance to consider the critical 
nature of the Department’s commitments, particularly those related to nuclear 
fuel procurement and international obligations, and to ensure that adequate 
funding is provided in a timely manner. The Committee urges upon the 
Department to implement these measures with immediate effect to restore the 
expenditure trajectory to match both the budget estimates and the requirements of 
multi-year projects. The Department took note of Committee’s observations and 
assured that the Department would fully utilize the allocations by the end of FY 2024-
25 and the actual expenditure would correspond with the figures for the last three years.

2.10 The Committee notes the significant 20% reduction in allocations to 
Industry & Mineral (I&M) Sector from the proposed ₹13,148.65 crore to the 
approved allocations of ₹10,510.45 crore. The DAE in a written reply informed the 
Committee that the cuts would impact uranium and rare earth exploration, affecting: 
(a)  Atomic Minerals Directorate for Exploration and Research (AMD), which 
oversees uranium mining and rare metal extraction; 
(b) IREL (India) Limited's rare earth processing initiatives, including an Indo-
Kazakh Joint Venture for titanium production; and 
(c) Delayed sub-surface drilling and mineral recovery projects. 
 
2.11 The Committee observes with concern that the Research & Development 
(R&D) Sector is the most impacted amongst all, with the R&D sector suffering the 
largest reduction of ₹3,289.60 crore, from a proposed BE of ₹14,516.38 crore to the 
approved BE ₹11,226.78 crore.  The Committee observes that the shortfall of 
₹3,289 crore would impact long-term research initiatives under BARC, IGCAR, 
and RRCAT, slowing down innovations in nuclear reactor safety, radioactive 
waste management, and new reactor technologies.The Mega Science projects, 
which include research on fusion energy and directed research, may also face 
delays. 
2.12  Upon enquiry by the Committee on the impact of shortfall in budgetary 
allocations for the year 2025-26, on the activities of the Department, the DAE stated 
that the substantial reduction in budgetary allocation will slow down activities related to 
basic research and derail the progress achieved over the years which shapes up into 
technologies over a considerable period of time. The DAE further stated that reduction 
in fund allocation would impact the high priority activities of developmental nature 
which have potential in transforming rural or industrial landscape. In response to these 
challenges, DAE outlined several mitigation strategies. The Department has committed 
to prioritising high-impact and time-sensitive projects, ensuring that operational power 
plants and essential fuel cycle activities continue without disruption. It also plans to 
optimise costs by reallocating resources within its existing budget, implementing 
austerity measures to minimise the financial strain. Furthermore, DAE explained to the 
Committee that it is exploring Public-Private Partnerships (PPP) for infrastructure 
development, particularly in the Bharat Small Reactors (BSR) initiative, to attract 
private investment into the nuclear sector. 
2.13 The Committee recognises the critical role of nuclear energy in India's energy 
security, scientific progress, and healthcare advancements. While DAE has outlined 
strategies to mitigate the impact of budget shortfalls, the risks posed by inadequate 
funding remain significant. The Committee acknowledges the fact that slowdown of 
nuclear research, isotope production, and uranium exploration may have lasting 
consequences on India’s scientific progress and energy security. The Committee, 
therefore, strongly recommends immediate action to restore essential funding, 
support nuclear research, and ensure uninterrupted progress in India’s nuclear 
energy and medical applications. The Committee recommends urgent restoration 
of at least ₹5,933.62 crore, particularly for the R&D and I&M sectors, to ensure 
continued progress in nuclear technology development and fuel production. 
2.14 The Committee supports DAE’s initiative to attract private investment. 
However, the Committee recommends that clear regulatory frameworks and risk 
mitigation strategies should be developed to ensure private sector participation in 
the critical and sensitive nuclear infrastructure projects. While the Committee

3.1 BHABHA ATOMIC RESEARCH CENTRE (BARC) 
 The Bhabha Atomic Research Centre (BARC) is India's premier 
multidisciplinary nuclear research institution under the Department of Atomic Energy 

13 
 
(DAE). Established in 1954 as the Atomic Energy Establishment, Trombay (AEET) and 
renamed BARC in 1966 in honour of Dr. Homi Bhabha, it has been instrumental in 
advancing nuclear science, technology, and energy security in India. BARC plays a 
crucial role in India's three-stage nuclear power programme, covering nuclear reactor 
research, fuel fabrication, waste management, isotope applications, and nuclear safety. 
The centre is also responsible for healthcare, agriculture, water purification, and 
radiation technology applications, reinforcing its impact beyond energy production. 
BARC is central to the implementation of India’s indigenous three-stage nuclear 
programme, designed to achieve energy security through the optimal utilisation of 
nuclear fuel resources. 
Stage I – Pressurised Heavy Water Reactors (PHWRs): BARC develops 
reactor safety technologies, nuclear fuel fabrication, and in-service reactor 
inspection tools to enhance the efficiency and safety of PHWRs. 
Stage II – Fast Breeder Reactors (FBRs): The centre contributes to spent fuel 
reprocessing, sodium-cooled fast reactor technology, and advanced fuel 
fabrication to sustain the second stage of the programme. 
Stage III – Thorium-based Reactors: BARC plays a vital role in research on 
Advanced Heavy Water Reactors (AHWRs) and Thorium fuel cycle 
technologies, leveraging India's vast thorium reserves. 
BARC has designed and operates research reactors such as Dhruva, Apsara-U, 
and KAMINI, which are essential for neutron beam research and isotope production. 
The centre is actively developing high-temperature gas-cooled reactors (HTGRs), 
molten salt reactors, and small modular reactors (SMRs) to explore future nuclear 
energy possibilities.BARC develops uranium and thorium fuel fabrication techniques, 
supporting India's expanding nuclear power infrastructure.The centre operates 
reprocessing plants for recovering plutonium and uranium, crucial for Fast Breeder 
Reactor technology. 
BARC is responsible for managing several research and industrial facilities that 
contribute to nuclear technology and allied fields. 
 (i) Research and Development Centres 
Indira Gandhi Centre for Atomic Research (IGCAR): Focuses on Fast 
Breeder Reactor (FBR) technology, materials science, and reactor engineering. 
Raja Ramanna Centre for Advanced Technology (RRCAT): Specialises in 
lasers, accelerators, and advanced optics for nuclear applications. 
Variable Energy Cyclotron Centre (VECC): Engaged in particle accelerator 
research, medical isotope production, and high-energy physics. 
(ii) Industrial and Service Organisations 
Nuclear Fuel Complex (NFC): Manufactures fuel bundles for PHWRs and 
BWRs, producing fuel components for India's nuclear power plants. 
Heavy Water Board (HWB): Manages the production and supply of heavy 
water (D2O) for nuclear reactors and industrial applications. 
Board of Radiation and Isotope Technology (BRIT): Engages in radioisotope 
production, nuclear medicine, and radiation technology applications for 
agriculture and healthcare. 
 
 
 

14 
 
(iii) Public Sector Undertakings (PSUs) Under DAE 
Nuclear Power Corporation of India Limited (NPCIL): Responsible for the 
construction and operation of commercial nuclear power plants in India. 
Bharatiya Nabhikiya Vidyut Nigam Limited (BHAVINI): Develops and 
operates Fast Breeder Reactors, essential for India's nuclear energy future. 
Uranium Corporation of India Limited (UCIL): Engaged in uranium mining 
and processing, a critical part of the nuclear fuel supply chain. 
3.2 The Committee notes that during the year 2023-24, BARC focused on 
several key areas, including nuclear waste management, isotope production for

the installation of an Indian Pressurised Water Reactor (IPWR) test facility, and the 
development of an indigenous network security system. Additionally, R&D efforts in 
post-irradiation examination of reactor materials and isotope production for cancer 
treatment were prioritised. 
For 2024-25, BARC undertook several crucial projects, including upgrading 
nuclear waste reprocessing systems, enhancing reactor safety features, and conducting 
feasibility studies on new-generation nuclear reactors. The installation of high-dose 
neutron irradiation facilities and the expansion of computational research capabilities 
were also among the key activities. 
     BARC has outlined priority areas for the financial year 2025-26, including: 
 Upgradation and operation of research reactors such as Dhruva and Apsara-U 
for isotope production and nuclear research. 
 Advancing reactor technology to support the Indian Nuclear Power Program, 
including work on Pressurized Heavy Water Reactors (PHWRs), Fast Breeder 
Reactors (FBRs), and Advanced Heavy Water Reactors (AHWRs). 
 Strengthening fuel cycle infrastructure, including fuel fabrication and waste 
reprocessing. 
 R&D in material sciences, astrophysics, and nuclear safety, with investments in 
sensors, detectors, and reactor shielding technologies. 
 Deployment of nuclear applications in agriculture, food preservation, and cancer 
treatment through isotope-based technologies. 
 Expansion of water purification technologies and enhancement of nuclear 
medicine applications.

3.19 Heavy Water Facilities 
The Heavy Water Board (HWB) under the DAE plays a critical role in India's 
nuclear programme by ensuring a steady supply of heavy water (D₂O) for Pressurised 
Heavy Water Reactors (PHWRs). These reactors constitute a major portion of India's 
nuclear power generation capacity, and any disruption in heavy water supply could 
affect reactor operations and power generation. HWB is also involved in the production 
of deuterium-based pharmaceuticals, enriched boron, and nuclear solvents, which are 
essential for fuel reprocessing, radiation shielding, and medical applications. The 
Department informed the Committee about the key activities undertaken in 2024-25 and 
planned for 2025-26, the notable ones being: 

18 
 
(i) Modernisation and Expansion of Heavy Water Facilities 
Upgrading ageing H₂S-based heavy water plants, including the retrofit of 
pipelines, exhaust systems, and heat exchangers, has been prioritised to enhance 
operational efficiency. 
Recommissioning of Heavy Water Plant, Tuticorin, in April 2025 is expected to 
increase India's domestic heavy water production capacity. 
Expansion of nuclear solvent production facilities, including TBP, D2EPHA-II, 
and TOPO at HWP, Tuticorin, to support fuel reprocessing and industrial 
applications. 
(ii) Research & Development in Deuterium-Based Pharmaceuticals 
Development of deuterated analogues of anti-cancer drugs, with biological 
evaluation in collaboration with ACTREC, Mumbai. 
Studies on deuterium-depleted water (DDW) for cancer treatment to explore its 
potential as a novel therapeutic agent. 
(iii) Expansion of Boron Enrichment Facilities 
Installation of high-vacuum induction furnaces and CNC grinding machines at 
HWP, Manuguru, to increase the production of boron carbide pellets for the Fast 
Breeder Reactor (FBR) programme.

₹6,991.44 crore. The Committee observes that this reduction in allocation could 
adversely impact the modernisation of heavy water plants, delay the expansion of 
nuclear solvent production, and slow down critical research on deuterium-based 
pharmaceuticals. Given that heavy water is a strategic material crucial for India’s 
nuclear power programme, ensuring adequate funding is essential for energy

3.39 Bharatiya Nabhikiya Vidyut Nigam Ltd (BHAVINI) 
Bharatiya Nabhikiya Vidyut Nigam Limited (BHAVINI) is a Government 
Company under the administrative control of the Department of Atomic Energy (DAE) 
incorporated on 22nd October, 2003 as Public Limited Company under the Companies 
Act, 1956 with the objective of constructing and commissioning the first 500 MWe Fast 
Breeder Reactor (FBR) at Kalpakkam in Tamilnadu and to pursue construction,

generation of electricity in pursuance of the schemes and programmes of Government

constructing a 500MWe Prototype Fast Breeder Reactor (PFBR) at Kalpakkam, 70 Kms

and is expected to provide energy security to the country. The PFBR is being built with 
the design and technology developed at the Indira Gandhi Centre for Atomic Research 
(IGCAR) located at Kalpakkam. The PFBR is a First-Of-Its-Kind (FOAK) reactor, 
designed to utilise plutonium-based mixed oxide (MOX) fuel and sodium as a coolant, 
paving the way for large-scale deployment of Fast Breeder Reactors (FBRs) in India. 
The objectives of BHAVINI emanate from the following mandate: 
Providing energy security for the nation through Fast Breeder Technology based 
power stations commencing with 500 MWe Prototype Fast Breeder Reactor.

nuclear power stations. Strengthening the public awareness programmes for enhancing 
and improving the public perception of Nuclear Power in the country. Strengthening the 
QA activities relating to Fast Breeder Technology based nuclear power programme 
within the organization and those associated with it and increasing the overall share of 
nuclear power. 
  BHAVINI’s 500 MW PFBR is in the advanced stage of integrated 
commissioning, with expected first criticality by 2025-26. Some of the key 
accomplishments are: 
 (i) Completion of major structural work: Reactor containment, sodium cooling loops, and steam 
generator systems have been installed.  
 (ii) Modifications to reactor components: 376 subassemblies were modified to address higher 
extraction force issues and ensure seamless handling.  
(iii) Deployment of high-temperature materials: Advanced materials have been incorporated to 
enhance reactor efficiency and safety.  
(iv) Operationalisation of the Demonstration Fast Reactor Fuel Reprocessing Plant (DFRP): 
This facility, capable of handling both carbide and oxide fuels, was commissioned in 2024 and has 
successfully completed two fuel reprocessing campaigns. 
(v) Indigenous production of medical isotopes (Strontium-89): The PFBR has been used for the 
pilot-scale production of radioisotopes, contributing to nuclear medicine and cancer treatment.

4. 100 GW Nuclear Energy Mission for Viksit Bharat 2047 
The Secretary, Department of Atomic Energy (DAE) made a presentation before 
the Committee, outlining the 100 GW Nuclear Energy Mission, an ambitious plan to 
achieve 100 GW of nuclear power capacity by 2047 in alignment with India’s energy 
security and decarbonization goals. The Committee was informed that this mission aims 
to leverage private sector participation, streamline regulatory frameworks, and scale up 
nuclear power production to meet India’s increasing energy demands. The Committee 
recognizes the strategic importance of this initiative in ensuring a sustainable, low-
carbon energy future but acknowledges significant challenges that need to be 
addressed to make this target achievable. 
4.2 The Department in a written reply informed the Committee that the present 
installed nuclear power capacity stands at 8.18 GW, with an additional 7.3 GW under 
construction/commissioning and 7 GW already sanctioned and currently undergoing 
pre-project activities. Upon completion of these projects, India’s nuclear power capacity 
is expected to reach 22.48 GW by 2031-32. Beyond this, NPCIL plans to add another 
15.4 GW through indigenous Pressurised Heavy Water Reactors (PHWRs) and 17.6 
GW through Light Water Reactors (LWRs) with foreign cooperation, bringing the total 
installed capacity to 55 GW. Additionally, BHAVINI is expected to contribute 3.8 GW 
through Fast Breeder Reactors (FBRs), while the remainder of the capacity will come 
from Small Modular Reactors (SMRs), Bharat Small Reactors (BSRs), and other 
advanced nuclear technologies developed with private sector collaboration. The 
Committee notes that scaling up from the projected 55 GW to 100 GW by 2047 
will require substantial investment, regulatory reforms, and policy interventions. 
4.3 On being asked about the capital investments in the 100 GW Mission, the 
Department informed that around 50% of the 100 GW target is expected to come from 
Public-Private Partnerships (PPP). However, the exact public-private and indigenous-
imported mix will emerge as amendments to the Atomic Energy Act and Civil Liability 
for Nuclear Damage (CLND) Act are introduced, along with necessary regulatory 
reforms. 
4.4 The Committee acknowledges that achieving 100 GW of nuclear capacity 
by 2047 requires a structured and phased approach. Instead of focusing solely on a 
long-term 100 GW target, the Committee recommends phased capacity additions, 
with clear short-term (2030), medium-term (2040), and long-term (2047) 
milestones. The Committee recommends that 2031-32 projection of 22.48 GW 

25 
 
should serve as a baseline, with subsequent capacity additions planned in 
alignment with investment inflows, policy reforms, and domestic manufacturing 
capabilities. 
4.5 The Committee notes that nearly 50% of the 100 GW capacity is expected 
to come from private sector participation and recommends establishing a robust 
financial model that includes Government incentives, Viability Gap Funding 
(VGF), and sovereign guarantees to attract both domestic and foreign investments. 
The Committee suggests that legislative amendments to the Atomic Energy Act 
and Civil Liability for Nuclear Damage Act should be expedited to encourage 
private investment in nuclear power generation. 
4.6 The Committee emphasizes the importance of Small Modular Reactors 
(SMRs) and Bharat Small Reactors (BSRs) in achieving the 100 GW target. The 
Committee recommends that SMRs and BSRs should be deployed in remote 
regions and industrial zones to support decentralized energy generation. The 
Committee also urges the Department to accelerate Fast Breeder Reactor (FBR) 
deployment, with the 500 MW Prototype Fast Breeder Reactor (PFBR) expected 
to attain first criticality by March 2026 and completion by September 2026. 
5. Nuclear Energy Mission for R&D of Small Modular Reactors (SMRs) 
Secretary, DAE made a presentation before the Committee on Nuclear Energy

8.2 The Department of Atomic Energy (DAE) plays a critical role in advancing 
India's nuclear research, energy generation, and scientific innovation. However, a 
review of its workforce distribution by the Committee reveals significant vacancies 
across its various units, which could affect efficiency and operational effectiveness. As 

31 
 
observed by Table 4 above, one of the most crucial institutions under the DAE, the 
Bhabha Atomic Research Centre (BARC), has a sanctioned strength of 14,445 
personnel, but only 10,604 employees are currently in position, leaving 3,841 
vacancies. This results in a vacancy rate of 26.6%, indicating a substantial shortage of 
skilled personnel in key research and technical positions. Similarly, the Indira Gandhi 
Centre for Atomic Research (IGCAR), which is responsible for advanced nuclear 
research and fast breeder reactor development, has 2,134 employees in position against 
a sanctioned strength of 2,730, leaving 596 vacancies. This 21.8% vacancy rate could 
slow down research projects and affect the development of next-generation nuclear 
technologies. An even more concerning situation exists in the Heavy Water Board 
(HWB), which is responsible for producing and supplying heavy water for nuclear 
reactors. With a sanctioned strength of 3,872 personnel, only 2,015 employees are in 
position, leaving 1,857 vacancies. This represents a staggering 48% vacancy rate, which 
could impact reactor operations and nuclear fuel production. 
8.3 NPCIL which operates India’s nuclear power plants, has a sanctioned workforce 
of 15,088 employees, but only 10,745 are in position, leading to 4,343 vacancies. This 
represents a 28.8% vacancy rate, which could affect the efficiency and safety of nuclear 
power plant operations. The Atomic Minerals Directorate (AMD), responsible for the 
exploration of atomic minerals such as uranium and thorium, also faces significant 
shortages. With 2,495 sanctioned posts, only 1,699 employees are in position, leaving 
796 vacancies, translating to a 31.9% vacancy rate, which could impact India's ability to 
expand its nuclear fuel reserves.A notable concern is the vacancy rate at the Tata 
Institute of Fundamental Research (TIFR), which is one of India's leading scientific 
research institutions. With a sanctioned strength of 1,448 employees, only 609 are in 
position, leaving 839 vacancies. This results in a 58% vacancy rate, reflecting a 
significant shortfall in scientific manpower. Such a high vacancy rate suggests that 
TIFR is heavily reliant on contractual and research-based staff rather than regular 
employees. Given the institute's crucial role in fundamental research, this issue requires 
immediate attention to ensure stability in long-term scientific projects. 
8.4 The Tata Memorial Centre (TMC), India's premier cancer treatment and 
research institution under the DAE, has a sanctioned strength of 6,229 personnel, with 
5,052 in position, leaving 1,177 vacancies. This results in a vacancy rate of 18.9%, 
which, although lower compared to other DAE institutions, still reflects a shortage of 
medical professionals and researchers who are essential for providing comprehensive 
cancer care and advancing oncology research. 
8.5 The DAE informed the Committee in its written reply that it has initiated a 
recruitment drive to mitigate workforce shortages. A Central Recruitment Agency 
(CRA) has been set up in the year 2022 to streamline recruitment across all DAE units. 
The Department further informed that a mission-mode recruitment drive in 2023-24 led 
to the hiring of 3,200 personnel, including scientific officers, technical staff, and 
administrative personnel. Trainee Scientific Officers (TSOs) are recruited annually for 
BARC Training Schools, and 180 officers were inducted in August 2024. The 
Department emphasized that Recruitment of Technical Officers, Scientific Assistants, 
and Paramedical Staff is ongoing, with empanelment of 2,269 technicians and 1,206 
scientific assistants. However, the Committee observes that recruitment delays still 
persist, and several advertised posts remain unfilled. 
8.6 The high vacancy rates across DAE institutions raise serious concerns 
about workforce planning, recruitment efficiency, and the overall sustainability of 
nuclear and research projects in India. The Committee expresses its concerns over 
these vacancies, especially in key operational areas such as nuclear power 
generation, research, and mineral exploration. The shortage of skilled personnel 
could lead to delays in research and development, inefficiencies in plant 
operations, and constraints in nuclear fuel production, ultimately affecting the 

32 
 
country's nuclear energy programme. To address this issue, the Committee 
strongly recommends an immediate recruitment drive to fill the existing vacancies. 
It is imperative that recruitment processes be streamlined to ensure timely hiring 
of personnel in technical, scientific, and operational roles. A review of existing 
workforce policies should be undertaken to identify areas where delays occur in 
the hiring process, and necessary reforms should be implemented. The DAE must 
adopt modern human resource management strategies to attract and retain talent, 
ensuring competitive salaries, career progression opportunities, and research 
grants for professionals in nuclear science, energy, and advanced research. 
8.7 The Committee observes with great concern contractual employment and 
temporary staffing, particularly in research institutions such as TIFR. The 
Committee urges a review of these employment practices and recommends 
converting long-term contractual positions into permanent roles wherever 
necessary. The Committee recommends that regularisation of employment will not 
only enhance job security for researchers and scientists but also ensure continuity 
in long-term research projects. Additionally, for institutions such as TMC, where 
medical professionals are in high demand, the Committee recommends expanding 
recruitment efforts and increasing budgetary support for healthcare staff and 
researchers.
