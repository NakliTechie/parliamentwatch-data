INTRODUCTION 
 
I, the Chairman of the Department-related Parliamentary Standing Committee on Education, 
Women, Children, Youth and Sports, having been authorised by the Committee to present the 
Report on its behalf, do hereby present this Three Hundred and Seventy-Seventh Report of the 
Committee on the Demands for Grants (Demand No. 101) of the Ministry of Women and Child 
Development for the year 2026–27. 
2. In accordance with the constitutional requirement under Article 113, the estimated 
expenditure of the Ministries/Departments of the Government of India projected under various 
Demands for Grants for the ensuing financial year is required to be voted by Parliament. 
Consequently, the Demands for Grants of the relevant Ministries/Departments stand referred to the 
concerned Department-related Parliamentary Standing Committee for detailed scrutiny under Rule 
270 of the Rules of Procedure and Conduct of Business in the Council of States (Rajya Sabha). 
While scrutinising the Demands for Grants, the Committee has made an appraisal of the 
performance, programmes, schemes and policies of the Ministry of Women and Child Development 
vis-à-vis the expenditure incurred out of the Consolidated Fund of India during the current financial 
year. 
 
3. In this Report, a scrutiny of the Budget allocations of the Ministry of Women and Child 
Development as sought under Demand No. 101 for the Financial Year 2026-27 has been made by 
the Committee. 
 
4. During the course of examination of the Demands, the Committee heard the views of the 
Secretary, Ministry of Women and Child Development, and other senior officers of the Ministry. 
Inputs from organisations/agencies, namely, the National Institute of Public Cooperation and Child 
Development (NIPCCD), Central Adoption Resource Authority (CARA), National Commission for 
Protection of Child Rights (NCPCR), and National Commission for Women (NCW), were also 
made available to the Committee at its sitting held on 19thFebruary, 2026. 
 
5. The Committee, while making its observations/recommendations, has mainly relied upon 
the following:- 
(i) Detailed Demands for Grants of the Ministry of Women and child Development for the year 
2026-27; 
(ii) Expenditure Budget 2026-27 and Output Outcome Framework 2026-27; 
(iii) Annual Report 2025-26; 
(iv) Presentations made by the Secretary, Ministry of Women and Child Development and 
inputs provided by Senior Officers from National Institute of Public Cooperation and Child 
Development (NIPCCD), Central Adoption Resource Agency (CARA), National Commission for 
Protection of Child Rights (NCPCR) and National Commission for Women (NCW); 
(v) Written replies furnished by the Ministry of Women and Child Development to the 
Questionnaires (Issues and Schemes & Budgetary Allocations) prepared by the committee; and to 
the points/issues raised by Members during the meeting of the Committee.

REPORT 
 
1.1  Overview of the Ministry  
Women and Children together constitute about 67.7% of the country's population as per the 
2011Census. Empowerment, protection and holistic development of women are central to the 
nation’s development, while the children are the future of the country. A major part of India’s 
population, around 158 million comprises children in the age group of 0-6 years. Ensuring their 
well being is a fundamental prerequisite for equitable development of a society. The Ministry of 
Women and Child Development (MWCD) is the apex body of the Government of India for 
formulation and administration of regulations and laws related to the welfare and development of 
women and children in the country. The Ministry came into existence as a separate Ministry with 
effect from 30th January 2006. It functioned as a Department under the Ministry of Human 
Resource Development since 1985. The Ministry was constituted with the prime objective of 
addressing the gaps in State action for women and children and for promoting inter-ministerial and 
inter-sectoral convergence to create gender equitable and child-centered legislations, policies, and 
programmes. 
 
1.2 Vision  
Empowered women living with dignity and contributing,  as equal partners in development 
in an environment free from violence and discrimination; and well-nurtured children with full 
opportunities for growth and development in a safe and protective environment. 
 
1.3 Mission  
1.3.1 Empowerment of Women  
Promoting social and economic empowerment of women through cross-cutting policies and 
programmes, mainstreaming gender concerns, creating awareness about their rights and facilitating 
institutional and legislative support for enabling them to realize their human rights and develop to 
their full potential. 
 
1.3.2 Safe and Secure Childhood  
Ensuring development, care and protection of children through cross-cutting policies and 
programmes, spreading awareness about their rights and facilitating access to learning, nutrition, 
institutional and legislative support for enabling them to grow and develop to their full potential. 
 
1.4 A brief note on the various Mission/ Schemes of the Ministry:   
To ensure better monitoring and efficient implementation, in the XVth Finance Commission 
Cycle, all the schemes being implemented by the Ministry of Women and Child Development have 
been clubbed into three verticals. All three missions are Centrally Sponsored Schemes (CSS) 
implemented in partnership with State Governments and Union Territory Administrations. States 
and UTs are the primary implementing partners and the Central Government releases grants-in-aid 
to them on a cost-sharing basis. 
 
1.5 Mission Saksham Anganwadi and POSHAN 2.0:  
The Government of India approved Mission Saksham Anganwadi and Poshan 2.0 which is a 
strategic shift in Mission mode to develop practices that nurture health, wellness and immunity 
from malnutrition. The Scheme has been rolled-out across 36 States and UTs covering all the 
districts. During the 15th Finance Commission (FC) period, various components such as nutritional 
support for children from 6 months to the age of 6 years, pregnant women and lactating mothers 
and Adolescent Girls; Early Childhood Care and Education of children (3-6 years) and Anganwadi 
infrastructure including modern, upgraded Saksham Anganwadi have been subsumed under an 

2 
 
umbrella Mission Saksham Anganwadi and Poshan 2.0 (Mission Poshan 2.0). Package of the 
following six services is provided under the Mission Poshan 2.0 scheme through a network of 
Anganwadi centres across the country: 
(i) Supplementary Nutrition Programme (SNP) 
(ii) Pre-school Non-formal Education  
(iii) Nutrition & Health Education 
(iv) Immunization 
(v) Health Check-up  
(vi) Referral Services  
 
Three of the six services, viz., Immunization, Health Check-up and Referral Services are 
related to health and are provided through National Health Mission (NHM) & Public Health 
Infrastructure. 
 
 Poshan Abhiyan: Poshan Abhiyan was launched in March 2018 to achieve improvement in 
nutritional status of Children from 0-6 years, Adolescent Girls, Pregnant Women and Lactating 
Mothers, in a time bound manner and to achieve reduction in stunting, wasting  and underweight in 
children (0-6 years) as well as reduction in anemia in women, children and adolescent girls. In 
addition to addressing the challenge of malnutrition, the achievement of the Sustainable 
Development Goals (SDGs) lies at the fore-front of this programme design. Poshan 2.0 shall 
contribute to the SDGs, specifically, SDG 2 on Zero Hunger and SDG 4 on Quality Education.  
 
1.6 Mission Shakti: The erstwhile scheme of ‘Mission for Protection and Empowerment of 
Women’ has been revamped and brought under the Umbrella Scheme of Mission Shakti from the 
year 2021-22. Mission Shakti aims at strengthening interventions for women safety, security and 
empowerment. Mission Shakti comprises of two verticals ‘Sambal’ and ‘Samarthya’. It seeks to 
realise the Government’s commitment for ‘women-led development’ by addressing issues affecting 
women on a life-cycle continuum basis.   
 
1.6.1 SAMBAL: SAMBAL aims at safety and security of women and its sub-schemes includes 
components viz. Beti Bachao Beti Padhao, One Stop Centre, Nari Adalat and Women Helpline.  
 
1.6.2 SAMARTHYA: SAMARTHYA aims at empowerment of women and its sub-schemes 
include various components viz Shakti Sadan, Shakhi Niwas, Palna, Pradhan Mantri Matru 
VandanaYojana, National Hub for Women Empowerment, Gender Budgeting, Research/ Skilling/ 
Training/Media Advocacy etc. 
 
1.7  Mission Vatsalya: The scheme is being implemented with a view to support Children in 
Need of care and Protection (CNCP) and Children in Conflict with Law (CCL). The objective of 
Mission Vatsalya is to ensure that the best interests of children are always taken care of while 
designing the projects/ programmes. The objectives include establishing essential services, 
emergency outreach services for children and include strengthening of institutional and non-
institutional care services. The Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015 
provisions and the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act, 2012 form the basic 
framework for implementation of the Mission.

1.9 Savitribai Phule National Institute of Women and Child Development (SPNIWCD), 
erstwhile NIPCCD (renamed via Gazette Notification dated 30thJune, 2025): 
SPNIWCD is an Institute of global repute in women empowerment, child rights, child 
protection and child development. It acts as a think tank, catalyst, and facilitator of women 
empowerment, child rights, child protection, and child development programs by pursuing capacity 
building of functionaries, research and evaluation, networking, consultancy, and advisory services, 
as well as provision of specialized services through interdisciplinary teams. 
 
Statutory Bodies: 
1.10 Central Adoption Resource Agency (CARA):  
CARA is the nodal authority for regulating and promoting in-country and inter-country 
adoption of children. The Authority operates under the legal framework of the Juvenile Justice 
(Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015 (as amended in 2021), the JJ Model Rules, 2016 (as 
amended in 2022), and the Adoption Regulations, 2022. It also serves as India’s designated Central 
Authority under the Hague Convention on Inter-country Adoption, 1993, ratified by India in 2003. 
 
1.11 National Commission for Protection of Child Rights (NCPCR):  
The National Commission for Protection of Child Rights (NCPCR) is a statutory body 
established in March 2007 under the Commissions for Protection of Child Rights (CPCR) Act, 
2005. Its vision and mission are centered on ensuring all laws, policies, and programs conform to a 
child-rights perspective as outlined in the Indian Constitution and the UN Convention on the Rights 
of the Child (UNCRC).  
 
1.12 National Commission for Women (NCW)  
The National Commission for Women (NCW) is a statutory body established in January 
1992 under the National Commission for Women Act, 1990. It functions as the apex national 
institution for protecting and promoting the rights and interests of women in India, operating under 
the aegis of the Ministry of Women and Child Development (MoWCD). NCW plays a critical role 
in strengthening the legislative framework through periodic reviews of laws and policies affecting 
women. These reviews are undertaken to assess the effectiveness of existing enactments, identify 
gaps in implementation, and recommend measures to ensure that the objectives of the legislations 
are fully realised. 
 
1.13 Rationalisation of Government Bodies 
The Government closed down the Central Social Welfare Board (CSWB), Food and 
Nutrition Board (FNB) and Rashtriya Mahila Kosh (RMK) as per recommendations in the Report 
on 'Rationalization of Government Bodies' prepared by the Principal Economic Advisor in 
November 2020. The process of merger and absorption of CSWB staff into SPNIWCD is 
underway.

2.  Budget Allocations: 
2.1 The Committee considered the Demands for Grants (2026-27) of the Ministry of Women 
and Child Development in its sitting held on 19th February, 2026. The Demand for Grants (2026-
27) of the Ministry of Women and Child Development has been presented to Parliament under the 
Demand Number 101. 
 
2.2 The Ministry of Women and Child Development has been allocated Rs. 28,183.06 crores 
(net of recoveries) under Demand No. 101 for the Financial Year 2026-27 as Budget Estimates 
(BE). This represents an increase of Rs. 1,293.37 crores (approximately 4.81%) over the BE of Rs. 
26,889.69 crores for 2025-26. More significantly, it represents an increase of Rs. 3,809.15  crores 
(15.63%) over the Revised Estimates (RE) of Rs. 24,373.91  crores for 2025-26, indicating the 
Government's intent to significantly ramp up spending in the welfare sector. 
 
2.3 The Gross allocation for 2026-27 is Rs. 28,638.06 crores (Revenue: Rs. 28,632.42 crores; 
Capital: Rs. 5.64 crores). The Net allocation after accounting for recoveries of Rs. 455 crores stands 
at Rs. 28,183.06  crores which is 0.53% (approx.) of total budgetary allocation of the Government 
of India for the FY 2025-2026.  
 
2.4 The Committee notes that Rs 26889.69 Crore was the total budgetary allocation in BE 
2025-26 which was reduced to Rs 24373.91 Crores at RE stage. The Ministry has informed in 
response that the allocation of Rs 26889.69 crores was reduced to Rs 24373.91 crores at RE stage 
in FY 2025-2026 as per Cabinet Approval for the financial outlay for the schemes for 15th Finance 
Commission Cycle and also due to availability of unspent balance with States/UTs under various 
schemes of the Ministry.

MISSION SAKSHAM ANGANWADI AND POSHAN 2.0 
3.   Mission Saksham Anganwadi and Poshan 2.0 is the flagship nutrition programme of the 
Government and the largest centrally sponsored scheme of the Ministry. The Scheme was 
announced in the Budget 2021-2022 as an integrated nutrition support programme. The mission 
deals with the nutritional and health needs of 0-6 year age group children, adolescent girls, pregnant 
women and lactating mothers in conjunction with the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare. Apart 
from this Early Childhood care and Education is an important vertical. For implementing all these 
schemes, Anganwadis are the focal point, run by Anganwadi Workers and Anganwadi Helplers. 
The programme is being operated in 36 States and UTs, 7075 projects have been sanctioned, 
14,02,983 Anganwadis are operating across the country and there are 8.9 crores beneficiaries on the 
POSHAN Tracker. The Mission is the only programme in the social sector in which all the services 
have been brought end-to-end on a digital platform. The biggest advantage of the POSHAN Tracker 
is that it can be operated on offline mode, except for registration purposes.  
 
3.1 The primary objectives of Mission Poshan 2.0 are:  
(i) To contribute to human capital development of the country;  
(ii)  Address challenges of malnutrition;  
(iii)  Promote nutrition awareness and good eating habits for sustainable health and wellbeing; 
and  
(iv)  Address nutrition-related deficiencies through key strategies. 
3.2 Beneficiaries and Service Delivery 
As on 31st December 2025, more than 8.86 crores beneficiaries are registered under Mission 
Poshan 2.0, including children up to 6 years of age, pregnant women, lactating mothers, and 
adolescent girls (14-18 years) in Aspirational Districts and North-Eastern States.  
Six services are provided through Anganwadi Centres (AWCs) as under:

(ii) Pre-school Non-formal Education;  
(iii) Nutrition and Health Education;  
(iv) Immunization (through NHM);

3.17 Anganwadi Workforce: Anganwadi Workers (AWWs) and Anganwadi Helpers (AWHs) 
are 'honorary workers' from the local community, who render their services on a voluntary basis in 
the area of child care and development and are paid a monthly honorarium. They are neither 
recruited nor appointed in the programme through any competitive process of recruitment. 
Currently an Anganwadi Worker is paid an honorarium @ Rs 4500 per month and an Anganwadi 
Helper is paid @ Rs 2250 per month on cost sharing basis between the Centre and State/UT along 
with performance linked incentive of Rs 250 per month for AWHs and Rs 500 to AWWs. 
States/UTs are also paying additional monetary incentives/honorarium to these functionaries from 
their own resources which vary from State to State. The details are as under: 
(a) Minimum prescribed qualification for AWW and AWH: 12th standard pass; Age: 18-35 years 
(b) Standard working hours of an Anganwadi centre: 6 hours per day 
(c) 50% posts of Supervisors shall be filled by promotion of AWWs with 5 years of experience 
(d) 50% posts of AWWs shall be filled by promotion of AWHs 
(e) Anganwadi Helpers are not entitled to mobile phones as part of their job 
(f) Monthly payment to AWW and AWH is NOT linked to data input in Poshan Tracker 
 
3.18 The Committee notes that the common grievance of AWWs and AWHs is 
enhancement of honorarium and giving permanent status to their appointments. The 
Committee is of the view that payment of fair wages is the rightful entitlement of the workers 
and the revision is pending for a long time and the honorarium paid to the AWWs and AWHs 
is very meager. Keeping in view the expanded scope of work of the Anganwadis, the 
Committee recommends that the honorarium of AWWs and AWHs should be increased at 
the earliest along with the amount of incentives. The Committee also recommends that the 
States that already top-up payments may be allowed to receive matching central incentives. 
 
3.19  Child Development Project Officers (CDPOs):  
Anganwadi Centres function under the Mission Saksham Anganwadi framework of the 
Ministry with a well defined governance structure. While the Ministry of Women & Child 
Development frames policies, lays down funding norms and issues guidelines at the central level, 
State Women & Child Development Departments are responsible for implementation, supervision 
and monitoring at the State level. At the district and project levels, District Programme Officers and 
Child Development Project Officers (CDPOs) oversee day-to-day functioning. AWWs and AWHs 
deliver services at the village/ward level with support from Supervisors. The CDPOs posts are 
sanctioned with respect to the number of projects sanctioned at the national level. A total of 7075 
projects were sanctioned at the time of inception of the programme i.e. 2008. Accordingly, there are 
7075 posts of CDPOs. The ratio of CDPOs to Anganwadi Workers is approximately 1:198, though 
there is an intervening supervisory layer between the CDPO and AWW. 
 

11 
 
3.20 The CDPO is an important functionary in the Saksham Anganwadi Scheme. As the 
CDPO works directly with the AWCs, he is an important link between the State authorities 
and the AWCs. A CDPO is also well aware of the ground realities and can assess the 
effectiveness of the Scheme. The Committee notes that the sanctioned strength of Child 
Development Project Officers (CDPOs) has not been revised since 2008-09, a period of 17 
years, when 7,075 projects were sanctioned. As per Annual Programme Implementation Plan 
(APIP) meetings held in February-March 2025, 2,348 out of 7,075 sanctioned CDPO posts 
(33.18%) are lying vacant across the country which impairs programme oversight at the field 
level. The Committee, therefore, recommends that the Government should undertake a 
review of sanctioned strength of CDPO expeditiously in light of current programme 
requirements and fill the vacancies.

3.24 Anganwadi cum Creche (Palna) Scheme: The objective of Palna Scheme is to provide 
quality crèche facility in safe and secure environment for children (from ages 6 months – 6 years), 
nutritional support, health and cognitive development of children, growth monitoring & 
immunization. 
 3.25 With rising employment opportunities a large number of women are engaged in gainful 
employment, working within or outside their homes. Lack of proper day-care services is, often, a 
deterrent for women to go out and work. Hence, there is an urgent need for day care 
services/crèches both in the organized and unorganized sectors. To address these difficulties faced 
by the working mothers, day-care/crèche facilities are being provided through the Palna Scheme. 
Crèche services formalise the child care responsibilities hitherto considered as part of domestic 
work. Availability of institutional day care services is vital to achieve the Sustainable Development 
Goal 8 – Decent work and economic growth. This will also enable more mothers, to take up gainful 
employment. In a first of its kind approach, Ministry has extended the services of childcare through 
Anganwadi cum Crèche (AWCC).    
                                                                                                                                                                                         
3.26 Anganwadi centres are the world's largest childcare programme dedicated to providing 
essential care and support to children ensuring delivery of care facilities till the last mile. AWCs 
ensure whole day childcare support ensuring their well-being in a safe and secure environment. 
Anganwadi cum Crèche initiative aims to increase ‘women work force participation’ in the 
economy.

4.5 Components of Sambal: One Stop Centres (OSCs): 
One Stop Centres (OSCs) is a centrally sponsored scheme and funded from Nirbhya Fund, 
w.e.f. 01st April, 20215, for supporting women affected by violence and those in distress. The 

17 
 
scheme provides integrated support to the affected women both in public as well in private space, 
under one roof. The total number of approved OSCs - 1025, number of operational OSCs - 896 and 
the number of approved OSCs coverage - 778 districts. A total of 12,88,260 women have been 
provided assistance till 31/10/2025. OSCs provide integrated services like medical, legal, 
psychological, and temporary shelter to women affected by violence. The OSCs are integrated with 
Women Helpline (WHL) 181. About one crore women have been benefitted through the Helpline. 
Each OSC operates with 13 functionaries: Centre Administrator, Psycho-social Counsellor, Case 
Worker, Para-medical personnel, and Paralegal Worker. 
 
4.6 An amount of Rs. 240  crores in RE 2025-26 and Rs. 405  crores in BE 2026-27 have been 
allocated for OSCs and Women Helpline component supported from Nirbhaya Fund. An amount of 
Rs.1213.30 Cr. has been released under One Stop Center (OSC) as on 28.01.2026 to States/UTs 
since inception.  
 
4.7 The Committee observes that One Stop Centres (OSCs) have become de facto Short-
Stay Homes rather than comprehensive one-stop facilities. The original vision that a victim 
need not narrate her trauma to 50 different people is not being realized. The Committee, 
therefore, recommends that OSCs should have police support and legal aid within the Centre, 
rather than requiring victims to visit police stations separately. The Ministry of Women and 
Child Development should get an assessment done through a third-party, of the impact and 
the outcomes of the Scheme.  Further, the Committee is of the view that the Ministry should 
use the Ministry of Rural Development reports on DISHA meetings and conduct a proper 
study through Cluster Random Sampling, so that the qualitative dimensions of the actual 
progress of the scheme is assessed. 
The committee recommends a capacity-building programme for the staff of the One 
Stop Centres. The committee noted that the number of people working in a small district in 
the North East and in a large district in Uttar Pradesh is the same. The committee 
recommends that staff should be allocated to districts according to the average number of 
cases they are handling.   
 
4.8 The Committee notes that out of 1,025 OSCs, 896 are presently operational. The 
Committee recommends that operationalisation of the OSCs should be expedited in the best 
interest of the women undergoing trauma and in need of immediate care. A comprehensive 
rehabilitation plan should be chalked out for the women after they leave the OSCs and some 
kind of monitoring mechanism should also be developed so that the women could be able to 
lead a normal life.  
 
4.9 The Committee recommends the Ministry the following:- 
 Increase the number of OSCs in high-population districts of each State. 
 Provide urban-specific OSC funding norms, since violence reporting and case 
load is higher in metropolitan areas. 
 Integrate OSC services with State women police stations and social welfare 
hostels. 
 
4.10 The Committee observes that in cases of centre-state funding pattern of a scheme, 
some States are sometimes unable to pay their share of contribution. Instead of putting the 
States in disadvantage position the Committee, therefore, recommends that the Ministry 
should review the existing funding pattern and accordingly modify the same in order to 
streamline the implementation of the scheme.  
 

18 
 
4.11 The Committee recommends that the Centre must simplify fund flow norms (Single 
Nodal Agency mechanism) and provide state-specific flexibility, where implementation 
capacity exists. 
 
4.12 The Committee notes that migrant women are particularly at risk of sexual violence 
and harassment. Given their physical presence and trained personnel, the Committee 
observes that OSCs present a strategic opportunity to address the needs of women migrating 
independently for work who lack access to safe, short-term accommodation and counselling 
in their destination cities. In this context, the Committee recommends that the Ministry 
consider introducing a structured Migration Support Function within OSCs to provide short-
duration transitional accommodation (10-15 days) for women migrating for verified 
employment, skilling, apprenticeships, or interviews. Alongside accommodation, OSCs could 
provide city orientation, information on safe hostels and rental options, linkages to safety 
helplines, and referrals to local employment and skilling institutions. Strengthening OSCs in 
this manner would shift them from a purely crisis-response institution to a preventive and 
enabling platform directly supporting safe migration and sustained workforce participation 
among first-generation women workers. 
4.13 Women Helpline (WHL 181): WHL 181 is a component of the Sambal Scheme under the 
umbrella mission Shakti. Women Helpline 181 was launched on 1st April 2015 providing 24 hours 
toll free telecom responses to women in distress. It is operational in 35 States/UTs (except West 
Bengal) and is integrated with ERSS-112. WHL 181 is integrated with all 655 functional OSCs. 
CDAC is the technology service provider for the integration. Each WHL is provisioned with 20-26 
staff: a Helpline Administrator, Call Operators (12-18), IT Supervisor, Multi-purpose Staff, and 
Security Guard. 
 
4.14 Regarding the effectiveness of WHL 181, the Committee observes that the Helpline has 
become a mere call centre rather than a ground-support response mechanism. The 
Committee recommends that the Helpline should be linked with ground support teams, with 
on-site rescue capabilities, transport and counsellors. Without any ground support, rescue of 
distressed women cannot be carried out effectively.  
 
4.15 Beti Bachao Beti Padhao (BBBP): Beti Bachao Beti Padhao (BBBP) Scheme was 
launched on 22nd January 2015 to help address declining Child Sex Ratio (CSR) and related issues 
of empowerment of girls and women. The scheme focuses on creating mindset and behavioral 
changes towards the girl child by informing, influencing, motivating, engaging, and empowering all 
stakeholders. Beti Bachao Beti Padhao (BBBP) has transformed from policy initiation into national 
movement by mobilizing various stake holders including government agencies, media, civil society 
and general public. 
 
4.16 The scheme aims to work towards the protection and education of girl child through multi-
sectoral interventions and encouraging greater spend on activities that have ground impact, e.g., 
promotion of sports among girls, awareness about Pre-Conception and Pre-Natal Diagnostic 
Techniques (Prohibition of Sex Selection) Act (PC-PNDT Act), and skilling of girls etc. 
 
4.17 An amount of Rs. 1010.86 Cr. has been released under Beti Bachao Beti Padhao as on 
10.02.2026 to States/UTs since inception.  
 
4.18 The Committee notes that the current focus of the Beti Bachao Beti Padhao scheme is 
on avoiding female infanticide and supporting female education. The Committee recommends 
that the Ministry consider expanding the scope of the campaign towards encouraging women 
in the workforce as well. The Committee notes that the existing institutional and 

19 
 
communication architecture can be leveraged to anchor a new campaign that extends the 
narrative from education to employment (jobsa nd entrepreneurship). The Committee 
further recommends the following:- 
  
i. A National Role Model Programme highlighting women working in non-
traditional roles, such as shop floor technicians in EV manufacturing, construction 
supervisors, logistics operators, to reshape societal perceptions. 
ii. Introduction of Gender Champion Awards to recognise corporates demonstrating 
substantial representation of women across entry, middle, and leadership levels. 
iii. Sectoral convenings in partnership with Ministry of Labour and with private 
employers to share best practices on recruitment, retention, workplace safety, and 
career progression for women. 
iv. Working with the Ministries of Labour and Corporate Affairs to encourage 
systematic use of gender-disaggregated disclosures by listed companies to identify 
workforce participation gaps and accelerate corrective action. 
4.19 Samarthya Sub-Scheme: Shakti Sadan (Swadhar Greh/Ujjawala/Widow Homes): 
Shakti Sadan is a component of the umbrella scheme Mission Shakti, designed to provide 
immediate care, protection, and rehabilitation to women in distress, particularly, victims of 
trafficking, violence, abandonment, or other forms of exploitation. The scheme ensures that the 
affected women are provided a safe and supportive environment where they can recover physically, 
emotionally, and socially while being assisted in rebuilding their lives. 
 
4.20 Under Shakti Sadan, institutional support is provided through dedicated shelter homes offer 
essential services to distressed women. These services include safe accommodation, nutritious food, 
clothing, and basic amenities. In addition, the scheme offers psychological counseling to help 
women overcome trauma, as well as provide legal assistance. Medical care is also provided to 
address immediate health needs and support long-term recovery. 
 
4.21 The Scheme focuses on rehabilitation and reintegration of rescued women. For this purpose, 
a financial provision of Rs. 2,50,000 has been earmarked towards rescue, repatriation, reintegration, 
and reward. These provisions facilitate coordinated efforts by authorities and support agencies to 
ensure that victims of trafficking are safely rescued and provided with appropriate support for 
recovery and reintegration into the society.

January, 2026) has been released to the States/UTs.

4.24 Sakhi Niwas (Working Women Hostel): Sakhi Niwas is a Government initiative aimed at 
providing safe, secure, and affordable hostel accommodation for working women, particularly those 
who migrate to cities for employment and lack family support or suitable housing facilities. The 
scheme enables women to pursue employment opportunities by providing them dignified and 
reasonably priced residential facilities in a safe environment. The Eligibility under the scheme is 
determined on the basis of income criteria. Women whose gross monthly income does not exceed 
Rs. 50,000 in metropolitan cities and Rs.35,000 in other areas are eligible to avail accommodation 
under Sakhi Niwas. This income-based eligibility ensures that the facility primarily benefits women 
from lower and middle income groups who may otherwise struggle to secure safe housing. 
 
4.25 At present, the scheme focuses mainly on accommodation support, and there is no provision 
for health insurance coverage for workers residing in Sakhi Niwas hostels.

4.28 Pradhan Mantri Matru Vandana Yojana (PMMVY): The Pradhan Mantri Matru 
Vandana Yojana (PMMVY) was implemented across the country w.e.f. 01.01.2017. The PMMVY 
is a Centrally Sponsored Maternity Benefit Scheme under which cash incentives of ₹5,000/- is 
provided directly to the Bank/Post Office account of the beneficiary in Direct Benefit Transfer 
(DBT) mode for the first child. The eligible beneficiaries receive the remaining cash incentive, as 
per approved norms towards maternity benefit under Janani Suraksha Yojana after institutional 
delivery, so that on an average a woman gets ₹6,000/-. Cash incentive of ₹6,000/- is also provided 
under PMMVY to eligible beneficiaries for second child subject to the second child being a girl.  

21 
 
4.29 With the introduction of umbrella Mission Shakti scheme from 1st April, 2022, the number 
of instalments under PMMVY has been reduced from three (3) to two (2). Further, maternity 
benefit under PMMVY is also extended for the second child subject to second child being a girl. As 
a result of revision of scheme, a new portal, Pradhan Mantri Matru Vandana Yojana Software 
(PMMVY Soft) was developed and launched in March, 2023. Under PMMVY Soft, Aadhaar 
authentication through UIDAI is done digitally and National Payment Corporations of India (NPCI) 
verification is ensured so that funds are directly transferred to their DBT-enabled Aadhaar-seeded 
bank or post office accounts.  
4.30 The Scheme is implemented by officials of States and UTs using the new Software 
mentioned above, through their field functionaries. The release of Central funds to States and UTs 
is done in a timely manner provided they utilise the funds available in the State Nodal Agency 
(SNA) Account in time. States/UTs are regularly requested to clear the payments of beneficiaries 
under PMMVY Scheme.  
 
4.31 Further, to facilitate the beneficiaries for tracking the status of their applications and 
payments, a ‘Track and Search’ feature is available on the PMMVY portal to show real time 
location and status of Application. Also, a Grievance redressal module has been implemented to 
lodge and track any PMMVY related grievances by any beneficiary.  
4.32 The Ministry conducts various awareness programs including those on Maternity Benefits 
through SANKALP-Hub for Empowerment of Women (HEW) scheme for all States/UTs. It 
includes various Information, Education and Communication (IEC) and Behavior Change 
Communication (BCC) activities such as Prabhat Pheri, Nukkad Natak, Newspaper 
Advertisements, Airing Radio Jingles, Selfie Campaign, Door to Door Campaign, and Community 
Programmes which are conducted at field functionary levels. Further, the Ministry is also running 
special campaign for registration of eligible beneficiaries in all the States and UTs under PMMVY 
on a periodic basis. 
4.33 Under PMMVY 2.0 (from 01.04.2023 onwards), maternity benefit of not less than rupees 
five thousand, in two instalments is provided to women aged between the group of 18 years and 7 
month – 55 years, belonging to socially and economically disadvantaged sections of society. 
However, any additional incentive available under any other scheme such as Janani Suraksha 
Yojana (JSY), shall continue to be availed. The eligible beneficiaries would receive the remaining 
cash incentives as per approved norms towards maternity benefit under Janani Suraksha Yojana 
(JSY) after institutional delivery so on an average a woman will get ₹ 6000.

return-to-work counselling. This would be especially useful for women in the informal sector 
who often exit work permanently after childbirth. There is also a need to focus on urban 
childcare infrastructure. The women’s health system has largely approached women through 
a narrow, reproductive lens. Reproductive, Maternal, Newborn, Child and Adolescent Health 
(RMNCH+A) programmes have delivered real gains; however, this focus narrows women’s

4.39 SANKALP: Hub for Empowerment of Women (HEW): The SANKALP: HEW (Hub for 
Empowerment of Women), has been introduced on 1st April 2022, under the umbrella Mission 
Shakti, which functions as a PMU for all components of Mission Shakti and serves as a single 
window inter-sectoral convergence mechanism provided to all women to avail the benefits of 
Central and State Government schemes. 
 
4.40 The objectives of SANKALP: HEW is to enable women to reach their full potential through 
assisting, directing, and connecting them to a variety of resources for empowerment and 
development. Further, it aims to facilitate inter-sectoral convergence of schemes and programs 
meant for women both at the national, state, and district levels with the mandate to create an 
environment in which women realize their full potential. 
 
4.41 SANKALP: HEW is being implemented in all States/UTs except the State of West Bengal. 
State SANKALP: HEWs are functional in 35 States/UTs and District SANKALP: HEWs are 
functional in 742 districts.  
 
4.42 A dashboard was launched on 22nd January 2024 to record the real-time tracking of scheme 
utilization and to monitor various parameters of the scheme including the number of women 
assisted, feedback and impact assessment. And as on 31st December 2025, more than 2.8 Lakhs 
women have been assisted through SANKALP: HEW since the launch of the Dashboard in January 
2024.  
 
4.43 The Committee notes that the District Hub for Empowerment of Women could be 
leveraged to support district-level convergence plans that map where women are dropping 
out of work because of childcare, mobility, housing, safety, or information failures, and then 
align existing schemes accordingly. 
 
4.44 POSH Act -Implementation and Sexual Harassment electronic Box (SHe-Box): Sexual 
Harassment electronic Box (SHe-Box) was launched by the Ministry of Women and Child 
Development on 29.08.2024, SHe-Box Portal (https://shebox.wcd.gov.in/) is a unique initiative of 
the Ministry to provide a publicly available centralised repository of information related to Internal 
Committees (ICs) and Local Committees (LCs) formed across the country, whether in government 
or private sector. 
 
4.45 As the Nodal Ministry for the Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, 
Prohibition and Redressal) Act, 2013, Ministry plays a pivotal role in effective implementation of 
the Act. To strengthen workplace safety, enhance compliance with the SH Act and advance a 
unified national vision for gender justice and digital governance, She-Box Portal provides a single 
window access to every woman, irrespective of her work status, whether working in organised or 
un-organised, private or public sector, to facilitate the registration of complaint related to sexual 
harassment. Any woman facing sexual harassment at workplace can register their complaint 
through this portal. Once a complaint is submitted to the ‘SHe-Box’, it will be directly sent to the 
concerned authority having jurisdiction to take action into the matter. 
As per the data available on SHe-Box portal, as on 21.01.2026, so far, over 1,37,000 
workplaces have been onboarded and over 54,000 ICs have been updated. Also, all 777 districts 
have uploaded their Local Committee details on the portal.

scheme, is the administrative ministry of the Scheme. The Ministry of Women and Child 
Development is the appraising Ministry. It appraises the proposals received from the Ministries, 
Departments and State Governments and sends them back to the concerned Ministry who spend the 
money out of the budget allocated to them. An amount of Rs 8212.85 crores has been provided 
since its inception and as on date, an amount of Rs 6581.84 crores has been released under the

appraised by Empowered Committee (EC) under Framework for Nirbhaya Fund has a staggered 
implementation schedule. Further, some of the appraised projects are directly implemented by 
Central Ministries/Departments, however, majority of the projects are implemented through State 
Governments/Union Territory Administrations in which Central Government releases the funds to 

25 
 
States/UTs as per prescribed fund sharing pattern of respective projects/schemes and 
implementation on ground is done by the States/UTs as per the approved implementation period. 
Further, there are schemes, which require recurring expenditure for providing services, in respect of 
which funds are released on receipt of Utilisation Certificates from the Implementing Agency. 
According to the Ministry, it is possible that more funds have been actually utilised, but Utilisation 
Certificates and Statement of Expenditures have not been submitted by them.

5.1 MISSION VATSALYA: The Mission Vatsalya Scheme is a Centrally Sponsored Scheme 
implemented by the States and Union Territories (UTs) with the aim to support Children in Need of 
care and Protection (CNCP) and Children in Conflict with Law (CCL). The objective of Mission 
Vatsalya is to ensure that the best interests of children are always taken care of while designing the 
projects/ programmes. The objectives include establishing essential services, emergency outreach 
services for children and include strengthening of institutional and non-institutional care services. 
The Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015 provisions and the Protection of 
Children from Sexual Offences Act, 2012 form the basic framework for implementation of the 
Mission.  
The scheme aims to: 
i. Support and sustain Children in difficult circumstances. 
ii. Develop context-based solutions for holistic development of children from varied 
backgrounds. 
iii. Provide scope for encouraging innovative solutions. 
 
5.2     Implementation of Mission Vatsalya is being done through the following statutory 
bodies/institutions-   
   Statutory Bodies:  
  (A) State Level 
 (i)  State Child Protection Society (SCPS) – State Government constitutes Child Protection 
Society for the State to take up matters relating to children with a view to ensure the 
implementation of the Act.  
 (ii)  State Adoption Resource Agency (SARA) - State Adoption Resource Agency (SARA) 
coordinates with Central Adoption Resource Authority (CARA) and District Magistrates, for 
promoting in-country and inter-country Adoptions. 
 
(B) District Level 
(i)   District Child Protection Unit (DCPU) –DCPU functions under the supervision of 
District Magistrate. State Government constitutes Child Protection Unit for every district to take 
up matters relating to children with a view to ensure the implementation of the Act.  
  
(ii)    Child Welfare Committee (CWC) - The Committee is headed by chairperson and four 
other members appointed by State government including one member shall be woman. CWC 
shall function as a Bench and shall have the powers conferred by the Bharatiya Nagarik 
Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 on a Metropolitan Magistrate or, as the case may be, a Judicial 
Magistrate of First Class. Empowered to take decisions with regard to the children in need of 
care and protection, keeping their best interest in mind. They are also mandated to monitor the 
function of the Child Care Institutions (CCIs) (Sections 27-30 of JJ Act). 
(iii) Juvenile Justice Board (JJB) - The Juvenile Justice Board consists of a Metropolitan 
Magistrate or Judicial Magistrate of First Class not being Chief Metropolitan Magistrate or 
Chief Judicial Magistrate. The bench shall have the powers conferred by Bharatiya Nagarik 
Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 on Metropolitan Magistrate or Judicial Magistrate of First Class. 
 Empowered to take decisions regarding the welfare of children in conflict with law (Sections 
04-09 of JJ Act).  
 (iv).   Special Juvenile Police Unit (SJPU) - State Government constitutes the SJPU in every 
police station to deal with children either as victims or perpetrators, in coordination with the 
police voluntary and NGOs. 
 

27 
 
II.         Institutional Care (Child Care Institution, CCIs): 
 A.        Children in Need of Care and Protection (CNCP)  
  
i. Children’s Home - These are homes for placement of children in need of care and 
protection and provide care, treatment, education, training, development and rehabilitation 
to such children.  
ii. Open Shelter - These are community-based facilities for children in need of residential 
support, on a short-term basis with the objective of protecting them from abuse or keeping 
them away from a life on the streets. 
iii. Specialized Adoption Agency - These are the agencies for the rehabilitation of orphan, 
abandoned or surrendered children, through adoption and non-institutional care. 
 
            B.        Children in Conflict with Law (CCL)  
  
i. Observation Homes - These are homes for temporary reception, care and rehabilitation of a 
child in conflict with law during the pendency of inquiry. 
ii. Special Homes - These are homes for rehabilitation of children in conflict with law who are 
found to have committed an offence. 
iii. Place of Safety (PoS) – PoS means any place or institution, not being a police lockup or 
jail, established separately or attached to an observation home or a special home, as the case 
may be, the person in-charge of which is willing to receive and take care of the children 
alleged or found to be in conflict with law, by an order of the Board or the Children’s Court, 
both during inquiry and ongoing rehabilitation after having been found guilty for a period 
and purpose as specified in the order. PoS is to place a person above the age of eighteen 
years or child in conflict with law, who is between the age of sixteen to eighteen years and 
is accused of or convicted for committing a heinous offence. 
 
III. Non-institutional Care: 
i. Foster Care - Children are placed in a family environment which is other than child’s 
biological family, which is selected, qualified, approved and supervised for providing 
care to children.  
ii. Sponsorship - Provides supplementary support, financial or otherwise, to the families to 
meet the medical, educational and developmental needs of the child. 
iii. Adoption: finding families for the children found legally free for adoption. Specialized 
Adoption Agencies (SAA) will facilitate the adoption programme.  
iv. After Care - This is a provision to ensure that any child leaving a child care institution 
on completion of eighteen years of age may be provided with financial support in order 
to facilitate child’s re-integration into the mainstream of the society in the manner as may 
be prescribed.  
The Scheme also provides for Non-Institutional Care of vulnerable children by way of sponsorship, 
Foster Care and Aftercare (up to the age of 21 years). It provides Rs.4000/- in Non-Institutional 
care (Sponsorship/ Foster Care/ Aftercare).

National Commission for Protection of Child Rights (NCPCR) 
6.18  The National Commission for Protection of Child Rights (NCPCR) is a statutory body 
established in March 2007 under the Commissions for Protection of Child Rights (CPCR) Act, 
2005. Its vision and mission are centered on ensuring all laws, policies, and programs conform to 
child-rights perspective as outlined in the Indian Constitution and the UN Convention on the Rights 
of the Child (UNCRC).  
6.19 The Commission envisages a rights-based perspective, which flows into national policies 
and programmes, including defined responses at the state, district and block levels, taking into 
account the specifics and strengths of each region. For the purpose of this, it is intended to make 
deep penetration into the communities and families of and it is expected that the collective 
experience gained in the field will be considered by all the authorities at the higher level. Thus, the 
Commission envisages an indispensable role for the state to ensure children and their well-being, 
strong institution-building processes, respect for local bodies and decentralization at the community 
level and greater social concern in this direction. 
 
Vision: The NCPCR envisions a nation where all children enjoy their basic rights: survival, 
development, protection, and participation, aligning with the Constitution, laws, and the UNCRC. 
This includes a rights-based perspective in national policies, securing children's environments, and 
engaging with communities and families.   
 
Mission: The mission is to protect, promote, and defend child rights nationwide by 
enforcing laws, investigating violations, and monitoring policy implementation. Key mission 
components involve monitoring acts like POCSO, JJ Act, and RTE, investigating complaints, 
advocating for child-centric policies, spreading rights awareness, and addressing the needs of 
vulnerable children.  
 
6.20 The Ministry has informed the Committee regarding major child rights issues that were 
addressed by NCPCR during the last year which are given as under:- 
Child Trafficking: Trafficking of Children for Child labour continues to be a serious issues. The 
NCPCR launched a National Campaign on “Rescue and Rehabilitation” of trafficked children. 
Due to persistent efforts, in collaboration of Law Enforcement Agencies, NCPCR has rescued over 
3000 children so far in year 2025-26.  Among them, over 2000 children have been rehabilitated. 
  
  NCPCR conducted Pan-India Online Trainings for DTFs, held review meetings with 
States/UTs, issued advisories for constitution and activation of DTFs. NCPCR also strengthened 
monitoring and reporting mechanisms and facilitated multi-stakeholder coordination.  
 
1. Mental Health Issue: There is a growing concern related to mental health issue among the 
children. Easy cyber access, increase competitive pressure, impact of social media 
interaction etc are among the contributory factors to the menace. The NCPCR runs the child 
help lines called SAHARA and SAMVEDNA where such children can avail the facility of 
online counselling.The NCPCR is also planning to start a targeted awareness program for 
teachers, parents, children and the CCIs to combat this issue. 
 
2. Lack of awareness and implementation of Safety and Security of children in schools 
Guidelines: Despite comprehensive guidelines have been issued relating to safety and 
security of children in the schools; the implementation of same is not satisfactory. To 
address the issue, NCPCR has undertaken 11 awareness programmes to make state/district 
level officials aware of the guidelines and device the strategy for effective implementation 

35 
 
these guidelines. Further, about 25 programmes have been planned during the current 
financial year to sensitize the stakeholders at State/District levels across the country. 
3. Substance abuse among children and adolescents: Rising drug and substance abuse 
among children and adolescents is a growing concern. Factors such as peer pressure, family 
dysfunction, academic stress, and lack of mental health support contribute to addiction, 
affecting children’s health, education, and future prospects. To combat the menace of Drugs 
and Substance use amongst children and its illicit trafficking; NCPCR in collaboration with 
Narcotics Control Bureau (NCB) formulated a “Joint Action Plan for Elimination of Drugs 
and Substance Use amongst Children and its illicit Trafficking” (JAP) popularly known as 
“एक युɮध नशे के ͪवǽɮध”. The JAP focuses on prevention, identification, intervention, and 
rehabilitation through clearly defined roles for schools, families, law enforcement agencies, 
health institutions, and child protection systems. It emphasizes school-based awareness 
programmes, life-skills education, peer support mechanisms, and counseling services to 
build resilience among children and prevent early initiation into substance use. The Plan 
also provides for early detection of vulnerable children and their linkage with appropriate 
de-addiction and mental health services, in coordination with District Child Protection 
Units (DCPUs), Child Welfare Committees (CWCs), and healthcare facilities. 
4. Spread of Child Sexual Abuse Material (CSAM): Child Sexual Abuse Material has been 
growing exponentially leading to serious child rights violations. The easy access to internet 
and smart phones has facilitated easier access to Social Media and similar platforms. To 
counter this menace; NCPCR has undertaken an initiative in collaboration with MEITY for 
development of AI based tool to proactively detect the availability of CSAM on various 
OTT platforms. With the help of above tools, NCPCR would envisage to take preventive 
steps to protect children from freely available CSAM. 
5. Childhood Obesity: Childhood obesity has emerged as a major public health concern in 
India, with long-term implications for physical, psychological, and social well-being of the 
children. Unhealthy dietary habits, excessive consumption of sugar-rich and ultra-processed 
foods, sedentary lifestyles, and increased screen time have significantly contributed to 
rising obesity levels among children and adolescents. In order to address this growing 
concern, the National Commission for Protection of Child Rights (NCPCR) has issued

6.27 The Committee notes that NCPCR has 36 sanctioned posts out of which only 2 posts 
has been filled by permanent staff and 34 posts have been left vacant. The Committee notes 
that not a single post in NCPCR is filled by permanent officers. A statutory body of this 
importance, mandated to protect child rights across the country, operating entirely on 
deputation and contractual basis is a matter of concern about institutional continuity, 
accountability and effectiveness. The Committee recommends that the Government should 
initiate the process for regular recruitment to NCPCR posts. 
 
6.28 The NCPCR has received Rs. 28.44 crore in BE 2026-27 which was Rs. 25 crore in BE 
2025-26 that shows an increase of 12.10%. The Committee is of the view that the allocated 
amount of Rs.28.44 crores may not be adequate to carry out the mandated responsibilities. 
The Committee recommends that the budget the allocation should be enhanced at RE stage to 
meet the requirements of additional manpower, regional outreach mechanisms, and a 
dedicated grievance redressal platform for child rights violations. 
 

39 
 
6.29 The Committee observes that a significant number of core positions within the 
NCPCR, including technical roles such as Senior Consultant (Legal) and Consultant (Juvenile 
Justice), are currently being filled on a contractual basis. The Committee is of the view that 
persistent vacancies in senior administrative posts like Registrar and Presenting Officer may 
not be helpful for the Commission to carry out its statutory mandate. The Committee 
therefore, recommends that the Ministry of Women and Child Development and the NCPCR 
should take steps to fill all sanctioned posts with permanent/regular officers rather than 
relying on deputation or contractual arrangements. This is essential to ensure institutional 
memory, accountability, and the effective protection and enforcement of child rights. 
 
6.30 The implementation of child rights depends heavily on the actions of State 
Governments and local authorities. The Committee is concerned by reports that several State 
Commissions (SCPCRs) have persistent vacancies and lack the requisite freedom to decide on 
broad policy matters. The Committee recommends that the NCPCR should institutionalize a 
quarterly review mechanism to monitor the functional status of SCPCRs, specifically 
regarding their vacancy levels and budgetary health. The Commission should issue directions 
ensuring that the Juvenile Justice Act and POCSO Act are implemented uniformly across all 
States/UTs. 
 
6.31 The Committee appreciates the development of portals such as GHAR (Go Home and 
Reunite) and e-Baalnidan for the restoration of children and grievance redressal. However, 
the Committee observes that coordination issues and resource constraints still hinder the 
comprehensive reach of these digital tools. The Committee recommends that the NCPCR 
should integrate its various portals i.e. Monitoring App for Seamless Inspection (MASI), Go 
Home and Reunite (GHAR) and POCSO Tracking into a unified national dashboard 
accessible to all district-level Child Welfare Committees (CWCs). This integration should 
facilitate real-time tracking of every child's rehabilitation plan, ensuring that no child 
remains in institutional care longer than necessary. 
 
6.32 The Committee notes that several challenges are being faced by the NCPCR in 
reaching marginalized and vulnerable communities in remote areas due to limited public 
awareness of its functions. The Committee, therefore, recommends the NCPCR to launch 
targeted awareness campaigns in Tier-II and Tier-III regions, focusing on child labour, 
trafficking, and the Right to Education (RTE).  
 
6.33 The Committee observes that the recommendations of NCW and NCPCR are non-
binding in nature which limits its ability to enforce child rights effectively, often leading to a 
lack of compliance from concerned authorities. The Committee recommends that the 
Ministry may evaluate the feasibility of strengthening the legal powers of the NCPCR 
through legislative amendments. This should include the authority to issue binding orders in 
specific cases of rights violations and the power to impose fines on non-compliant agencies to 
ensure that the Commission's oversight is not merely recommendatory but reformative in 
nature.

6.46 Efforts Undertaken by the Ministry to Promote Adoption of Special Needs Children 
1. Statutory Recognition of “Special Needs Child”: Regulation 2(25) of the Adoption 
Regulations, 2022 explicitly define “special needs child” as a child suffering from any disability 
recognised under the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016, as detailed in Schedule XVIII 
and Schedule III (Part E) of the Regulations. This statutory definition ensures priority treatment, 
specialised matching, and policy focus for such children. 
2. Preferential Eligibility of Prospective Adoptive Parents (PAPs): Couples with two or more 
children are ordinarily restricted from adoption unless the child is a special needs child or a hard-to-
place child, thereby creating a larger eligible pool specifically for special needs children 
(Regulation 5(7)). Age-related eligibility norms for PAPs are relaxed for adoption of older and 
special needs children, enabling wider participation. 
3. Special Referral Procedure for Special Needs Children: A separate and expedited referral 
mechanism is prescribed: 
a) Special needs children are first made available to Resident Indians, NRIs, and OCI 
cardholders for 15 days, and 
b) Thereafter, to all categories of Prospective Adoptive Parents, including foreign PAPs, 
without waiting for standard timelines (Regulation 8(2)). 
This ensures faster visibility and reduced waiting period for adoption of special needs children. 
4. Choice-Based Willingness Declaration by PAPs: The Designated Portal (CARINGS) enables 
PAPs to express willingness specifically for adoption of special needs children, ensuring: 
a) Targeted referrals, and 
b) Informed, conscious adoption decisions (Regulation 9(2)). 
5. Medical Transparency and Informed Consent: Mandatory preparation and disclosure of 
Medical Examination Report (Schedule III – Part E) and Child Study Report, it ensure PAPs 
receive full disclosure of the child’s medical condition, treatment needs, and prognosis before 
acceptance (Regulations 6, 11). PAPs are also permitted to seek independent medical opinion prior 
to accepting a special needs child. 

48 
 
6. Counselling and Pre-Adoption Orientation: Specialised Adoption Agencies are mandated to 
provide pre-adoption counselling, and orientation on special care, medical needs, and long-term 
support required for special needs children during the matching stage (Regulation 11(8)). 
7. Post-Adoption Monitoring and Support: Enhanced post-adoption follow-up for two years is 
mandated, including: 
a) Six-monthly follow-up reports, 
b) Counselling support in case of adjustment issues, and 
c) Monitoring of the child’s health and rehabilitation needs (Regulation 14). 
d) In cases of difficulty, professional counselling and linkage to district/state services is 
compulsory before any disruption or dissolution is considered. 
8. Priority Treatment under “Hard-to-Place” Framework: Special needs children who remain 
unadopted after prescribed timelines are categorised as “hard to place”, causing: 
(i) Wider circulation across all PAP categories, and 
(ii) Priority matching irrespective of seniority (Regulation 2(13) read with Regulation 8). 
9. Centralised Digital Platform for Visibility and Matching: The CARINGS through Mission 
Vatsalya portal ensures: National-level visibility of special needs children, transparent referral and 
matching and reduction of delays caused by geographical limitations. 
10. Capacity Building of Adoption Functionaries: In accordance with Regulation 41(11) of the 
Adoption Regulations, 2022, CARA has conducted and continues to conduct structured capacity-
building and sensitisation programmes for adoption functionaries across the country, these include:  
a) State Orientation Programmes on the Adoption Regulations,  
b) National and regional consultative workshops with States/UTs,  
c) Adoption Awareness Meets involving adoptive and prospective adoptive families,  
d) Regular virtual refresher trainings for stakeholders such as State Adoption Resource 
Agencies, Specialised Adoption Agencies, District Child Protection Units and Child 
Welfare Committees, each comprising a dedicated session on adoption of children with 
special needs and hard-to-place categories.  
 
6.47 These initiatives are aimed at strengthening procedural compliance, enhancing 
understanding of statutory provisions, and promoting timely, transparent and legally sound 
placement of children—particularly those with special needs—into family-based care.
