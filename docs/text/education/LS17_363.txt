INTRODUCTION 
 
I, the Chairman of the Department-related Parliamentary Standing Committee on 
Education, Women, Children, Youth and Sports, having been authorised by the Committee to 
present the Report on its behalf, do hereby present this Three Hundred and Sixty Third 
Report of the Committee on the Demands for Grants (Demand No. 25) of the Department of 
School Education & Literacy for the year 2025-26.  
2. In accordance with the constitutional provisions under Article 113, the estimated 
expenditure of the Ministries/Departments of the Government of India projected under 
various Demands for Grants for the upcoming financial year needs to be voted by Parliament. 
As a sequel thereto, Demands for Grants of the relevant Ministries/Departments stand 
referred to concerned Department-related Parliamentary Standing Committee to make a close 
scrutiny thereof under Rule 270 of Rules of Procedure and Conduct of Business in the 
Council of States (Rajya Sabha). While making scrutiny of the Demands for Grants, the 
Committee has made an appraisal of the performance, programmes, schemes and policies of 
the Department of School Education and Literacy vis-a-vis expenditure made out of the 
Consolidated Fund of India in the current financial year. 
 
3. The Budget allocations of the Ministry of Education have been sought under two 
Demand Numbers, i.e, Demand No. 25 pertains to the Department of School Education and 
Literacy and Demand No.26 pertains to Department of Higher Education. In this Report, a 
scrutiny of Demand No. 25 pertaining to the Department of School Education and Literacy of 
the Ministry of Education for the Financial Year 2025-26 has been made by the Committee. 
While scrutiny of Demand No. 26 pertaining to Department of Higher Education is covered 
in Report number 364th of the Committee. 
 
4.  During the course of examination of Demands, the Committee heard the views of the 
Secretary, Department of School Education and Literacy; Commissioner, Navodaya 
Vidyalaya Samiti (NVS); Commissioner, Kendriya Vidyalaya Sangathan (KVS); 
Chairperson, National Institute of Open Schooling (NIOS); Chairman, Central Board of 
Secondary Education (CBSE); Director, National Council of Educational Research and 
Training (NCERT); and Director, National Council for Teacher Education (NCTE) etc., in its 
meeting held on 20th February, 2025.

1. OVERVIEW OF THE DEPARTMENT OF SCHOOL EDUCATION & 
LITERACY 
1.1 Education forms the foundation of any society and it is a major factor in the 
economic, social, and political growth and development of a country. The growth of society 
depends upon the quality of education that is being imparted and the schools play an 
important role in moulding a nation’s future by facilitating all round development of its 
citizens. Education system in general and schools in particular, thus plays a pivotal role in not 
only shaping the young minds but also central to nation building. It is in this backdrop, the 
Committee on Education, Women, Children, Youth and Sports considered the Demands for 
Grants 2025-26 of the Department of School Education & Literacy, Ministry of Education. 
1.2 The Ministry of Education comprises two Departments, namely, (i) Department of 
School Education and Literacy and (ii) Department of Higher Education. Each of the 
Departments functions under the overall supervision of a Secretary. 
1.3 The Department of School Education and Literacy decides the policy and programmes 
relating to school education, adult education and digital education with focus on quality, 
equity and inclusion. The main objectives of the Department are: 
 Formulating the National Policy on school education and to ensure that it is 
implemented in letter and spirit. 
 Planned development, including expanding access and improving quality of the 
school educational institutions throughout the country, including in the regions where 
people do not have easy access to education. 
 Paying special attention to disadvantaged groups like the economically disadvantaged, 
females and the minorities 
 Providing financial help in the form of scholarships, loan subsidy, etc, to deserving 
students from deprived sections of the society. 
 Encouraging international cooperation in the field of education, including working 
closely with the UNESCO and foreign governments, to enhance the educational 
opportunities in the country. 
1.4 The following Autonomous Bodies/ Institutions under the Department are involved in 
policy formulation and implementation of various schemes relating to school education and adult 
education: 
a) National Council of Educational Research and Training (NCERT) 
b) National Council for Teacher Education (NCTE) 
c) Central Board of Secondary Education (CBSE) 
d) National Institute of Open Schooling (NIOS) 
e) KendriyaVidyalayaSangathan (KVS) 
f) NavodayaVidyalayaSamiti (NVS) 
g) National BalBhavan (NBB) 

2 
 
 
1.5 The Department also administers the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory 
Education (RTE) Act, 2009, as amended from time to time. The Act mandates the appropriate 
Government to provide free and compulsory elementary education to every child of the age 
group of 6 to 14 years in a neighbourhood school.  
 
1.6.1 The major Centrally Sponsored Schemes of the Department are as under:  
(i) Samagra Shiksha Abhiyan 
(ii) PradhanMantriPoshan Shakti Nirman (PM-POSHAN) 
(iii) New India Literacy Programme (NILP) 
(iv) Strengthening Teaching-Learning and Results for States (STARS) 
(v) PM-SHRI 
1.6.2 The Central Sector Schemes of the Department of School Education and Literacy are as 
under:- 
(i) National Means Cum Merit Scholarship Scheme 
(ii) PradhanMantri Innovative Learning Programme (DHRUV).

3.4 SAMAGRA SHIKSHA ABHIYAN (SSA) 
 
3.4.1 The Department of School Education and Literacy informed the Committee that it is 
implementing an integrated centrally sponsored scheme for School education- Samagra 
Shiksha which treats school education holistically and provides support for the implementation 
of the RTE Act. The Scheme is aligned with the recommendations of the National Education 
Policy (NEP) 2020 and aims to ensure that all children have access to quality education.  
 
Under the scheme, financial assistance is provided to States and UTs for undertaking 
various activities for universalization of education and for implementation of various 
interventions including opening of new schools/ upgradation/ strengthening of existing schools. 
This is based on the annual plans prepared by the States and UTs, as per their requirements/ 
priority and is reflected in their respective Annual Work Plan and Budget (AWP&B) Proposals. 
These plans are then appraised and approved/estimated by the Project Approval Board (PAB) 
in the Department of School Education & Literacy in consultation with the States and UTs as 
per the programmatic and financial norms of the scheme, and physical and financial progress of 
the State for the interventions approved earlier. 
 
3.4.2      The major objectives of the Scheme are:- 
 
(i) Support States and UTs in implementing the recommendations of the National 
Education Policy 2020 (NEP 2020);  
(ii) Support States in implementation of Right of Children to Free and Compulsory 
Education (RTE) Act, 2009;  
(iii) Focus on Early Childhood Care and  Education;  
(iv) Emphasis on Foundational Literacy and Numeracy;  
(v) Thrust on Holistic, Integrated, Inclusive and activity based Curriculum and Pedagogy 
to impart 21st century skills among the students;  
(vi) Provision of quality education and enhancing learning outcomes of students;  
(vii) Bridging Social and Gender Gaps in School Education;  
(viii) Ensuring equity and inclusion at all levels of school education;  
(ix) Strengthening and up-gradation of State Councils for Educational Research and 
Training (SCERTs)/State Institutes of Education and District Institutes for Education 
and Training (DIET) as nodal agency for teacher training;  
(x) Ensuring safe, secure and conducive learning environment and maintenance of 
standards in schooling provisions; and  
(xi) Promoting skill education.

3.4.4 The Committee observes that to achieve near-universal primary education, 
Government of India spent several years pursuing policies that focused on the goal and 
provided adequate resourcing to back it up. The Sarva Shiksha Abhiyan and the Right to 
Education Act, 2009 were notable interventions in this regard. While near-universal levels 
of primary education have been reached, there still remain 11.7 lakh children across the 
country who are Out of School (OoSc). The Committee is of the view that this is partly 
because there is no proper mechanism to ensure that these children are retained in school. 
Section 4 of the RTE Act, 2009 primarily focuses on a ‘curative/rehabilitative approach’ in 
the form of bridge courses, etc., after the child has dropped out for several months – there 
is no protocol for timely preventive action by authorities to retain children in schools. This 
absence of a preventive protocol is incompatible with the concept of a Fundamental Right. 
 
3.4.5 The goal of the Samagra Shiksha Abhiyan is to enable RTE implementation in state 
schools. However, the Committee is pained to note that 15 years after the RTE Act was 
passed, we have still not reached universal achievement of RTE norms by schools and 
teachers. In far too many schools, the pupil-teacher ratio, the number of classrooms, or the 
availability of water and sanitation facilities and/or playgroundsis inadequate. The 
Committee notes the tremendous achievements of the Samagra Shiksha Abhiyan butcalls 
for a revamping of the scheme to function on mission mode. The mission must be to achieve 
near-saturation of the RTE’s requirements by 2029. The Finance Minister’s Budget 
announcement of achieving universal broadband connectivity for rural secondary schools 
within the year offers a model for the scale and timeline at which the Government should 
be operating at to upgrade the physical and human resources offered by schools. 
At the same time,it is not advisable to impose the RTE’s norms and requirements on 
schools without forethought.There are several schools, especially in underserved, remote, 
and Adivasi-majority areas, which may be unable to meet the formal requirements 
demanded by the RTE. Recently, the only Gondi-language school in Maharashtra, in 
Gadchiroli district, was forced to shut down since it did not have the requisite approval 
from the State Government as mandated by the RTE.Similar stories have emerged from 
ST dominant areas in Assam. Recognizing that the only feasible alternative to these schools 
is no school, the Department must relax RTE norms in such instances.Relaxation of RTE 
norms can only be done with a view to preserve schools – not with a view to consolidate 
them or to continue to under-resource them.

recommends that the Department should develop effective mechanism to stop closure of schools 
and to open new Government schools in view of the increasing population density of the area and in 
compliance of RTE Act, 2009 to ensure cheapest and best education to poorest of the poor children 
of the country and other disadvantaged sections of the society. 
 
3.4.12 The Committee notes that in some areas, considerable number of the children in 
schools that have shut down drop out of the school, and that the effects are 
disproportionately felt by girl children, children with disabilities, and Scheduled Tribe and 
Scheduled Caste communities. In this context, the Committee feels that being the nodal 
Department for school education, it is responsibility of the Department to collect data on school 
closures and consolidations and Committee should be apprised thereof. The lack of data precludes 
any systematic study or policy of the impact that such school closures may have on students.The 
Committee, therefore, directs the Department to develop a policy on school closure and 
consolidation. Some features of such a policy may include –  
 Clear guidelines on when it is acceptable to consider school consolidation, ensuring 
that RTE guidelines are not violated 
 Mandatory impact assessment of each school closure 
 Mandatory centralised collection of all data relating to school closures, since the 
Department has been entrusted with overseeing RTE implementation 
 Protocol for tracking students impacted by school closures and ensuring they are 
transferred to another school 
 Provision of transport allowance for students impacted by closure of neighborhood 
schools to travel to a more distant school complex 
The Committee also feels that any school consolidations or closures should not violate RTE 
norms on the physical proximity of primary schools to children. The Committee notes that in its 
discussion of school complexes, the NEP does not reiterate the RTE’s guidance on the necessity to 
have primary schools (Grade I-V) within 1 km of a neighborhood and upper primary schools 
(Grade VI-VIII) within 3 km of a neighborhood. However, this does not mean that the NEP’s 
proposed school complexes overrule or modify the provisions of the RTE Act, 2009. The Committee 

17 
 
emphasizes that the RTE is a law passed by Parliament which deals with the fundamental rights of 
Indian children and. Department should evolve a mechanism to ensure that School consolidations 
are not done in a manner that contradicts the RTE Act, 2009.

3.4.50  The Committee recognizes that large parts of India are disaster-prone, with

on the eastern coast of the country. However, it notes that there is no existing, 
comprehensive policy guidance on how school education should respond to a natural 
disaster. There is a need to do so given that most schools become relief camps in the event 
of a disaster, and schooling activities come to a stop, sometimes for months at a time. This 
leads to a learning loss, in addition to damage to school property. Accordingly the 
Committee recommends the formulation of a policy for school education in disaster-hit

Management Authority. Some features of this policy could include –  
 Provision for additional textbooks and uniforms in disaster-hit areas;

 Examination relief for students in disaster-hit communities, who cannot give exams 
on the same timeline as the rest of the state or country; 
 An assessment of the learning loss that occurs in such areas and formulation of a 
strategy to minimise the damage in the medium-term; and 
 Integration of disaster management into curriculum.

the mother tongue or that which is spoken by local communities,” the NEP doesn’t 
adequately grapple with the circumstance of linguistic minorities who speak a language 
different from that of their state and/or region. As a result, as the Department has 
acknowledged,Samagra Shiksha has no special focus or programs for students belonging to 
linguistic minorities. This has a cascading impact on state-level policies to support linguistic 
minorities.For instance, Maharashtra – which has ~4.6 lakh Gondi speakers as of 2011 – 
does not have a single Gondi-medium school.  
Accordingly, the Committee recommends that the Department should create a special 
program within Samagra Shiksha that supports states in the following –  
 Coordinate with NCERT, NBT, and SCERT to ensure that TLM and all other 
resources are widely available (online for free and marketable) in all key languages 
– especially those spoken by Adivasi and tribal communities; 
 Develop and financially support bridge courses to teach the state’s dominant 
language as a second language to children belonging to linguistic minorities; 
 Develop pedagogy, teacher training, and TLM for instruction in multiple languages; 
and 

31 
 
 Support recruitment of new teachers from the local linguistic communities that the 
school caters to. 
 
3.4.52 The Committee observes that often times, there is lack of correspondence between 
Government data and independent evaluations of school education. For instance, the Annual 
Survey of Education Report (ASER) 2024 report shows that only 76% of schools have implemented 
Vidya Pravesh, only 90% of those schools who claim to have electricity connection actually have it, 
only 92% of eligible schools have mid- day meals, only 78% have drinking water, and 72% have a 
usable girls toilet. These figures are considerably lower than official data reports. Accordingly, the 
Committee recommends that the Department should set up a separate data monitoring team 
composed of Cluster Resource Coordinators or other officials to help improvement in the quality of 
the Department’s data on government schools. 
 
3.4.53 The Committeetakes note of the Vidyanjali initiative to bring in community 
volunteers into education. However, the Committee would suggest some changes to the 
scheme’s operation – 
 The Department must publish detailed data on asset contributions, services 
delivered, and volunteer participation through the scheme; 
 The Department must publish data on how many service requests have been filled in 
their entirety since scheme’s inception, as well as how many volunteer hours have 
been leveraged through it, and how much CSR has been directed to education 
through it; 
 The Departmentmust conduct a formal evaluation study for Vidyanjali to evaluate 
its impact; 
 There need to be clear guidelines on the eligibility and background of the volunteers 
in Vidyanjali, specifically to avoid the entry of politically motivated individuals into 
schools through the avenue of this scheme; and 
 The current process for Vidanjali appears to be impersonal and bureaucratic given 
that it is primarily accessed digitially. Such efforts are unlikely to result in the 
“community involvement and mobilization” that the NEP envisions. The Committee 
recommends a more local, community-centered approach to education volunteering, 
one that involves the existing Government apparatus of the Nehru YuvaKendras 
and the NSS.  
 
3.4.54 The Committee observes that the Department needs to empower the Cluster Resource 
Coordinators (CRCCs) and officials of the Block Resource Centers (BRCs) hired through the 
Samagra Shiksha Abhiyan given their importance in ensuring the scheme’s implementation– 
 The role of the CRCC has moved from mentorship to data collection and administration, 
but these are different skill sets. Clarity on the role of the CRCC can ensure states don’t 
hire non-teachers who aren’t able to mentor effectively; and 
 For administrative, data collection, and mentoring roles, clear provisions must be made for 
TA/DA to enable CRCCs to visit the ground. The Samagra Shiksha financial norms specify 
a discretionary budget of up to Rs. 3 lakh per annum for “TLE/TLM, recurring 
expenditure, meetings, contingencies etc”. However, it is not clear whether this amount is 

32 
 
for the state, a district, or a block. A specific budget for each BRC official and CRCC for 
their travel should be given. 
3.4.55 Early Childhood Care and Education (ECCE)-The Committee observes that 90% of 
an infant’s brain development occurs between 2-5 years. Universal ECCE, as envisioned in 
the NEP is therefore critical to securing lifelong thinking and reasoning capabilities for all 
of India’s children. However, the Committee observes that universal ECCE by 2030, as 
envisioned by NEP, remains a distant dream five years after the adotion of the NEP. The 
Committee regrets that the Kasturirangan Committee’s recommendation that ECCE be an 
integral part of RTE Act did not find space in the NEP. It therefore strongly recommends 
the expansion of the RTE to include ECCE for children aged 3 years to 6 years. The 
Committee is deeply concerned about the limited progress the Ministry has made in 
achieving universal ECCE. Physical convergence of Bal Vatikas and Primary Schools with 
Anganwadishas not occurred. More importantly, neither has operational convergence.The 
Committee observes that children enrolled in Bal Vatikas (pre-schools in the Education 
Department) get mid-day meals through PM POSHAN while those in Anganwadis get it 
through the Saksham Anganwadi scheme. In Anganwadis, children are offered a welcome 
snack in addition to the meal. Children in Bal Vatikas get only a meal. Nutritional 
guidelines for both appear to be different, as are the cost normsSEL has prepared the 
Integrated Teacher Education Program for ECCE and NISHTHA ECCE. However, 
Anganwadi workers are being trained by NIPCCD under Poshan Bhi Padhai Bhi scheme. 
The Committee is very keen on ensuring that regardless of which department runs the 
ECCE program, children need to be guaranteed the same food and the teachers need to be 
trained in a standardized fashion. The Committee also emphasizes that ECCE includes 
early childhood care and education – care is an integral part of what must be available to 
children, and Anganwadi workers are therefore part of the solution. The Department must 
work with the WCD Ministry to achieve universal ECCE. The Committee observes that 
Department of SEL and WCD have created a Joint Task Force to implement universal 
ECCE, but it appears to be ineffective. Given the demonstrated inadequacy of the two 
departments concerned, i.e., SEL and WCD, in making progress on universal ECCE, the 
Committee recommends a national-level mission under the supervision of the PM to 
operationalize and accelerate convergence between the Bal Vatikas and Anganwadis.   
3.4.56 The Committee observes that over the past five years, ECCE has consistently received only 
about 2% of the total budget allocated under the Samagra Shiksha Scheme. Such limited budgetary 
provisions compromise the availability of essential resources such as quality teaching-learning 
materials (TLM), training and capacity-building programs for educators, and recruitment of 
adequate human resources to effectively implement ECCE initiatives. Inadequate funding 
undermines the capacity of ECCE programs to deliver impactful and quality early education, 
which is crucial for fostering cognitive and social skills among young learners. To 
effectivelystrengthen ECCE, enhance classroom interactions, and ensure foundational 
preparedness of young children for formal schooling, an increased budgetary commitment is 
imperative. Therefore, it is recommended that the budget allocation dedicated to ECCE be 
increased substantially, ideally to at least 5% of the total Samagra Shiksha budget. Such enhanced 
investment will significantly contribute to achieving the overarching goals of holistic child 
development, inclusive educational access, and improved foundational learning outcomes 
nationwide.

3.4.57 The Committee takes note of the 2022 amendment to Section 20 of the RTE, which 
mandates schools to have 1 special education teacher for every 10 CwDs for classes 1 to 5, and 1 
special education teacher for every 15 CwDs for classes 6 to 8. While many states have already 
successfully sought grants for the same, the Committtee strongly recommends the earmarking of 
specific funds through Samagra Shiksha for conducting a systematic census based identification 
exercise of CwD (Children with Disabilities), the hiring of special educators in all schools, and the 
training of special educators. Based on the identification exercise held by states, more funds may 
need to be dedicated to the hiring of special educators. 
3.4.58 The Committee notes that UDISE data from 2023-2024 shows that 90.4% of government 
schools have electricity connections. However, data from the Annual Status of Education Report 
(ASER) 2024 shows that only 90% of those schools which are designated as having electricity 
connection actually had functional connections that were active on the day of the ASER team’s 
visit. The Samagra Shiksha’s Financial Norms allow funds for payment of electricity bills and for 
electricity connections, but there is no clear provision for upgrading schools with existing electricity 
connections to ensure that these connections are active. The Committee recommends the provision 
of a separate budget for providing functional electricity connection in all schools. 
3.4.59 The Committee notes that as per Samagra Shiksha financial norms for upper-primary 
grades, Rs. 500 per child per year is allotted to each school for a “Learning Enhancement 
Program” (LEP) which can be utilized for remedial or “catch up” programs so that children 
acquire the skills expected of them upon completion of primary grades. This amount is given 
keeping in mind only 25% of student strength of the school – however, data repeatedly shows that 
the proportion of children requiring remedial support is much higher than the 25% for whom 
funds are sanctioned.  The Committee recommends that the financial provision be enhanced to 
support remedial learning for upto 50% of the student strength. 
3.4.60 The Committee notes the recent publication of the UDISE+ report for 2022-2023 and 2023-
2024 but notes the delay in the publication of the 2022-2023 report. Such delays must be avoided in 
the future. It also notes that the archival repository of DISE data before 2022 (earlier accessible at 
http://schoolreportcards.in) is not available anymore. The Committee recommends that this data 
may be made publicly available at the earliest in the interest of transparency.  
3.4.61 The Committee notes that the Department runs the Foundational Learning Study (FLS) 
and the National Assessment Survey (NAS) to evaluate student learning outcomes nationally. 
However, it recommends that their methodologies be standardized and rationalized – 
 NAS numeracy tests at Grade 3 include three-digit numbers and operations, which is 
beyond the scope of many children’s learning capacity 
 Literacy assessments in NAS focus only on reading comprehension, but need to incorporate 
the critical sub-skills of vocabulary and phonics essential for reading proficiency  
NAS should be redesigned to conduct one-on-one oral assessments as used in the FLS – this enables 
deeper evaluation of FLN skills and identification of gaps and learning difficulties 
3.4.62 The Committee notes that the Department of School Education and Literacy does not 
currently track state-level student academic IDs. It recommends that the Department do so and 
ensure the linking of these state IDs to the APAAR ID to ensure single unified data sets and 
interoperability. 

34 
 
3.4.63 The Committee recommends that the process to give UDISE+ codes to the 22,298 schools 
without these codes must be expedited and that all students in these schools must be given APAAR 
IDs at the earliest. Further, it recommends that no student who is unable to or elects not to furnish 
their Aadhaar should be denied their APAAR ID. 
3.4.64 The Committee recommends that DIKSHA portal should be linked to UDISE+, Aadhaar, 
and APAAR ID to enable granular tracking of the uptake of DIKSHA facilities. 
3.4.65 The Committee recommends that the Kasturba Gandhi Balika Vidyalayas be expanded, 
particularly in ST-majority districts like Jhargram, including the Nayagram Block and Bandwan 
Block. 
3.4.66 The Committee notes that teachers are at the heart of the NEP – and none of the ideas 
discussed in the policy are possible without re-establishing “teachers, at all levels, as the most 
respected and essential members of our society” (NEP). The Committee believes that the first step 
towards restoring the status of teachers is to ensure that all teachers have the same service 
conditions, regarding pay, in-service teacher-training, transfers and other benefits. The dual system 
of regular teachers and contractual teachers creates a system of different pay for equal work, which 
goes against the spirit of the constitutional guarantee of equal pay for equal work. The hiring of 
contractual teachers also undermines the Constitutional right of SCs, STs, and OBCs to reservation 
in public employment. The Committee notes that the NEP 2020, unlike the draft version released in 
2019, does not make any unequivocal statement on discontinuing the practice of hiring contract 
teachers. The Committee therefore recommends that the Department direct the utilisation of 
Samagra Shiksha funds by states towards the hiring of regular teachers alone, or the regularisation 
of contractual teachers. The Department must develop a clear roadmap for transitioning 
contractual teachers into permanent positions within the next five years.States must also be 
encouraged to count years on contract as being part of a teacher’s service when considering 
promotions. 
3.4.67 The Committee notes that single-teacher schools continue to exist despite years after the 
passage of the RTE. It notes that the RTE’s stipulated Pupil Teacher Ratio of 30:1 for primary 
schools and 35:1 for upper primary schools took the focus away from the importance of having one 
teacher per grade. Thus, the Committee unequivocally advocates for a new policy of requiring one 
teacher per grade. 
3.4.68 The Committee recommends increased funding for regional language learning resources in 
school and higher education curriculums to implement NEP’s vision of instruction in the child’s 
mother tongue.” 
3.5 PRADHAN MANTRI POSHAN SHAKTI NIRMAN (PM POSHAN) SCHEME  
3.5.1 The Department has informed the Committee thatPradhanMantriPoshan Shakti Nirman 
(PM POSHAN) Scheme is one of the foremost rights based Centrally Sponsored Schemes. 
Under the Scheme one hot cooked meal is served to students studying in BalVatika and Classes 
l-VIII in Government and Government aided Schools on all school working days. The main 
objectives of the PM POSHAN Scheme are as follows: 
i) Improving the nutritional status of children studying in BalVatika and classes I – 
VIII in Government and Government-aided schools.  
ii) Encouraging poor children, belonging to disadvantaged sections, to attend school 
more regularly and help them concentrate on classroom activities.  

35 
 
iii) Providing nutritional support to children of elementary stage in drought affected 
areas during summer vacation and during disaster times.  
3.5.2 Under the PM-POSHAN Scheme Central Assistance is released for the following 
components: 
a) Recurring components 
i) Cost of Food Grains 
ii) Transportation Assistance 
iii) Management, Monitoring & Evaluation (MME) 
iv) Material Cost 
v) Honorarium to Cook-cum-Helpers 
b) Non-recurring components 
i) Construction of kitchen-cum-stores 
ii) Repair of kitchen-cum-stores 
iii) Procurement of kitchen devices 
 
3.5.3 The details of Centre-State sharing of the cost of Components is as follows: 
(i) The Central Government bears entire cost of following components: 
o Food grains,  
o Transportation cost  
o Monitoring Management and Evaluation (MME). 
(ii) The material cost and honorarium to cook-cum-helpers is shared as follows: 
o Between the Centre and the NER States & Himalayan States and UT with 
Legislature (J&K) on 90:10 basis,  
o 100% cost is borne by Centre for UTs without legislature,  
o Between other States and UTs with Legislature (GNCT of Delhi and Puducherry) 
on 60:40 basis 
(iii)The non-recurring central assistance for kitchen-cum-stores, procurement/replacement of 
kitchen-devices and repair of ten years old kitchen-cum-stores is shared between the 
Centre and the NER States & Himalayan States on 90:10 basis, 100% for UTs and 60:40 
with other States and UTs with Legislature (GNCT of Delhi and Puducherry).The 
Scheme provided for a 5-tier institutional mechanism at National, State, District, Block 
and School levels for effective implementation of the Scheme.

3.5.10 The Committee notes that while PM POSHAN has contributed significantly to 
school enrollment and attendance, the scheme remains incomplete in coverage and lacks 
effective monitoring mechanisms to ensure meal quality etc.. Additionally, adolescent 
malnutrition continues to be a concern, as students in secondary and senior secondary 
schools are excluded from the program. Strengthening nutritional support will directly 
impact student performance and retention. The Committee, therefore, recommends that 
the Department of School Education and Literacy should take initiatives on the following:- 
(i) Expansion of PM POSHAN to Secondary Education: PM POSHAN coverage should 
be extended to students upto Class 12th (upto the age of 18 years), with a focus on 
preventing adolescent dropouts and addressing gender disparities in nutrition. This 
is a natural corollary of the Committee’s recommendation to extend the RTE until 
the age of 18. 
(ii) Breakfast Inclusion: A national breakfast program should be introduced to combat 
morning hunger and enhance cognitive performance, particularly in rural and 
economically weaker sections. This has already been envisioned in the NEP but is 

38 
 
yet to be implemented by the Department. 
(iii) Decentralized Procurement & Localized Meal Planning: Community-led food 
sourcing, integrating locally grown grains, vegetables, and dairy should be 
promoted to improve nutrition while supporting local farmers as well. 
(iv) Third-Party Quality Audits: Quarterly meal quality, hygiene, and taste of the mid-
day meal checks should be conducted through independent agencies, ensuring 
compliance with dietary standards and addressing reported issues in real-time. 
(v) Nutritional Fortification: Iron and protein fortification in mid-day meals should be 
implemented to tackle widespread deficiencies in school-aged children.  Provision of 
a glass of milk daily should be ensured under the scheme, adopting the Karnataka 
model of Ksheera Bhagya in view of the fact that around 40 to 50% of children in 
the country are stunted. 
(vi) Revision of Nutritional Norms:The nutritional norms should be revised for the 
meals under PM POSHAN on the line of recommendations by the ICMR.  
(vii)  The Department should significantly hike the compensation paid to mid-day meal 
workers. 
 
 
3.5.11 The Committee is of the view that there is inadequate convergence with other 
Ministries/ Departments and Schemes being run by them which is adversely affecting 
effective implementation of crucial schemes like PM POSHAN. The Committee 
recommends that the Department of School Education and Literacy should make sincere 
efforts for convergence amongst various Ministries, like Ministries of Education, Health 
and Women and Child Development, for seamless implementation of schemes of various 
Ministries related to children and their development.  
 
3.5.12 The Committee appreciates that the Department has revised the existing rates of 
material cost to Rs. 6.19 per child per day for Bal Vatika and  Primary classes and Rs. 9.29 
per child per day for upper primary classes w.e.f. 1st December, 2024. However, the 
Committee strongly feels that Rs. 6.19 per child per day for Bal Vatika and  Primary 
classes and Rs. 9.29 per child per day for upper primary classes, are too meagre amounts to 
feed nutritious food to the children of the country  in view of inflationary trends in the 
country. The Committee, therefore, recommends that the Department should relook into 
the revision of rates of material cost per child per day and revise it  to a reasonable limit 
per child per day, so that, provision of a glass of milk, iron and protein fortification and 
breakfast can be ensured for the school children.

for ensuring the effective implementation of the scheme.

3.7 STRENGTHENING TEACHING-LEARNING AND RESULTS FOR STATE (STARS): 
3.7.1 The Department has apprised the Committee that the  STARS program is designed to improve the 
quality of school education in India with a focus on ensuring inclusivity, equity, and quality education 
across various levels from pre-school to senior secondary.The STARS program is aligned with key 
initiatives of the Government of India, including the SamagraShiksha Scheme and the National 
Education Policy (NEP) 2020, which emphasizes holistic development and inclusive education.It 
focuses on Early Childhood Care and Education (ECCE), teacher training, including the completion of 50 
hours of Continuing Professional Development (CPD), and skill education. Thus, STARS assists in 
achieving the objectives of NEP 2020. The program aims to bridge social and gender gaps, enhance the 
educational outcomes for marginalized communities, and promote vocational education. It also seeks to 
improve governance and the management of the education system in the selected States. The program is 
being implemented in six states i.e. Himachal Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, Maharashtra, Odisha, 
and Kerala. The total financial outlay for the program is US $500 million (1$US = Rs. 75.39, as on 
Programme Appraisal Date 02-06-2020) provided by the World Bank as a loan. The rest of the funding 
comes from the participating States. The program spans five years, from FY 2020-21 to FY 2024-25.The 
project started on February 23, 2021, with an initial five-year timeline set to end in FY 2024-25.The key 
focus areas of STARS are as under: 
 Improving Early Education: Strengthening early years of education to ensure foundational 
learning for children. 
 Teacher Development: Enhancing teacher performance through training and professional 
development, which is critical to improving classroom practices. 
 Learning Assessment Systems: Strengthening systems to assess learning outcomes and ensure 
they meet national standards. 

46 
 
 School-to-Work Transition: Focusing on skills development and vocational education to aid 
students in transitioning from school to employment. 
 Governance and Decentralized Management: Strengthening governance mechanisms to ensure 
better management and delivery of educational services at the state and district levels.

3.8 NEW INDIA LITERACY PROGRAMME/ UNDERSTANDING OF LIFELONG 
LEARNING FOR ALL IN SOCIETY (ULLAS)/ULLAS- NAV BHARAT 
SAAKSHARTAKARYAKRAM 
3.8.1 ULLAS - Nav Bharat SaakshartaKaryakram, a Centrally Sponsored Scheme is an educational 
initiative implemented from FY 2022-23 to 2026-27. ULLAS, which stands for Understanding of 
Lifelong Learning for All in Society, aligns with the National Education Policy (NEP) 2020 and aims to 
provide educational opportunities to adults aged 15 and above who have missed formal schooling. The 
scheme is implemented in a hybrid mode, States/UTs have flexibility in offline, online, or combined 
approaches. The scheme has five components, namely: (i) Foundational Literacy and Numeracy, (ii) 
Critical Life Skills, (iii) Basic Education, (iv) Vocational Skills, and (v) Continuing Education. 
 
3.8.2 The scheme has a total outlay of 1037.9 crores over the period of five years, out of which Central 
Share is 700 crores and State Share is 337.90 crores. Equipped with classrooms, libraries, and digital 
resources, schools can transform into SamajikChetnaKendras at various places, including government-
aided and unaided schools, Panchayat Offices, Community Halls, and One Stop Centres, where 
foundational literacy, numeracy, and life skills are taught both online and offline. Moreover, using school 
infrastructure ensures the effective organization and administration of testing centers for neo-literates, 
reinforcing the importance of schools in the ULLAS framework. A dedicated ULLAS Mobile App has 
been created for registering learners and volunteer teachers and also enables teaching learning process by 
providing access to primers in 26 languages. Currently, 38,36,861Volunteer Teachers are registered on 
the ULLAS Mobile App, actively contributing to the mission. Additionally, students from Schools, 
Teacher Education Institutions, and Higher Education Institutions are volunteering under ULLAS. These 
students are involved in imparting learning, motivating learners, and conducting surveys, thereby playing 
a crucial role in the program's success. To participate in the scheme, anyone with an educational 
qualification of Class VIII and above, who can operate a mobile phone and is ready to offer voluntary 
services in teaching-learning activities, can become a Volunteer Teacher (VT) under ULLAS. 
Registration on the ULLAS Mobile App/Portal is mandatory for VTs. These volunteers may offer their 
services at the School, Gram Panchayat, Ward, Block, Town, or District level.To provide academic and 
resource support, a dedicated National Centre for Literacy (NCL) has been established at 
NCERT.Institutions such as UGC, AICTE, and NCTE are actively engaging their resources in supporting 
the ULLAS scheme in a comprehensive manner. 
To evaluate its impact, the Foundational Literacy and Numeracy Assessment Test (FLNAT) 
certifies non-literate learners, with over 22.88 lakh learners passing the test in 2024, showing improved 
enrolment and success rates compared to previous years. Over 1 crore learners have appeared for the 
FLNAT, which has been held four times. States/UTs have the flexibility to conduct FLNAT according to 

50 
 
their convenience.  Celebratory events like the UllasMela showcase state-wise progress, with the 
participation of dignitaries, volunteer teachers, and learners. Capacity-building initiatives, the 
development of resource materials, and the establishment of model SamajikChetnaKendras further 
strengthen the scheme's implementation, aiming to ensure education for all and contribute to the nation's 
growth.

4.3 THE NATIONAL MISSION ON FOUNDATIONAL LITERACY AND 
NUMERACY (NIPUN BHARAT MISSION)  
4.3.1 National Mission for universalizing Foundational Literacy and Numeracy called National 
Initiative for Proficiency in Reading with Understanding and Numeracy (NIPUN BHARAT) 
launched on 5th July 2021. The mission aims to ensure that every child in the country attains 
foundational literacy and numeracy by the end of Grade 2, by 2027. Set up under the aegis of the 
centrally sponsored scheme of Samagra Shiksha. All 36 States/Union Territories are 
implementing NIPUNUnder this mission, The national mission lays down priorities and 
actionable agenda for states and UTs to achieve the goal of proficiency in foundational literacy 
and numeracy for every child by the end of grade 3. Vidya Pravesh, a 3 Months Play Based 
School Preparation Module instruction for the children entering of Grade I was developed and 
launched on 29th July, 2021. Vidya Pravesh is now an annual calendar and over 1 crore students 
from 8.94 lakh schools participated in Vidya Pravesh programme across States/UTs in 2024-25. 
 
4.3.2 Learning outcomes have been formulated in consultation with stakeholders of each level 
of foundational stage. These are the based on the learning  of children in the area of Reading, 
Writing, Arithmetic and Socio Emotional Skills. The Learning Outcomes or Lakshyas on various 
parameters have been redefined in alignment with the NEP 2020, NCF-FS and NIPUN 
guidelines and have been shared with all the States & UTs on 20.08.2024. It will provide clarity 
on the outcomes to be achieved and can be measured for targeted inputs and evaluation.  
4.3.3 The NEP 2020 emphasizes on developing relevant concepts and attaining requisite 
competencies to facilitate optimal learning when children start schooling. Keeping in focus the 
holistic development, a 3 Months Play Based ‘School Preparation Module and Guidelines’ for 
Grade I named ‘Vidya Pravesh’ was launched on 29th July, 2021. The goal of Vidya Pravesh 
programme is to promote school preparedness in all children coming to Grade- I from diverse 
backgrounds (Balvatika, AWCs, at home, private play schools etc.), to ensure a smooth transition 
of children to Grade-I. It provides play based, age and developmentally appropriate learning 
experiences in a joyful and stimulating environment leading to holistic development to bolster a 
child’s pre-literacy, pre-numeracy, cognitive and social skills. All States/UTs are implementing 
Vidya Pravesh programme. The Vidya Pravesh program was implemented in 8,85,902 
government and government-aided schools in 2023-24, benefiting 1,13,60,923 students 
4.3.4 The Committee observes that foundational literacy and numeracy skills are critical 
for long-term educational success. However, fund underutilization and insufficient per-
teacher allowances have limited the program’s reach and its impact. Strengthening 
implementation mechanisms can accelerate the improvement of literacy rates and 
numeracy competencies across the States. Accordingly, the Committee recommends the 
Department of School Education and Literacy as under:- 

57 
 
(i)  Enhanced Teacher Resource Allocation: The Department should increase per-
teacher resource allowance from ₹150 to ₹1,500 per year, ensuring sufficient access to 
graded reading materials, manipulatives, and remedial tools; 
(ii) Annual Literacy & Numeracy Assessments: The Department should conduct 
standardized, state-level competency assessments, ensuring targeted interventions for 
underperforming schools; 
(iii) Community-Based Learning Support: The Department should establish volunteer-
based reading camps in rural areas, engaging educated youth and retired teachers in 
literacy initiatives; 
(iv) Multilingual Learning Strategies: The Department should develop mother-tongue-
based literacy modules, integrating bilingual learning resources to ease transition into 
formal education; and 
(v) Tech-Enabled Learning: The Department should deploy AI-driven adaptive 
learning tools to track student progress and personalize remedial instruction based on real-
time learning data. 
(vi) Teaching Learning Material (TLM): Given the absence of at-home learning and 
lack of access to print material at home, students must not have shortage of pedagogical 
material like workbooks etc in class and must have print-rich classroom environments. The 
Department may evaluate the need for increased TLM funding from existing norms.  
 
4.3.5 The Committee observes that the National Education Policy (NEP) 2020 accorded 
the highest priority to FLN, stating that “the rest of this Policy will become relevant for our 
students only if this most basic learning requirement (i.e., reading, writing, and arithmetic 
at the foundational level) is first achieved.” Accordingly, the Committee encourages the 
Department to regard NIPUN Bharat as one of its flagship missions, and provide resources 
and visibility to it accordingly. The Committee recommends that the Department publish 
an annual report on the NIPUN Bharat Mission.  
 
4.3.6 The The Committee notes that the recently released Annual Status of Education 
Report (ASER) survey 2024 showed some encouraging findings for Foundational Literacy 
and Numeracy (FLN), with Grade III students recovering the learning loss in reading

deadline for the NIPUN Bharat Mission must be extended from 2027 to 2032. The

4.3.7 Given the limited capacity of state governments to spend on education, the 
Department must also continue to provide budgetary support to NIPUN Bharat to ensure 
its success.

network with state educational departments, universities, NGOs and other educational 
institutions;

and  
e. act as a nodal agency for achieving the goals of Universalisation of Elementary 
Education.

(iii) Incorporation of Feedback Mechanisms:-Updation is also done on the basis of

education is a subject in the Concurrent List of the Constitution. Schools, other than those 
owned/funded by the Central Government, are under the jurisdiction of the respective State 
Governments. Therefore, matters relating to fees charged by private schools and related issues 
are regulated in terms of the Rules and Instructions of the respective State Government 
concerned. As far as CBSE is concerned, it has issued advisories to all its affiliated schools time 
and again not to indulge in commercial activities by way of selling of text books, note books, 
stationary items, uniforms etc and to adhere to the provisions of Affiliation Bye-Laws of the 
Board. Clause 9.1.3 of Affiliation Bye Laws of the Board stipulates that “It shall ensure that the 
school is run as a community service and not as a business and that commercialization does not 
take place in the school in any manner whatsoever.”  Further, the Board vide circular NO. 
CBSE/ AFF/CIRCULAR/2018/1393058 dated 21.06.2018 had issued advisory to schools 
regarding not to coerce parents/students for buying the books, stationery, uniforms or any other 
items from the school tuckshop or from any other specified vendors.

5.2.8 The Committee observes that few private schools force students and their parents to 
purchase books, shoes, dresses etc. from specific stores recommended by those schools, on 
very higher prices. Moreover, these schools have no capping on fees and other charges 
collected from students. Private schools are running like commercial business instead of 

67 
 
community service against the provisions of Clause 9.1.3 of Affiliation Bye Laws of the

5.2.9 The Committee is expresses its concerns about the CBSE's draft policy for the

5.3 KENDRIYA VIDYALAYA SANGATHAN (K.V.S)  
5.3.1 The Department has informed that the Government of India approved the Scheme of 
KendriyaVidyalayas in November 1962 to provide educational facilities for the children of 
transferable Central Government/ Defence employees.  Initially, 20 Regimental Schools in 
Defence Stations were taken over as Central Schools during the Academic Year 1963-64 and 
functioned as a unit (Central School Unit) of the Ministry of Education, Government of India. 
On 15.12.1965 it, was registered as Central Schools Organization as a Society and became an 
autonomous organization. On 03.07.1967, the name of “Central School Organization” was

the Central Schools, henceforth called KendriyaVidyalayas.  As on 01.10.2024 there are 1256 
functional KendriyaVidyalayas including three KendriyaVidyalayas abroad at Kathmandu, 
Moscow and Tehran with 13,56,258 students.  Recently, Cabinet Committee on Economic 
Affairs (CCEA) in its meeting held on 06.12.2024 has approved the proposal for opening of 85 
new KVs in Civil/Defence Sector across the country and expansion of one existing KV 
Shivamogga, Karnataka.  KVS has 05 Zonal Institutes for Educational Training i.e. one each at 
Gwalior (MP), Mysore (Karnataka), Mumbai (Maharashtra) Chandigarh (UT) and 
Bhubaneshwar, (Odisha).  All these ZIETs are engaged in the conduct of In-service courses, 
Induction Courses and short duration workshops aimed at empowering the teaching and non-
teaching staff. 
 
5.3.2     The main objectives of KendriyaVidyalayas are as under: 
a) to cater to the educational needs of the children of transferable Central Government 
employees including Defence and Para-Military Personnel by providing a common 
programme of education. 
b) to provide, establish, endow, maintain, control and manage schools, hereinafter called 
‘the KendriyaVidyalayas’ for the children of transferable employees of the Government 
of India, floating population and others including those living in remote and undeveloped 
locations of the country and to do all acts and things necessary for or conducive to the 
promotion of such schools. 

68

d) to initiate and promote experimentation and innovativeness in education in collaboration 
with other bodies like the CBSE, NCERT etc., and

children.

5.7 NATIONAL COUNCIL FOR TEACHER EDUCATION (NCTE): 
5.7.1 The National Council for Teacher Education (NCTE) was established as a statutory body 
by an Act of Parliament (Act No. 73 of 1993) with a view to achieve planned and coordinated 
development of teacher education throughout the country, the regulation and proper maintenance 
of norms and standards in the teacher education system and for matters connected therewith. The 
NCTE Act 1993 (NCTE Act) came into force w.e.f. 1st July 1995 and NCTE came into existence 
on 17th August 1995 with its Head Office in New Delhi. The Council is the highest decision 
making body of NCTE consisting of 43 Members comprising of the Chairperson, Vice-
Chairperson, Member Secretary on full time basis besides the Education Secretary of the MoE, 
GoI, Adviser (Education), Planning Commission, Education Secretaries of the State 

79 
 
Governments, eminent academicians and Heads of National Educational Institutions like 
NCERT, NIEPA, UGC, CBSE, AICTE etc. The Chairperson, Vice-Chairperson and Member 
Secretary are appointed by the GoI. The NCTE is headed by the Chairperson who is vested with 
executive powers and, along with the RCs, is responsible for implementing the provisions of the 
NCTE Act, Rules and Regulations made thereunder, and also the policy decisions of the Council 
taken from time to time. 
5.7.2 As per Section 20 of the NCTE Act, the 4 RCs i.e. Eastern, Western, Northern and 
Southern deal with matters related to grant of recognition / permission to the TEIs under Section 
14/15 of the NCTE Act in the States/UTs under their jurisdiction. The RCs receive applications 
from the applicant institutions for starting a new teacher education programme or for 
introduction of another teacher education programme in an existing TEIs or for additional intake 
in an existing teacher education programme under Section 14/15 of the NCTE Act. The RCs also 
exercise powers to withdraw recognition/permission of a recognized institution under Section 17 
of the NCTE Act, provided it is established that the concerned institution has contravened the 
provisions of the NCTE Act / Rules / Regulations under which recognition/permission was 
granted to it. The RCs, besides the functions under Section 14, 15 and 17 of the NCTE Act, 
perform such other functions as may be assigned to it by the Council or as may be determined by 
Regulations. No Budgetary support is provided to NCTE by the Department of School Education 
& Literacy. However, grants under Samagra Shiksha are provided to NCTE for specific projects. 
5.7.3 The details of NEP 2020 mandates pertaining to NCTE are as under: 
(i) Integrated Teacher Education Programme (ITEP): As outlined in Para 15.5 of NEP 2020, 
the Integrated Teacher Education Programme (ITEP) is a pioneering initiative that combines 
education with a specific discipline and stage-specific specialization.  
(ii) National Mission for Mentoring (NMM): National Mission for Mentoring (NMM) as 
envisaged in para 15.11 of NEP 2020, envisioned the creation of a large pool of outstanding 
professionals willing to provide mentoring to schoolteachers. These potential mentors, regardless 
of the age or position, will contribute towards realizing 21st century developmental goals of 
nation building.  
(iii) National Professional Standards for Teachers (NPST): National Professional Standards 
for Teachers (NPST), as envisioned in Para 5.20 of the National Education Policy (NEP) 2020, 
aims to enhance the personal and professional development of teachers by providing clear 
expectations and guidelines for performance improvement. It seeks to ensure that all students are 
taught by passionate, motivated, highly qualified, professionally trained and well-equipped 
teachers

SAMAGRA SHIKSHA ABHIYAN (SSA) 
 
 
8. The Committee observes that to achieve near-universal primary education, Government 
of India spent several years pursuing policies that focused on the goal and provided 
adequate resourcing to back it up. The Sarva Shiksha Abhiyan and the Right to Education 
Act, 2009 were notable interventions in this regard. While near-universal levels of primary 
education have been reached, there still remain 11.7 lakh children across the country who 
are Out of School (OoSc). The Committee is of the view that this is partly because there is 
no proper mechanism to ensure that these children are retained in school. Section 4 of the 
RTE Act, 2009 primarily focuses on a ‘curative/rehabilitative approach’ in the form of 
bridge courses, etc., after the child has dropped out for several months – there is no 
protocol for timely preventive action by authorities to retain children in schools. This 
absence of a preventive protocol is incompatible with the concept of a Fundamental Right. 
(Para 3.4.4) 
9. The goal of the Samagra Shiksha Abhiyan is to enable RTE implementation in state 
schools. However, the Committee is pained to note that 15 years after the RTE Act was 
passed, we have still not reached universal achievement of RTE norms by schools and 
teachers. In far too many schools, the pupil-teacher ratio, the number of classrooms, or the 

85 
 
availability of water and sanitation facilities and/or playgroundsis inadequate. The 
Committee notes the tremendous achievements of the Samagra Shiksha Abhiyan butcalls 
for a revamping of the scheme to function on mission mode. The mission must be to achieve 
near-saturation of the RTE’s requirements by 2029. The Finance Minister’s Budget 
announcement of achieving universal broadband connectivity for rural secondary schools 
within the year offers a model for the scale and timeline at which the Government should 
be operating at to upgrade the physical and human resources offered by schools. 
 
At the same time,it is not advisable to impose the RTE’s norms and requirements on 
schools without forethought.There are several schools, especially in underserved, remote, 
and Adivasi-majority areas, which may be unable to meet the formal requirements 
demanded by the RTE. Recently, the only Gondi-language school in Maharashtra, in 
Gadchiroli district, was forced to shut down since it did not have the requisite approval 
from the State Government as mandated by the RTE.Similar stories have emerged from 
ST dominant areas in Assam. Recognizing that the only feasible alternative to these schools 
is no school, the Department must relax RTE norms in such instances.Relaxation of RTE 
norms can only be done with a view to preserve schools – not with a view to consolidate 
them or to continue to under-resource them. 
(Para 3.4.5)

towards restoring the status of teachers is to ensure that all teachers have the same service 
conditions, regarding pay, in-service teacher-training, transfers and other benefits. The dual system 
of regular teachers and contractual teachers creates a system of different pay for equal work, which 
goes against the spirit of the constitutional guarantee of equal pay for equal work. The hiring of 
contractual teachers also undermines the Constitutional right of SCs, STs, and OBCs to reservation 
in public employment. The Committee notes that the NEP 2020, unlike the draft version released in 
2019, does not make any unequivocal statement on discontinuing the practice of hiring contract 
teachers. The Committee therefore recommends that the Department direct the utilisation of 

101 
 
Samagra Shiksha funds by states towards the hiring of regular teachers alone, or the regularisation 
of contractual teachers. The Department must develop a clear roadmap for transitioning 
contractual teachers into permanent positions within the next five years.States must also be

promotions. 
(Para 3.4.66)

teacher per grade. Thus, the Committee unequivocally advocates for a new policy of requiring one 
teacher per grade.

(i) Expansion of PM POSHAN to Secondary Education: PM POSHAN coverage 
should be extended to students upto Class 12th (upto the age of 18 years),

ensuring compliance with dietary standards and addressing reported issues 
in real-time.

children.  Provision of a glass of milk daily should be ensured under the

that around 40 to 50% of children in the country are stunted.

the meals under PM POSHAN on the line of recommendations by the ICMR.  
(vii) The Department should significantly hike the compensation paid to mid-day 
meal workers.

end of 2023, so that, NEP 2020 is implemented in letter and spirit". The Committee takes 
serious note of the casual approach of Department and CBSE with respect to 
recommendations of the Committee and directs the Department of School Education and 
CBSE to fill up the vacancies including backlog vacancies in mission mode and in 
timebound manner at the earliest and latest by the end 2025.   
(Para 5.2.7) 
99. The Committee observes that few private schools force students and their parents to 
purchase books, shoes, dresses etc. from specific stores recommended by those schools, on 
very higher prices. Moreover, these schools have no capping on fees and other charges 
collected from students. Private schools are running like commercial business instead of 
community service against the provisions of Clause 9.1.3 of Affiliation Bye Laws of the 
Board. The Committee, therefore, recommends the CBSE to monitor these malpractices in 
private schools affiliated to CBSE and ensure compliance of provisions of Clause 9.1.3 of 
Affiliation Bye Laws of the Board by these profit making schools. 
(Para 5.2.8) 
100. The Committee is expresses its concerns about the CBSE's draft policy for the 
conduct of two annual board examinations, which announces a planned enhancement of 
examination fees. It recommends that the examination fees not be hiked for all students, 
but only for those who are giving an additional attempt in the board examinations. 
Moreover, given CBSE’s considerable surplus revenues, there may be occasion to reduce 
the fees charged to students for exams.  
(Para 5.2.9) 
101. The Committee expresses its concerns about the draft policy’s notification that 
examination for remaining subjects will be conducted on 2 to 3 days. This offers potential 
for question paper leaks and other irregularities which must desperately be avoided. The 
Committee recommends further thought be given to the system to make it foolproof.  
(Para 5.2.10)

when there are huge numbers of teaching and non-teaching posts are lying vacant, is
