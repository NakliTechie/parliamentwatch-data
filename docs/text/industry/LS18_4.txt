FOURTH REPORT OF THE COMMITTEE 
I, the Chairman of the Department Related Parliamentary Standing Committee on 
Industry having been authorised by the Committee to present the report on its behalf, 
present this Fourth Report of the Committee on the status of Mini Steel Plants. 
2. The Committee on Industry deals with the subjects falling within the jurisdiction of 
the Ministries of Industry, Steel and Mines. 
3. The Committee at its meeting held on 15th June, 1993 identified 'Mini Steel Plants -
their status' as one of the subjects relating to the Ministry of Steel for its indepth study and 
report. The Committee directed the Secretariat to obtain a note on the subject for its 
consideration. 
4. The Committee held a general discussion on the existing status of Mini Steel Plants in 
the country on Monday, the 28th June, 1993 in the light of the note furnished by the 
Ministry. The Committee finalised a questionnaire for further clarifications by the Ministry 
on the subject. 
5. The Committee also heard the oral evidence of the Secretary of the Ministry on the 
subject on the 29th September, 1993. 
6. The Committee considered the subject in three sittings and considered and adopted 
the Report at its sitting held on 20th December, 1993.

availability of steel melting scrap , units were allowed to produce mild steel ignots to meet 
the demand of structural steel for construction purposes. At present, there are 177 Mini

Besides there are also a large number of small induction furnaces which were estabiished 
as small-scale units to manufacture castings but now are also producing mild steel ingots. 
The Government are, however, not encouraging manufacture of steel in induction furnaces 
as these units are of small size wherein it is not possible to adequately refine steel in such 
furnaces. These furnaces , however , need less power than electric arc furnaces. The n•Jmber 
of induction furnaces is not known but it is estimated between 400 to 700. Their estimated 
production during 1992-93 was estimated around 1.01 million tonnes. The working group on 
Iron and Steel Industry fo.- the 8th Five Year Plan has estimated that the production of 
crude steel by Mini Steel Plants will increase from about 4.00 million tonnes in 1991-92 to 
7.5 million tonnes in 1996-97 and 11 million tonnes in 1999-2000 AD. 
7.2 In the new industrial policy announced by the Government in July , 1991, the iron 
and steel industry has been removed from the list of industries reserved for the public sector 
and also exempted from compulsory licensing under the Industries (Development and 
Regulation) Act, 1951.

7.3 The broad characteristics of the electric arc furnaces in the country are: 
(a) majority of them are of small size and scattered all over the country. About 73 per 
cent have capacities upto 12 tonnes only, therefore, their modernisation and 
upgradation of technology is limited. 
(b) only about 27 per cent are of sizes 15 tonnes and above and only about 5 per cent 
are in the range between 20 to 40 tonnes and 20 percent have capacity above 40 
tonnes per year which can be upgraded by modem technologies for quality steel 
production at economic costs. The Secretary of the Ministry during the course of his

7.4 There have been considerable technological innovations and improvements in 
electric arc furnace steel making during the last twenty years which have resulted in 
reduction in power consumption, improvement in quality and yield. As many as 104 mini 
steel plants have adopted facilities like ultra high power transformer, secondary refining 
system, continuous casting machines etc.

per cent of the available cost of production. The availability and price of scrap are major 
factors affecting the operation of mini steel plants . The Govenment furnished the following 
data regarding indigenous availability of scrap and imports during the last five years as

Projected demand of melting Scrap/Sponge Iron for 1993-94 is 
estimated at 76 lakh tonnes. Indigenous availability of melting Scrap 
and Sponge Iron is expected to be around 30 lakh tonnes and 25 
lakh tonnes respectively. Balance requirement of 21 lakh tonnes is 
expected to be met through imports. 
The import of scrap has since been decanalised and freely allowed. The rate per tonne 
of imported scrap varie'S according to fluctuations in demand and supply in the international 
scrap market. The Ministry have stated that it' is, therefore, not possible to indicate a 
specific rate throughout the year. The scrap prices were around Us $ 135 per tonnes (C&F) 
from May/June, 1992 upto December, 1992. Thereafter the prices have increased sharply, 
rising upto US $ 180 per tonne in June, 1993. The prices have now slightly declined to 
around US $ 165 to 170 per tonne and it is expected that they will settle around US $ 160 per 
tonne. At this rate , landed cost is around Rs. 5935/- per tonne. Main sources of import, 
countries of origin-wise are USA, U . K., Belgium and Germany. 
7.6 In order to reduce dependence on imported scrap, Government has identifed the 
sponge iron industry as a thrust area for increased production. Indigenous capacity of 24.9 
lakh tonnes has already been created and a further capacity of about 33.5 lakh tonnes is 
under implementation. Clarifying the extent to which the scrap is made available from Ship 
Breaking and the projected availability of scrap from this source during the next five years, 
the Ministry have stated that the total capacity of the shipbreaking industry in the country is 
estimated to be 1.7 million LDT per year with about 1.5 million LDT capacity available at 
the Alang Shipbreaking Yard alone. The process of shipb,reaking generates oply about 
8-1()% of melting scrap for use in the electric arc furnace units. The bulk of the scrap 
generated (about 66-68%) is re-rollable scrap which is used by there-rolling mills to produce 
steel for the construction industry .• The current production is about 1 million LDT or 7 lakh 
tonnes of re-rollable scrap. This is expected to increase to about 1.5-2 million LDT in the 
next five year i.e. 1-1.4 million tonnes of re-rollable scrap. 
The mini steel plants are highly power intensive in their operations and heavily 
dependent on uninterrupted electricity supply to them. Shortage of electricity greatly affects 
the productivity and viability of the mini steel plants. The viability of operations of mini 
steel plants vary from State to State because their is no uniform electricity tariff policy. 
7.7 The Ministry while clarifying a specific point relating to the per unit cost of 
production of mini steel plant in the country and how does it compare with other countries 

and steps which are proposed to be taken to reduce it substantially during 8th plan informed 
that the average cost of production of 1 tonne of mild steel ingot is about Rs. 10,887/­
through EAF route, (assuming imported scrap at US$ 160 per tonne), though the exact cost 
varies from plant to plant. In the absence of any authentic information of the cost of 
production of steel in other countries, it is not possible to compare the cost of production of 
steel with that of other countries. However, cost of scrap and power which are the major 
inputs for Electric Arc Furnace units is generally lower in the developed countries. 
7.8 The Ministry informed the Committee that the steel industry has requested for 
various reliefs to improve their viability, since demand is sluggish and input costs have gone 
up. The budget proposals for 1993-94 contain measures aimed at reducing the capital and 
production costs of industrial projects including steel plants. These include lowering of 
customs duty on project imports and reduction of minimum mterest rate on commercial 
advances. Besides, reduction of customs duty on imported raw materials & consumable like 
pig iron, isrro-alloys, refractories, graphite electrodes etc. will also reduce the production 
costs of steel. More recently representations from the industry regarding reduction of import 
duty on scrap as well as reduction in excise duty on steel is under consideration. 
8.1 Taking into account the documents and other material made available to it, the 
Committee finds that the present installed capacity of mini steel plants is 73.12 lakh tonnes 
per annum whereas the actual production level is 34 lakh tonnes only. The Committee notes 
with regret that no concrete measures were taken by the Ministry for ensuring optimum use 
of the installed capacity. The country can not afford any alibi in the matter of production of 
steel during the last several years. The Committee is not convinced with the argument that 
the shortage of raw material and power are the only constraints in achieving the objectives. 
The Committee has been informed that out of 177 mini steel plants, 73 units are lying closed 
ranging from one year to ten years for various reasons and, therefore, going by the figures, 
it will be seen that 40 per cent installed capacity is not giving any production. 
The Committee, therefore, recommends that as a long term measure a technical 
evaluation group of the experts in the field may be constituted immediately to examine the 
problems of the mini steel plants in general and of the closed mini steel plants in particular. 
The Committee also suggests that short term ..remedial measures may also be introduced so 
that at least the installed capacity is made use of and the vast resources blocked in the closed 
units are generated for the larger good of the country. 
8.2 The Committee intends to emphasise that the mini steel plants play a dominant role 
in our economy. The construction sector is dependent on the mini steel plants for the iron 
which is an essential input for construction of houses, buildings, over-bridges, bridges, etc. 
The mini steel plants have however been left to fend for themselves and this sector has been 
neglected and has not been properly developed. The Committee is informed that the mini 
steel plants were brought under the licensing regime way back in 1973 in order to regulate 
the setting up of new units or expansion of the existing ones. But knowing fully well that 
majority of such plants were having capacities upto 12 tonnes only limiting the capacity of 
the plants to modernise and adopt modem technologies, such plants were not persuaded 
properly to increase their capacities to at least to 20 tonnes so that they could be modernised 
in due course. For the establishment of new plants permission should have been granted for 
large furnaces only keeping in view their future utility and modernisation. 
In the new Industrial Policy of 1991, the mini steel plants have been taken out from 
compulsory licensing schedule. Prior to that the mini steel plants were regulated for their 
modernisation, expansion and establishment. The Committee has not been informed that 
there was any system which could have motivated them for expansion on required lines 
where modernisation could be feasible. They were just allowed to expand without any future 

6 
planning. Their capacities were not taken into account while granting permission for their 
establishment or expansion. The money was just pumped into the plant without any 
consideration for future expansion. This is the picture, the Committee gets. Having come to 
this stage of having a large number of plants with a low capacity of 12 tonnes or below, their 
future and economic viability is doomed. They can neither expand nor modernise. They 
cannot become self supporting. They are a drain on the national resources. Government has 
not applied its mind to this problem. And all of a sudden in the new industrial Policy, the 
licensing has been taken away leaving them on their fate to the wiped out of the economic 
scene of the country. The Commitee records that this is not a very happy situation for a 
developing economy. The concern of the Ministry now at this stage should be to direct its 
attention to the basic question of the survival of these mini steel plants so that economic 
assets which are blocked into them start giving some return to the nation. It can be done if 
some scheme of their amalgamation or cluster type of management and strict monitoring are 
resorted to. This could be done, if Government could talk to the entrepreneurs of these mini 
steel plants on the methods for their survival. 
The Committee, therefore, recommends that the Government should give due priority to 
the survival of these mini steel plants and prepare a compre~ensive plan for their development 
which may include providing fmancial assistance to them for their expansion and modernisa­
tion, and also making raw material namely the scrap and power, available at reasonable rates 
to the mini steel plants. 
8.3 The mini steel plants are facing major problem in respect of steel melting scrap 
which is the main raw material input of these plants and the cost of scrap constitutes over 60 
per cent of the variable cost of production. The entire demand of scrap can not be met 
indigenously and about 3 million tonnes of scrap has to be imported every year which is too 
costly in view of steep rise in the prices of scrap ·in the international market. However during 
the course of his oral evidence the Secretary could not specify the concrete steps which are 
being taken or proposed to be taken by the Government to overcom~ this problem. 
The Commitee, therefore, recommends that concrete planning is needed to increase the 
indigenous availability of scrap to reduce dependence on imported scrap. Though the import 
of scrap has since been decanalised but it is causing hardships to those mini steel plants whose 
capacity is very low as they are unable to import the scrap due to paucity of funds and also 
due to less quantity required by them. The Committee recommends that efforts should be 
made by the Government to bring together these mini steel plants and import their requisite 
quota of scrap on their behalf and distribute the same among them at "no profit no loss" 
basis. 
In this connection the Committee would like to observe that in order to provide raw 
material to the mini steel plant sector, ship breaking industry could be exploited - though 
ship breaking industry is concentrated at Alang in Gujarat and some Japanese companies 
have shown keen interest in this industry. In view of the fact that single huiied ships in the 
country are now being phased out and having a long coastal line at our disposal, ship 
breaking industry could be developed. 
The Committee would, therefore, recommend that while encouraging ship breaking 
industry proper and adequate protection to the fiShing industry and environmental protection 
need to be taken care of fully to get raw material for the mini steel plants to the extent 
possible. The Committee is conscious of the fact that major portion of the scrap that comes 
out from the ship breaking industry goes directly to rolling mills but it will certainly help the 
mini steel sector also. 
8.4 The another problem being faced by the mini s"teel plants is power which is in short 
supply. Mini steel plants are highly power intensive and require uninterrupted power supply 

for their operations. Unfortunately projections indicate that most of the States will remain 
deficit in power even by the end of the 8th and 9th Five Year Plans. 
Since the shortage of power greatly affects the productivity and economic viability of mini 
steel plants the Committee recommends that Government should evolve a policy to assist the 
mini steel . plants to generate electricity for their consumption without depending upon 
Electricity Boards which can be done by way of providing low rate interest loans by fmancial 
institutions for installing mini power plants to be jointly shared by cluster of industries 
including mini steel plants. At the same time it is essential that States may also be advised to 
fix special rates of tariff for the mini steel sector. 
8.5 The Committee finds that there is no mini steel plant in the backward region of 
North Eastern States and only 33 units in the Eastern a.1d 30 units in the Southern region 
respectively. It clearly reflects that in the entire North Eastern region and most of the 
industrially backward States, the mini steel sector has not been properly developed inspite of 
various concessional packages. 
The Committee, therefore, recommends that Government should provide infrastructural 
development of all the backward States/areas so that mini steel plants are established in such 
States which may develop their economy as well as generate employment for the people, of the 
area. 
8.6 The Committee finds that infrastructure of closed mini steel plants is not being 
utilised in any manner. The machinery, raw material, buildings and land are lying unused. 
The machines are becoming junk and similar is the case of material lying therein. If there is 
no possibility of their revival, efforts should be made to utilise the infrastructure somewhere 
else in other units . 
The Committee recommends that Government should undertake a survey of those closed 
mini steel plants in the country which can not be revived, about their assets and report to 
both the Houses of Parliament with a clear cut picture and action proposed to be taken 
thereon at the earliest opportunity. 
8. 7 The Committee finds that in the case of 18 units which were closed recently, the 
major reason advanced has been the market condition and the high prices of scrap. It has 
already been pointed out that the estimated demand for scrap in the country is 7 million 
tonnes per annum and indigenous availability of scrap is about three million tonnes per 
annum thus leaving a gap of 4 million tonnes which has to be imported. The import is now 
freely allowed but the international prices of scrap have risen period from US $ 135 per 
tonne to US $ 180 per tonne. Apart from this high cost factor the Government is charging an 
import duty of 12.50 per cent on the scrap. The Committee is of the opinion that this has 
certainly escalated the production costs of the tbe mini steel plants resulting in the closure of 
mini steel plants. The mini steel plants despite of raising the prices of their produce very 
frequently are not economically viable. The Committee appreciates that the import duty on 
the scrap was brought down from 35 per cent to 12.50 per cent but for the survival of the 
mini steel plants this is not enough. The prices of scrap in the international market is really 
on the high side. 
The Committee, therefore, recommends that the import duty on scrap be brought down 
considerably so as to enable the mini steel plants or Electric Arc Furnaces to survive. 
8.8 The mini steel plants generally produce ingots/billets or long products such as bars 
and rounds which are mainly used for construction purposes. In the General Budget 1993-94 
the excise duties on the products of mini steel plants was raised from Rs. 29()1.. to Rs. 100()1.. 
per tonne. This has further deepened the crisis in the mini steel plants industry which has 
been hard hit by the high price of scrap abroad and import duty on scrap. The increase in 
the excise duty has certainly affected the viability of mini steel plants. 

The Committee; therefore, recommends that the excise duty on the products of mini steel 
plants be lowered considerably so that the industry could survive. The Committee feels tltat 
since the excise duty on sponge iron is quite higher than that of scrap the mini steel plants 
cannot make use of sponge iron as a raw material at reasonable prices. The Committee, 
therefore, recommends that the issue of excise duty on sponge iron and scrap be examined 
together with a view to maintain a rationale. 
8.9 The main raw material input for the mini steel plants is steel melting scrap. Present 
requirement of mini steel plants is 7 million tonnes of scrap whereas indigenous availability 
of scrap is about 3 million tonnes. Sponge iron is a partial substitute for scrap. The 
Committee appreciates that some efforts are being made by the Government to increase the 
availability of sponge iron. 
The Committee, however, recommends that much more thrust is required to be made to 
increase the production of sponge iron in the country for the benefit of mini steel plants. 
8.10 At the .:!nd, the Committee notes with concern that more than ten thousand 
workers have been rendered jobless due to the closure of mini steel plants in various States 
and Union Territories in the country particularly in the States of Andhra Pradesh, Gujarat, 
Kerala, Maharashtra, Uttar Pradesh and West Bengal and no definite proposals for their 
rehabilitation have been placed before the Committee by the Government. It appears that 
the hapless workers of the closed mini steel plants have been left to fend for themselves. 
The Committee, therefore, recommends that Government should immediately undertake 
rehabilitation measures for such workers of the closed mini steel plants by either providing 
alternative jobs to them or giving them adequate help to enable them to gain self employment 
at the earliest. According to the Committee, the rehabilitation measures should specifically 
provide for training to the workers suited to their skill and potential so that they could utilise 
such training while opting for a suitable job somewhere else. 
8.11 The Committee, in general, has noted that the steel sector as a whole is in 
doldrum with a number of plants either closed or at the brink of closure due to continuous 
decline in their viability. In such continuing trend, privatisation of certain plants by 
Government particularly that of liSCO , Burnpur is on anvil. 
The Committee expresses its deep concern over the situation and impresses upon 
Government to explore all possible ways to revive the liSCO and other sick steel plants. 
8.12 The Committee trusts that the observations and recommendation s as mentioned 
above will receive serious consideration for implementation.
