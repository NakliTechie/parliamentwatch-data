CHAPTER 1: OVERVIEW OF THE MINISTRY 
BACKGROUND OF THE MSME SECTOR 
1.1 Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises (MSME) form a vital pillar of India's economy 
by generating employment, supporting exports and enabling decentralized industrialisation. 
As per the Economic Survey 2025 –26, MSMEs account for about 31.1 per cent of India‘s 
Gross Domestic Product (GDP), 35.4 per cent of manufacturing output and 48.58 per cent of 
India‘s exports, and employ around 32.82 crore persons. The Ministry's digital registration 
platforms, Udyam Registration Portal and Udyam Assist Platform, re cord the universe of 
registered enterprises and are the primary administrative source for scheme eligibility and 
outreach. As per the Ministry‘s Background Note, more than 7.69 crore enterprises have 
registered on the Udyam Registration Portal and Udyam Assist Platform. 
1.2 The Ministry‘s programmes are designed to support MSMEs as ancillary units to 
larger industry, strengthen supply chains, and promote regional and social inclusion through 
finance, technology, market access, skilling and infrastructure interventions. 
VISION AND POLICY APPROACH 
1.3 Vision: Sustainable development of globally competitive MSMEs as an engine of 
growth for the Indian economy. 
1.4 The Ministry pursues an integrated policy approach addressing constraints across the 
enterprise lifecycle. Principal strategic priorities and their rationale are: 
 Formalisation and Inclusion. Expand Udyam registration and simplify compliance so 
informal micro -enterprises can access credit, procurement, subsidies and social 
protection. Formalisation impr oves targeting of training, Direct Benefit Transfer 
(DBT) payments and infrastructure support. 
 Access to Credit and Finance. Strengthen availability and affordability of credit 
through bank lending, credit guarantee mechanisms and equity instruments. 
Instruments such as the Credit Guarantee Trust for Micro and Small Enterprises 
(CGTMSE) reduce collateral constraints, while the Self Reliant India (SRI) Fund (a 
Fund of Funds) catalyses equity investment into higher-growth MSMEs. 
 Access to Market. Expand publi c procurement opportunities for Micro and Small 
Enterprises (MSEs), accelerate onboarding on the Government e -Marketplace (GeM) 
and support export promotion and international cooperation to diversify market 
linkages. 
 Industry-grade Skilling. Deliver Nation al Skills Qualifications Framework 
(NSQF)-aligned training through Technology Centres, the National Institute for 
Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises (ni -msme), the Khadi and Village Industries 
Commission (KVIC) and the National Small Industries Corporatio n (NSIC) to 
improve employability and enterprise capabilities. 

10 
 
 Infrastructure and Cluster Development. Establish Common Facility Centres (CFCs), 
cluster development programmes and targeted infrastructure in the North -Eastern 
Region (NER) and aspirational districts to raise productivity and reduce unit costs. 
 Technology, Innovation and Green Transition. Scale Technology Centres, Tool 
Rooms and the Technology Centre Systems Programme (TCSP) to accelerate 
adoption of advanced manufacturing, quality testing, de sign and environmentally 
sustainable processes. 
 Digital Governance and Data -driven Policy. Strengthen multilingual portals, 
geo-tagging of assets, performance dashboards and data analytics to improve 
transparency, monitoring and evidence based decision making. 
1.5 Each priority is implemented through flagship schemes, statutory bodies and 
partnerships with State Governments, financial institutions and industry associations. 
MANDATE AND LEGAL FRAMEWORK 
1.6 The Ministry of Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises was formed in 2007 to 
coordinate policy, planning and implementation for MSME, khadi, village and coir 
industries. The Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises Development (MSMED) Act, 2006 
provides the statutory framework for recognition, promotion, developmen t and protection of 
MSMEs, including provisions for preferential procurement, credit facilitation and redressal of 
delayed payments through Micro and Small Enterprises Facilitation Councils (MSEFCs). 
1.7 With effect from 1 April 2025, the Ministry revised the classification criteria for 
enterprises to enable scaling and improved access to resources. The revised thresholds are:

1.8 Sector-specific statutes such as the Khadi and Village Industries Commission Act, 
1956 and the Coir Industry Act, 1953 continue to govern traditional and rural industries.

2.2 The Committee notes with serious concern that while out of eight Budget 
announcements, only two — both led by the Ministry of MSME itself — have been 
operationalised. The remaining six, where the Ministry is a supporting agency, continue 
to languish at the stage of draft guidelines, inter -ministerial consultations, or 
preliminary deliberations without clearly defined or publicly disclosed rollout timelines. 
This pattern raises fundamental questions about the effectiveness of inter -ministerial 
coordination mechanisms and the Ministry's ability to influence outcomes where it is 
not the lead agency. 
2.3 The Committee further observes that two announcements of particular 
significance to the MSME ecosystem — credit cards for micro enterprises and the 
scheme for first -time entrepreneurs (women and SC/ST) — remain unimplemented 
nearly twelve months after their announcement. The credit card initiative, intended to 
reach ten lakh micro enterprises in the first year, has not progressed beyond the draft 
stage. The first-time entrepreneur scheme, which carries important equity and inclusion 
objectives, remains at the EFC memorandum stage. Taken together, these delays 
indicate a serious disconnect between the policy intent articulated in the Budget speech 
and the administrative machinery required for last-mile delivery. 
2.4 In view of the abov e, the Committee strongly recommends that a time -bound 
implementation framework be put in place for all Budget announcements concerning 
the MSME ecosystem, irrespective of whether the Ministry of MSME is the lead or a 
supporting Ministry. In particular, for the credit card scheme for micro enterprises and 
the term -loan scheme for first -time women and SC/ST entrepreneurs, the Committee 
urges the concerned Ministries to work in close coordination to finalise scheme design, 
obtain requisite approvals and opera tionalise these initiatives within a clearly defined 
outer limit, preferably not later than the first half of FY 2026 -27. The Committee 
further recommends that progress on all eight announcements be monitored through a 
quarterly, inter‑ministerial review m echanism, anchored by the nodal 
Ministries/Departments concerned with active participation of the Ministry of MSME, 
with milestones, responsible agencies  and timelines clearly specified. A  consolidated 
implementation status be compiled and shared with the Committee at regular intervals 
so that the credibility of Budget announcements is reinforced by demonstrable, 
time‑bound outcomes on the ground.

2.6 The Committee notes with serious concern that out of the 7.61 crore MSMEs 
registered on the Udyam portal [including Informal Micro Enterprises on Udyam Assist 
Platform (UAP) as on 31 January 2026], an overwhelming 7.56 crore (99.3%) fall under 
the micro category, while only about 4.88 lakh are classified as small and approximately 
36,816 as medium enterprises. This highly skewed distribution clearly indicates that a 
vast majority of enterprises operate at extremely small scales and are likely to have 
significantly different financial capacities, risk profiles, and support requirements 
compared to relatively larger micro units. 
2.7 The Committee reiterates that the upward revision of investment and turnover 
limits has the unintended consequence of allowing co mparatively larger enterprises to 
remain within the micro segment, thereby risking dilution of the original intent of the 
MSME framework and constraining access to benefits for genuinely small and 
household-level enterprises. 
2.8 The Committee recalls that  in its 327 th Report it had specifically recommended 
rationalisation of the MSME definitions and the creation of a separate Nano enterprise  
category; however, no tangible action has been taken by the Ministry so far. The 
Committee finds this inaction particularly concerning in light of emerging evidence and 
policy developments. In this regard, the Committee notes that the approach 
recommended by it has already been operationalised by the Government of Kerala, 
which has recognised Nano Enterprises as a disti nct segment for units with investment 
up to ₹10 lakh and has introduced targeted schemes such as interest subvention, margin 
money assistance, and household enterprise promotion to address the specific needs of 
ultra-small entrepreneurs. This State -level e xperience demonstrates both the 
administrative feasibility and developmental necessity of such differentiation. 
2.9 In view of the foregoing, the Committee strongly reiterates its earlier 
recommendation and urges the Ministry to undertake, without further delay, a 
comprehensive review of the MSME classification framework and introduce a separate 
Nano enterprise category with an appropriate threshold around ₹10 lakh investment. 
The Committee expects the Ministry to furnish a concrete and time -bound action pl an, 
along with an assessment of the likely impact on credit access, employment generation, 
and inclusion, so as to ensure that policy support reaches the most vulnerable and 
genuinely small enterprises. 
Other Announcements: Credit Access and Inclusion Gaps 
2.10 From a perusal of the submissions of the Ministry, t he Committee further notes that 
despite enhancement of credit guarantee cover, access to institutional credit at the ground 
level remains constrained. Micro enterprises, first -time entrepreneurs and  women-led units 
continue to face informal collateral requirements, unexplained loan rejections and prolonged 
processing timelines at bank branches. This concern is compounded by the fact that the 
Budget announcement regarding credit cards for micro enterp rises continues to remain 
unimplemented and confined to the stage of draft guidelines and consultations. 

17 
 
2.11 The Committee also observes serious delays in the operationalisation of 
inclusion- and employment -oriented Budget announcements, particularly the scheme 
for first -time entrepreneurs for women and SC/ST beneficiaries and the proposed 
logistics and service delivery support to MSMEs through India Post. These 
announcements continue to remain under inter -ministerial consultation without clearly 
defined or publicly disclosed rollout timelines. 
2.12 The Committee is further concerned that continued reliance on standalone credit 
interventions, without integration of technology upgradation, market access and enterprise 
capability-building support, limits the effectiveness of these measures and risks increasing 
MSME indebtedness rather than improving competitiveness. 
2.13 The Committee therefore recommends: 
a. All pending MSME -related Budget announcements be operationalised within a 
clearly defined and publicly no tified timeline, not exceeding six months from the 
date of the Budget presentation. 
b. The Ministry be formally designated as the nodal authority for all MSME credit-
related Budget announcements and be empowered to enforce bank -level 
accountability. 
c. The Minis try should mandate written reasons for rejection of MSME loan 
applications covered under credit guarantee schemes, prescribe time -bound 
grievance redressal mechanisms, and recommend penal action against lending 
institutions for repeated non-compliance. 
d. No MSME credit scheme be approved or reported as "implemented" unless it 
incorporates mandatory components for technology upgradation, market 
linkage, and enterprise capability enhancement. 
IMPACT OF US TARIFFS ON MSME SECTOR 
Background 
2.14 During the year 2 025, the United States  (US) introduced a series of trade measures, 
including tariff increases, safeguard actions and stricter trade -remedy investigations such as 
anti-dumping and countervailing duties. Effective from 27 August 2025, the US imposed 
tariffs totaling approximately 50% on Indian exports, combining a 10% baseline duty with a 
25% reciprocal tariff and sector -specific additions. The Committee through its various 
interactions with a wide range of stakeholders noted that t hese measures affected seve ral 
export-oriented sectors where Indian MSMEs have significant participation, notably textiles 
and apparel, engineering goods, hand tools, chemicals, auto components, and select 
manufacturing segments. 
2.15 Given that the United States remains India‘s lar gest export destination, these 
unilateral tariff hikes have direct and immediate implications for the competitiveness, export 
viability and employment potential of Indian MSMEs. MSME -intensive sectors across 

18 
 
manufacturing and services are expected to face order compression, margin pressure and 
heightened employment risks. Export -oriented MSMEs supplying intermediate goods, 
components, light engineering products, textiles and garments, leather and footwear, and a 
broad range of fabricated and assembled items are already experiencing a discernible squeeze 
on volumes and profitability, with likely second -round effects on subcontracting chains and 
informal employment. 
Government Response and Mitigation Measures 
2.16 The Ministry during its oral submission on the  subject before the Committee on 
29.10.2025 informed that the Government of India adopted a multi -pronged approach to 
mitigate the adverse impact of these trade measures, including bilateral trade discussions, 
efforts to promote export market diversificati on, policy support through existing credit 
facilitation and guarantee schemes, technology upgradation initiatives, and cluster -based 
development programmes. However, the Committee observes that the Ministry's response has 
been largely reactive and does not  reflect a sector -specific, proactive strategy for MSME -
intensive export clusters. 
Committee's Assessment and Recommendations 
2.17 Based on its examination and deliberations with key stakeholders — including 
representatives of the Ministry of MSME, the Min istry of Textiles, the Ministry of 
Commerce and Industry, as well as industry associations and export -oriented MSME 
representatives — the Committee observes the following: 
 The MSME sector's high dependence on a limited number of export markets exposes 
it to significant vulnerability from external trade policy shocks. 
 MSMEs generally lack the institutional capacity, legal resources and market 
intelligence required to effectively respond to foreign trade -remedy actions such as 
anti-dumping and countervailing duty investigations. 
 Market diversification efforts remain uneven across sectors and regions, and 
coordination among Ministries dealing with trade, industry, textiles and MSMEs 
requires further strengthening. 
 The Procurement and Marketing Support (PMS) all ocation has remained stagnant at 
₹65 crore despite the critical need for export promotion support, with the Ministry 
informing the Committee that "No budgetary constraint has been experienced". The 
International Cooperation scheme spent only ₹14.43 crore a gainst ₹19.07 crore RE in 
FY 2025 -26, with only 1,612 MSMEs benefitted under IC over the entire last five 
years. These allocations are manifestly inadequate given the scale and urgency of the 
tariff challenge. 
 Total infrastructure allocation remained near -stagnant at ₹3,131.04 crore (BE 2026 -
27) versus ₹3,156 crore (BE 2025 -26), despite heightened need to strengthen MSME 
quality, productivity and competitiveness post-August 2025 tariff imposition. 

19 
 
2.18 The Committee, accordingly, strongly recommends: 
a. The Government intensify efforts to diversify export destinations for MSME 
products to reduce over -dependence on a few major markets and minimise 
concentration risk. 
b. Establishment of a dedicated MSME Trade Defence and Support Mechanism to 
provide legal, te chnical and advisory assistance in dealing with foreign tariff 
actions and trade-remedy investigations. 
c. Design and operationalise an MSME Tariff Resilience Package within RAMP 
and PMS, with components for targeted working -capital support, subsidised 
testing/certification/quality upgradation, and dedicated support for market 
diversification towards EU, ASEAN, West Asia and Africa. 
d. Creation of targeted contingency credit lines or interest subvention mechanisms 
for MSMEs in affected clusters facing immediate f inancial stress from sudden 
tariff changes. 
e. Development of an early -warning and real -time monitoring system to track 
global trade policy developments impacting MSMEs, enabling proactive policy 
intervention. 
f. Structured inter -ministerial coordination among t he Ministries of Commerce, 
MSME, Textiles and Finance be institutionalised to ensure a unified, timely and 
effective response to future external trade disruptions.

Structural Barriers and Ground Assessment 
2.48 During t he Committee's study visits , the committee had several interactions with 
CPSEs confirm ing that compliance with sub -targets for SC/ST and women -owned MSEs 
remains persistentl y weak — often below 2 per cent in several individual CPSEs. Key

inadequate utilisation of the L1 price -matching provision by procuring officers; platform -

tools; absence of a centralised, state -wise vendor directory; and fragment ed data governance 
arising from manual reporting on the Sambandh Portal and its incomplete integration with 
GeM. Vendor Development Programmes (VDPs), though numerically significant — 4,310 
general-category and 1,059 SC/ST -specific VDPs conducted in FY 202 4–25 — lack post -

conversion of VDP participation into actual procurement outcomes. 
2.49 In the light of the information gathered by the Committee, it strongly 
recommends that the Ministry, in coordination with the Department of Public 
Enterprises and procuring CPSEs, put in place a time -bound and enforceable 
mechanism to achieve the mandated sub -targets for SC/ST -owned and women -owned

deductions. The Committee further recommends targeted vendor de velopment drives 
exclusively for SC/ST and women -owned MSEs in technical and high -value sectors, 
with mandatory handholding for GeM onboarding and tender participation, and 
institutionalised post-VDP mentoring with structured vendor -to-buyer matching. Time-
bound integration of the MSME Sambandh Portal with GeM and creation of a 
centralised national vendor directory should be completed within a defined timeline to 
enable real-time accountability and evidence-based procurement planning.

2.50 The Committee notes the Ministry‘s submission that there is presently no 
provision for monitoring procurement from MSEs owned by Other Backward Classes

category may increase compliance burden and create overlaps with existing SC/ST and 
women sub-targets. The Committee, while conscious of these constraints, recommends 
that the Government examine the legal and administrative feasibility of tracking, and 
where appropriate  incentivising, procurement from OBC -owned MSEs, without 
diluting the focus on achieving the existing sub -targets for SC/ST - and women-owned 
MSEs.

MSME sector. The report notes that by FY2021 on ly about 19 per cent of the estimated 
MSME credit demand of nearly ₹99 lakh crore was met through formal sources, leaving an 
unmet demand of roughly ₹80 lakh crore. A subsequent study by the Small Industries 
Development Bank of India (SIDBI) titled Underst anding the Indian MSME Sector: Progress 
and Challenges (May 2025) estimates the addressable credit gap at around ₹30 lakh crore, 
equivalent to about 24 per cent of total demand, with higher gaps in the services sector 
(around 27 per cent) and among women -owned MSMEs (about 35 per cent). Comparative 
industry assessments, including analyses by Ernst & Young, indicate that MSME credit 
penetration in India is around 14 per cent, significantly lower than about 37 per cent in China 
and around 50 per cent in the U nited States, underscoring the scale of the financing 
challenge. Meanwhile, the Credit Guarantee Fund Trust for Micro and Small Enterprises 
(CGTMSE) approved guarantees of nearly ₹2 lakh crore in FY2024, and its guarantee ceiling 
has been enhanced to ₹10 c rore from April 2025, though its corpus of about ₹16,500 crore 
requires careful monitoring as commitments expand. Structural imbalances also persist, with 
micro enterprises accounting for about 97 per cent of MSMEs, small enterprises around 2.7 
per cent, and medium enterprises about 0.3 per cent, although the latter contribute nearly 40 
per cent of MSME exports, reflecting the sector‘s ―missing middle‖. On the skills front, data 
from the National Statistical Office Periodic Labour Force Survey (2023 –24) show that only 
about 4.1 per cent of persons aged 15 –59 have received formal vocational training, while 
around 65 per cent have received no vocational training, and technology adoption remains 
limited, with industry studies suggesting AI adoption among Indian  MSMEs remains below 
15 per cent compared with around 35–40 per cent in advanced economies. 
Key Recommendations of the NITI Aayog‘s report 
2.58 The report recommends: 
i. bringing CGTMSE under a regulatory authority and raising guarantee coverage to 
100% for women-led units; 
ii. reducing CGTMSE premium rates and mandating transparency on bank websites; 
iii. scaling SIDBI's balance sheet to provide wholesale financing to NBFCs and 
reclassifying bank-to-NBFC lending as indirect MSME priority-sector finance; 

31 
 
iv. creating clus ter-based, sector -specific occupational standards with cost -sharing 
models for training; 
v. converting Technology Centres into SME 4.0 Competence Centres; 
vi. establishing a dedicated R&D cell within the MSME Ministry; 
vii. upgrading the Udyam portal with cluster -wise, state-level data for evidence-based 
policymaking; and 
viii. introducing digital risk management and insurance solutions, particularly sensor -
based supply chain tracking for food processing and logistics. 
2.59 Taking the Report of NITI Aayog on ‗Enhancing MSMEs ' Competitiveness in 
India‘ forward, the Committee recommends that the Ministry formally adopt a 
competitiveness framework aligned with NITI Aayog's recommendations, mapping 
each major scheme to the specific pillars of financing, skills, technology adoptio n and 
market access. In view of the structural ―missing middle‖, wherein medium enterprises 
account for only a small share of MSME units but contribute a disproportionately high 
share of MSME exports, a dedicated Medium Enterprise Window within RAMP and 
the SRI Fund should be designed and operationalised for Industry 4.0 adoption, R&D 
collaboration and deeper integration into global value chains. Cluster -based testing and 
certification facilities under MSE -CDP and Technology Centres should be accelerated 
through a time -bound action plan, prioritising sectors and clusters with high export 
potential identified in the NITI Aayog report.

CREDIT GUARANTEE SUPPORT: CGTMSE 
3.6 The Credit Guarantee Scheme for Micro and Small Enterprises, operated through the 
Credit Guarantee Fund Trust for Micro and Small Enterprises (CGTMSE), provides 
collateral‑free a nd third‑party‑guarantee‑free loans to micro and small enterprises through 
MLIs. Guarantee cover ranges from 75 –90%, with 90% for women‑led units, and 
concessional guarantee fees for SC/ST, OBC, PwD, Agniveers, Transgenders, ZED‑certified 
units, aspiration al districts and the North‑Eastern Region; medium enterprises are not 
presently covered. 
3.7 The guarantee ceiling has been raised to ₹10 crore per borrower from 1 April 2025. 
Since inception in FY 2001 up to 31.12.2025, the scheme has extended 1.35 crore guarantees 
amounting to ₹12.39 lakh crore, with substantial coverage of women and vulnerable social 
categories and reasonable performance in claim settlement. 
3.8 The Committee observes that charts on guarantees approved under the scheme capture 
only the scale of approvals and amounts under guarantee. While this reflects expanded risk 
coverage and lender participation, it remains largely input‑ and output‑oriented and does not 
establish whether guaranteed credit has led to productive utilisation, enterprise sustainability, 
employment generation or improved repayment. 
3.9 During its deliberations in Kolkata in November 2025, the Committee interacted with 
MSME entrepreneurs, industry associa tions, bankers, State Government and the Ministry 
officials, and reviewed case studies of both beneficiary and non‑beneficiary enterprises. 
Based on this ground‑level examination, the Committee observed a perceptible gap between 
policy intent and implementation. CGTMSE has expanded significantly in scale and coverage 
in FY 2023–25, however: 
 Collateral-free lending objectives are diluted by continued collateral from Banks; 
 Implementation is inconsistent across regions and branches, with weak enforcement and 
limited accountability; 

34 
 
 Effective cost of borrowing is high due to guarantee fees, bank charges and insurance 
premiums, often levied on sanctioned rather than outstanding amounts; 
 Excessive reliance on CIBIL scores excludes youth and  first‑generation entrepreneurs 
despite viable proposals; 
 Awareness of CGTMSE is limited in rural/semi‑urban areas; bank outreach focuses 
more on retail products than MSME credit schemes; and  
 Uniform, publicly available data on applications, sanctions, re jections, sectors, 
employment and start‑ups are lacking, even as some States (Telangana, West Bengal, 
Uttar Pradesh) demonstrate better convergence models. 
3.10 In view of the above, the Committee strongly recommends that the Ministr y 
urgently shift the assessment of the Credit Guarantee Scheme from a mere approval‑ 
and disbursement‑driven framework to a results‑ and impact‑based monitoring system. 
The Ministry should mandatorily track and place before the Committee 
beneficiary‑level outcomes, including enterprise survival beyond one and three years, 
employment generated or sustained, turnover growth, repayment and NPA trends, and 
graduation of units from micro to small categories, with disaggregation by social 
category and region. 
3.11 The Committee further recommends strict enforcement of collateral‑free lending 
norms under CGTMSE. Banks must not dilute the intent of the scheme by demanding 
collateral or third‑party guarantees. Any deviation from scheme guid elines should 
require mandatory written justification, and repeated violations at the branch level 
should attract deterrent penalties. The Committee also recommends rationalisation of 
guarantee fees, bank charges and insurance practices, including limiting  insurance 
coverage strictly to the outstanding loan balance, in order to reduce the effective cost of 
borrowing for micro and small enterprises. 
3.12 The Committee also recommends comprehensive reform of credit appraisal 
practices u nder CGTMSE. Excessive reliance on CIBIL scores should be curtailed, 
particularly for first-generation entrepreneurs and youth, and alternative assessment 
parameters based on business viability, cash flow and local economic conditions should 
be institutionalised. To address persistent awareness gaps, the Committee recommends 
mandatory outreach targets for banks in rural and semi‑urban areas, supported by 
structured handholding through MSME Development Institutes and State‑level 
agencies. 
3.13 The Committee further recommends strengthening transparency, monitoring 
and institutional coordination. A comprehensive public dashboard for CGTMSE should 
be created, providing scheme‑wise data on applications, sanctions, rejections, sectoral 
coverage and employment outcomes. Successful State‑level MSME convergence models 
should be replicated nationwide, and structured coordination among Central 
Ministries, State Governments, banks and MSME support institutions should be 

35 
 
institutionalised to ensure  uniform implementation and long‑term resilience of the 
MSME credit ecosystem.

3.19 Based on extensive interactions during study -visits with beneficiaries, banks, and 
implementing agencies, the Committee‘s on -ground assessment indicates that PMEGP is

recommends a comprehensive recalibration of PMEGP to restore it s relevance,

inclusiveness and employment‑generating potential. The Committee strongly

persistently high bank rejection rates, the Committee urges the Ministry to issue

or partial credit‑guarantee mechanisms to realign lending behaviour with the scheme‘s

Programmes by restoring adequate duration, strengthen ing physical training 
components and linking EDP completion with structured post‑sanction handholding, 
mentoring and market‑linkage support. In parallel, the Committee calls for urgent 
augmentation of manpower and field capacity in KVIC, DICs and implement ing offices 
to ensure effective outreach, appraisal and monitoring. Finally, the Committee

with dedicated support for high‑potential activities such as oil and gas services in Assam 
and agarwood‑based enterprises in the North‑East, through coordinated policy backing, 
technical assistance and market linkage, to maximise PMEGP‘s developmental impact. 
3.24 Further, in view of high demand, strong historical utilisation (around 98 per cent 
of the approved outlay under the current EFC cycle in the case of PMEGP) and the 
critical role of these schemes in supporting first -generation and vulnerable 
entrepreneurs, the Committee recommends that, subject to the overall fiscal 
consolidation path and resource availability, the Government consider higher 
allocations for PMEGP and PM Vishwakarma in forthcoming Budget exercises. Within 
the envelope approved, the Committee further urges the Ministry to give clear

COIR BOARD 
5.11 The Coir Board  is a statutory body set up under the Coir Industry Act, 1953 to 
promote the development of the coir sector and improve the livelihoods of workers engaged 
in this traditional industry. Headquartered in Kochi, it supports research, production, 
regulation, and export promotion of coir products through its nationwide establishments. 
5.12 India is the world‘s largest producer of coir, contributing over 70% of global output, 
and the sector provides employment to about 7 lakh people, nearly 80% of who are rural 
women. 
5.13 Originally concentrated in Kerala, the industry has now expanded to several coconut -
producing states such as Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Odisha, West Bengal, 
Maharashtra, Assam, and Tripura, and has strong potential for growth through  value addition 
and technology upgradation. 
5.14 The Coir Board  implements the umbrella scheme Coir Vikas Yojana (CVY) to 
promote the sustainable growth of the coir sector and improve the livelihoods of coir artisans 
across the country. Approved for the pe riod FY 2021 –22 to FY 2025 –26 with an outlay of 
₹466.60 crore, the scheme comprises key components including Science and Technology, 
Skill Upgradation and Mahila Coir Yojana, and Market Promotion (export and domestic). 
5.15 Under the Science and Technology  component, research institutes such as CCRI, 
Alappuzha and CICT, Bengaluru undertake R&D for modernization, product diversification, 
eco-friendly technologies, and technology transfer, with several innovations developed in 
machinery, blended fabrics, and coir-based products. Skill development initiatives focus on 
artisan training, entrepreneurship development, and women empowerment through Mahila 
Coir Yojana, under which women artisans receive stipendiary training in motorized yarn 
spinning. During the rep orting period, over 1,400 artisans were trained across various 
programmes. 
5.16 The Board also undertakes Export Market Promotion by facilitating participation of 
MSMEs in international exhibitions, registering exporters, providing Market Development 
Assistance, and promoting coir as a sustainable natural fibre globally. Simultaneously, 
Domestic Market Promotion activities are carried out through showrooms, exhibitions, 
publicity campaigns, and participation in major events such as industrial expos and trade fairs 
to enhance awareness and sales of coir products within the country.

NATIONAL SMALL INDUSTRIES CORPORATION (NSIC) 
5.21     The National Small Industries Corporation Ltd. (NSIC), a Government of India 
enterprise under the Ministry of Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises, has been a key 
institutional pillar in the promotion and development of Micro, Small and Medium 
Enterprises across the cou ntry. The Corporation has been entrusted with the mandate of 
providing integrated support services in the areas of marketing, finance, technology and skill 
development, with a particular focus on enhancing MSME competitiveness, facilitating 
access to publi c procurement and fostering entrepreneurship among socially and 
economically disadvantaged groups. 
5.22     NSIC operates through an extensive nationwide institutional network comprising 101 
field offices, including 15 National SC/ST Hub Offices (NSSHOs), along with multiple 
Technical Service Centres and training -cum-incubation facilities. Through this network, 
NSIC delivers a range of services aimed at addressing critical constraints faced by MSMEs, 
particularly those relating to market access, technology adoption and capacity building. 
5.23     The Corporation provides integrated services through its field offices and eight 
National Technical Service Centres (NTSCs). Its major interventions include raw material 
distribution, credit facilitation and bill discounting, Single Point Registration for participation 
in Government procurement, and digital market access through the MSME Global Mart 
platform. During the year 2025, NSIC distributed 3, 64,374.60 metric tonnes of raw materials 
valued at ₹3,089.13 crore, indicating sustained demand for its input -support services. The 
MSME Global Mart platform onboarded 10,905 MSMEs during the year, thereby expanding 
digital market access for small enterprises. NSIC has also placed significant emphasis on skill 
development and technology transfer through its NTSCs, training 86,098 trainees in 2025 and 
providing MSMEs access to advanced manufacturing technologies such as CNC machining, 
CAD/CAM, 3D printing and emergin g applications in artificial intelligence and machine 
learning.

5.24     In furtherance of national priorities on social inclusion and equitable growth, NSIC 
implements the National SC/ST Hub (NSSH) Scheme, launched in 2016 to empower SC/ST 
entrepreneurs t hrough capacity building, market linkage and facilitation of participation in 
public procurement. NSIC also plays a central role in facilitating equity funding for 
high-potential MSMEs under the Self Reliant India (SRI) Fund, a ₹50,000 crore Fund of 
Funds initiative leveraging Government and private capital. Equity support under the SRI 
Fund is being channeled through 69 empanelled Daughter Funds under NSIC Venture Capital 
Fund Limited (as of October 2025). The Committee notes that NSIC‘s investment facilit ation 
activities are managed entirely through Internal and Extra -Budgetary Resources (IEBR), 
reflecting the Corporation‘s financial stability and operational maturity. During FY 2024 -25, 
NSIC recorded revenue of ₹3,431 crore, profit after tax (PAT) of ₹146 .30 crore (up 15.6% 
YoY) and paid its highest -ever dividend of ₹43.89 crore to the Government. The recent 
upgradation of NSIC from Schedule ‗B‘ to Schedule ‗A‘ Central Public Sector Enterprise 
(announced February 2026) further underscores its strategic imp ortance within the MSME 
ecosystem. 
5.25     The Committee notes that NSIC has demonstrated strong institutional capacity 
and operational performance through its nationwide network, integrated service 
delivery model and consistent financial results. However , the Committee is of the 
considered view that the scale of financing and growth capital required by the MSME 
sector—particularly for first -generation entrepreneurs, technology -intensive 
enterprises, export-oriented units and enterprises located in aspirational or underserved 
regions—far exceeds the present resource envelope available with NSIC. The 
Committee observes that the absence of a dedicated, large -scale growth financing 
mechanism significantly constrains the Corporation‘s ability to catalyse privat e 
investment and support MSMEs in scaling up operations, thereby limiting its potential 
developmental impact. 
5.26 The Committee, therefore, strongly recommends that the Ministry of MSME, in 
consultation with the Ministry of Finance, operationalise the pro posed ₹10,000 crore 
SME Growth Fund under NSIC in a phased, multi‑year manner, with a clearly defined 
implementation framework, timelines and measurable outcome parameters. The 
Committee emphasises that the proposed Growth Fund should be structured as a 
professionally managed vehicle, distinct from existing credit and guarantee schemes, 
and should focus on providing growth‑stage financing through blended‑finance, 
risk‑sharing and co‑lending models in partnership with financial institutions. Priority 
should be accorded to MSMEs engaged in technology upgradation, export‑oriented 
activities and enterprises operating in underserved and aspirational regions. 
5.27     The Committee further recommends that robust governance mechanisms be 
institutionalised for the p roposed Growth Fund, including the establishment of an 
independent investment committee, transparent eligibility criteria, periodic public 
disclosure of fund utilisation and outcome‑based monitoring linked to employment 
generation, enterprise growth and ma rket access. A mid‑term evaluation framework 

64 
 
should also be incorporated to enable timely course correction and ensure that the Fund 
contributes meaningfully to scaling MSME competitiveness and long‑term 
sustainability, while remaining consistent with the overall fiscal consolidation path. 
5.28     The Committee also observes that despite NSIC‘s expanded mandate, upgraded 
Schedule ‗A‘ status and commendable financial performance, its existing equity base of 
approximately ₹532 crore remains modest relative t o the scale of operations and 
financial support expected from the Corporation. The Committee notes that this limited 
capital base constrains NSIC‘s leverage capacity and necessitates greater reliance on 
borrowings from banks and financial institutions, oft en at higher costs, which 
ultimately affects the affordability of assistance extended to MSMEs. 
5.29     Accordingly, the Committee recommends that the Ministry consider substantial 
equity infusion into NSIC  at the earliest to strengthen its capital base i n line with its 
strategic importance and enhanced responsibilities. The Committee is of the view that 
enhanced equity support would reduce dependence on high -cost borrowings, improve 
internal resource generation, and enable NSIC to offer more competitive i nterest rates, 
lower service charges and affordable support to MSMEs. 
5.30     The Committee further recommends that any equity infusion into NSIC be 
explicitly linked to measurable expansion in MSME coverage, particularly in priority 
sectors and underserv ed regions, so that the intervention directly translates into lower 
cost of finance, improved credit accessibility and sustainable scaling of NSIC 
operations, in alignment with national objectives such as Atmanirbhar Bharat and 
Viksit Bharat.

BUDGET ANNOUNCEMENTS OF 2025-26 AND IMPLEMENTATION STATUS 
1. The Committee notes with serious concern that while out of eight Budget 
announcements, only two — both led by the Ministry of MSME itself — have been 
operationalised. The remaining six, where the Ministry is a supporting agency, continue 
to languish at the stage of draft guidelines, inter -ministerial consultations, or 
preliminary deliberations without clearly defined or publicly disclosed rollout timelines. 
This pattern raises fundamental questions about the effectiveness of inter -ministerial 
coordination mechanisms and the Ministry's ability to influence outcomes where it is 
not the lead agency.  (Para-2.2) 
 
2. The Committee further observes that two announcements of particular 
significance to the MSME ecosystem — credit cards for micro enterprises and the 
scheme for first -time entrepreneurs (women and SC/ST) — remain unimplemente d 
nearly twelve months after their announcement. The credit card initiative, intended to 
reach ten lakh micro enterprises in the first year, has not progressed beyond the draft 
stage. The first-time entrepreneur scheme, which carries important equity and inclusion 
objectives, remains at the EFC memorandum stage. Taken together, these delays 
indicate a serious disconnect between the policy intent articulated in the Budget speech 
and the administrative machinery required for last-mile delivery. (Para-2.3) 
 
3. In view of the above, the Committee strongly recommends that a time -bound 
implementation framework be put in place for all Budget announcements concerning 
the MSME ecosystem, irrespective of whether the Ministry of MSME is the lead or a 
supporting Ministry. In particular, for the credit card scheme for micro enterprises and 
the term -loan scheme for first -time women and SC/ST entrepreneurs, the Committee 
urges the concerned Ministries to work in close coordination to finalise scheme design, 
obtain requisite ap provals and operationalise these initiatives within a clearly defined 
outer limit, preferably not later than the first half of FY 2026 -27. The Committee 
further recommends that progress on all eight announcements be monitored through a 
quarterly, inter‑min isterial review mechanism, anchored by the nodal 
Ministries/Departments concerned with active participation of the Ministry of MSME, 
with milestones, responsible agencies and timelines clearly specified. A consolidated 
implementation status be compiled and  shared with the Committee at regular intervals 
so that the credibility of Budget announcements is reinforced by demonstrable, 
time‑bound outcomes on the ground. (Para-2.4) 
 
4. The Committee notes with serious concern that out of the 7.61 crore MSMEs 
registered on the Udyam portal [including Informal Micro Enterprises on Udyam Assist

Platform (UAP) as on 31 January 2026], an overwhelming 7.56 crore (99.3%) fall under

compared to relatively larger micro units. (Para-2.6) 
 
5. The Committee reiterates that the upward revision of investment and turnover 
limits has the unintended consequence of all owing comparatively larger enterprises to

9. The Chairman welcomed the Secretary and Officers of the Ministry of MSME and its 
allied agencies to the meeting to discuss the Ministry‘s budgetary allocations for FY 2026-27. 
In his introductory remarks, the Chairman: 
 
(i) Acknowledged the budgetary allocation made to the Ministry for 2026–27; 
(ii) Sought clarification on the gap between projected demand and actual allocation 
(iii) Expressed concern over the relatively low proportion of capital expenditure; 
(iv) Emphasized on revising the definition of MSMEs and consider creating a lower 
category (e.g., nano enterprises) to better support very small units;  
Asked about employment generation targets and technological upgra dation 
measures; 
(v) Sought data on MSME closures and sector-wise NPAs; 
(vi) Raised concerns regarding awareness and accessibility of digital platforms and 
schemes. 
 
10. After the Chair‘s permission, the Secretary, M/o MSME apprised the Committee on 
the 2026-27 budget highlights, including: 
 
(i) Key budgetary highlights and major policy initiatives introduced to strengthen the 
MSME ecosystem, with particular emphasis on enhancing competitiveness, 
technology adoption and market integration. 

86 
 
(ii) Measures undertaken to facilit ate access to credit, including expansion of credit 
guarantee schemes, interest subvention support and development of customised 
financial products tailored to the needs of micro and small enterprises. 
(iii) Priority sectoral interventions focusing on industries  such as footwear, leather, 
toys, clean technology and startups, aimed at promoting innovation, employment 
generation and export potential. 
(iv) Implementation of Public Procurement Policy provisions, including targeted 
support and capacity -building measures fo r SC/ST and women entrepreneurs to 
improve their participation in government procurement. 
(v) Steps taken to promote formalisation of enterprises, bridge existing credit gaps 
and enhance domestic and global market access through digital platforms, cluster 
development and institutional support mechanisms. 
11. The Chairman and Members of the Committee raised a wide range of issues, inter 
alia, the following: 
(i) Absence of clear implementation roadmaps, timelines and accountability 
mechanisms for major Budget announcements such as the SME Growth Fund, 
TReDS integration and Corporate Mitra programme; 
(ii) Disproportionate emphasis on equity financing vis -à-vis skill development and 
entrepreneurship promotion; 
(iii) Pendency, verification delays and rejection trends under P M Vishwakarma 
Yojana, and assessment of income enhancement of beneficiaries; 
(iv) Need for collateral-free, cash-flow-based lending and concerns regarding ground -
level banking practices; 
(v) Impact of global trade developments, including tariff-related issues, on MSMEs; 
(vi) Flat allocations for procurement and marketing support despite emerging export 
challenges; 
(vii) Performance and revival of SFURTI clusters, including concerns regarding stalled 
and non-operational clusters; 
(viii) Variations in utilisation figures between Budget Estimates, Revised Estimates and 
Actual Expenditure, particularly with respect to GECL provisions; 
(ix) Outreach, awareness and district -level engagement to improve MSME 
registrations, skilling and institutional coordination, especially in rural areas; 
(x) Mandate, performance and outcomes of technology centres and institutions under 
the Ministry, including MGIRI.

point-wise written replies would be furnished on all issues raised by the Members.
