The Ministry of Home Affairs has been entrusted broadly with the following functions:
            (a)        To deal with all matters relating to internal security;
(b)        To deal with all matters relating to Centre-State relations and Inter-State relations;
(c)        Implementation of the provisions of the Constitution relating to Official Language and the provisions of the
Official Language Act, 1963;
(d)        To discharge certain basic functions like notification of assumption of office by the President and the Vice-
President; notification of appointment of the Prime Minister and other Ministers; notification of
appointment, resignation and removal of Governors and Lt. Governors, appointment of Lt. Governors, Chief
Commissioners and Administrative Officers in Union Territories;
(e)        To deal with matters like citizenship and naturalisation, census of population, National Anthem, National
Flag, etc.; and
(f)         To deal with all constitutional provisions with respect to the State of Jammu and Kashmir and all other
matters relating to the State of Jammu and Kashmir excluding those with which the Ministry of External

12.       Demand Nos. 93, 94, 95, 96 and 97 which are given in Volume-II of the Demands for Grants of the Ministry of
Home Affairs, relate to Union Territories (without Legislature), namely Andaman and Nicobar Islands, Chandigarh, Dadra
& Nagar Haveli, Daman & Diu and Lakshadweep, respectively. The entire budget of the Union Territories (without
Legislature) on the Plan as well as Non-Plan side is reflected in the Demands for Grants of the Ministry. Though the budget
of these Union Territories forms part of the Ministry of Home Affairs, being the nodal Ministry, it, however, does not dilute
the responsibilities of the Ministries of the Central Government from scrutinising and sanctioning the schemes under their
purview. The factual position regarding budgetary procedure in this regard is that the entire budget requirement for Plan
and Non-Plan, revised estimates for current year and Non-Plan budget estimates for the ensuing year is discussed by the
Ministry of Finance in the pre-budget meetings with the representatives of the concerned Union Territories and Ministry of
Home Affairs. An overall ceiling is fixed in respect of each Union Territory. The Planning Commission decides annual
Plan allocations for the ensuing year. The Union Territory Administrations prepare budgetary break-up showing budget
provisions according to their respective budgetary heads and also showing the allocation against Ministries/Departments of
the Central Government administratively concerned. This budgetary break-up is included in the Demands for Grants of the
Ministry of Home Affairs.
 
PRESENTATION OF HOME SECRETARY ON DEMANDS FOR GRANTS (2002-2003)
13.       The Committee considered the Demands for Grants of the Ministry in its meetings held on 20, 21, 24 and 25 March
2003.  The Home Secretary made a power-point presentation before the Committee on 21 March 2003.
14.       The Home Secretary began his presentation with an overview of internal security situation in the country.  He
apprised the Committee of the progress made on various fronts and the steps and measures taken during 2002-2003.  He
stated that living in a unipolar world with a few nation states deciding the course of action anywhere in the globe, as
evidenced by the war on Iraq which began recently, was difficult. The alliance against terrorism which was formed
subsequent to September 11, 2001 attacks on the World Trade Centre and Pentagon, had given way to an alliance of powers
based on their own perceptions of what was good for the world and in the process, the multilateralism of the UN
mechanism had been marginalized and bypassed.  This had implications for our internal security inasmuch as the
perception that Pakistan was cooperating in the war against terrorism was masking, for the powers that be, the fact that it
was also a breeding ground for Jihadi  terrorism.  Though India's position regarding Pakistan sponsored terrorism was
accepted in the international fora, for short-term gains, this reality was being papered over.  The Home Secretary

emphasized that it would be, therefore, realistic to say that India had to fight against cross-border terrorism on its own. He
further mentioned that during the course of the year, there had been no let up in terrorist activities.  During the course of last
12 months, there were a number of major events of terrorist strike such as attack on Raghunath Mandir in March and
November, 2002, Killings at Kaluchak in Jammu in April, 2002, attack on Akashardham Mandir, Gandhinagar in
September, 2002, attack on Amarnath Yatris in August, 2002,Mumbai bomb blasts in a bus at Ghatkopar in December,
2002, and  bomb blasts in a local train at Mulund in March, 2003 in Mumbai.
 
14.1     He further stated that given the geo-political scenario, the continued reliance of the U.S. on Pakistan and Pakistan’s
own domestic compulsions would mean that there would be no immediate respite from cross-border terrorism primarily in
Jammu & Kashmir but aided and abetted elsewhere also in the country.  He believed that eternal vigilance, therefore, would
have to be the order of the day.
           
15.       Thereafter, the Home Secretary gave a detailed description of the internal security trends in vulnerable and sensitive
parts of the country.
 
16.       With regard to the situation in Jammu & Kashmir, the Home Secretary stated  that the promise made by President
Musharraf of Pakistan in January 2002 on the issue of cross-border terrorism remained unfulfilled.  The releasing of chiefs
of terrorist outfits like LeT and JeM by Pakistan Government proved beyond doubt its nefarious designs and double speak.
Hence, the Union Government was pursuing a multi-pronged strategy to bring peace & normalcy in the State which
comprised tackling cross border terrorism, accelerated economic development and dialogue with people/groups of J&K
who eschewed the path of violence.  He stated that as part of mobilizing public opinion against terrorism, the democratic
structure was strengthened in the State by holding Panchayat elections after 24 years and free and fair Assembly elections
with large voter turnouts.  He placed before the Committee the statistics relating to terrorist violence in the State during
2002 and stated that 453 security forces personnel were killed as compared to 536 in 2001, 255 explosions took place as
compared to 402 in 2001 whereas the number of terrorists killed was 1707 as compared to 2020 in 2001 out of which as
many as 508 were foreign mercenaries.  He hoped that the trend of decline in those figures registered after 2001, would
continue to be maintained over the next few years. He observed that Pakistan had not stopped using cross-border infiltration
as tactical tool for fomenting terrorist violence in Jammu and Kashmir despite the diplomatic pressure exerted by the world
community.
 
16.1     The Home Secretary drew the attention to the fact that terrorist attacks were clearly directed against Government
establishment and security establishments and security forces with more emphasis on Fidayeen  or suicide attacks. He
stressed that terrorists responsible for Fidayeen  attacks were all Pakistanis. He went on to emphasise that terrorists were
aided and abetted by the forces across the border through the help in the form of arms and ammunitions as well as finances.
He, however, mentioned that Fidayeen or suicide attacks declined to 10 in 2002 from 28 in 2001.    Further, he stated that
terrorist activities remained unabated notwithstanding the PDP-Congress combine Government’s sympathetic gesture.  He
informed that a Standing Committee headed by Cabinet Secretary had been constituted to monitor the development
programmes of the Central Ministries in the State.  He also referred to infrastructural development projects for the State
announced by the Prime Minister during his visit in May 2002 and stated that the financial outlay on these schemes/projects
would be to the tune of Rs. 6000 crore.  Besides, he mentioned the appointment of Shri N.N. Vohra as an interlocutor to
carry forward dialogue with elected representatives and   informed that he had already begun his work.
 
17.       While apprising the Committee of the situation in North-East, the Home Secretary stated that the level of violence in
the region had declined, though the internal security situation particularly in Nagaland, Assam, Manipur and Tripura needed
to be handled with sensitivity and urgency. He enumerated various outfits active in those States and observed that presence
of long porous border and various inhospitable terrains coupled with inflow of illegal arms and influx of illegal migrants
had facilitated the growth and movement of militant groups. In addition, lack of economic development and employment
opportunity, and assistance from Pak ISI were also contributing to the growth of militancy in the region.  While giving
details of the terrorist violence in the region, the Home Secretary stated that during the year 2002, there were 1312 incidents
of violence compared with 1338 incidents in 2001. During 2002, 457 civilians and 147 security personnel were killed in
terrorist violence as against the figures of 600 civilians and 175 security personnel during 2001. Similarly 571 militants
were killed in 2002 compared to 572 killed the year before. Arms looted by militants were 87 in 2002 as compared to 111
in 2001. The number of arms recovered was 610 in 2002 as compared to 577 in 2001. He stated that the statistics proved
that the number of extremist related incidents came down along with the decline in losses of life of civilians as well as
security personnel.  On the other hand the recovery of arms increased and the number of extremists killed remained the

same. Further, he expected that with the peace initiative taken by the Government the extremist related violence would
substantially go down.
 
17.1     The Home Secretary informed that the number of items to be reimbursed under the scheme of Security Related
Expenditure was expanded substantially. Besides, Ministry of Home Affairs was also implementing a 100% centrally
funded scheme i.e. “Scheme of Special Central Assistance in Kind” to the North Eastern States for modernisation of their
police forces.  The Ministry had also sanctioned 24 India Reserve Battalions to the North Eastern States including Sikkim
(2 each for Arunachal Pradesh, Meghalaya, Mizoram and Nagaland; 6 for Assam, 4 for Manipur, 5 for Tripura and 1 for
Sikkim) with the objective to reduce the dependence of States on CPMFs. Referring to the peaceful and successful
conclusion of Assembly elections in Nagaland, Tripura and Meghalaya, held in February 2003, the Home Secretary stated
that adequate CPMFs were made available to the State Governments for that purpose.
 
17.2     Further elaborating upon the peace initiatives taken by the Government in the North-Eastern region, the Home
Secretary informed that the formal ceasefire between Government of India and NSCN (I-M) group of Nagaland had been
extended upto 31 July, 2003.  The ban under Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, 1967 on NSCN (I-M) was not renewed
after 26 November 2002 to facilitate holding of peace talks in India during January 2003.  After the talks were held in
February 2003, an understanding had been reached for continuation of formal talks until a lasting settlement was reached. 
Meanwhile, both sides reaffirmed the need for a peaceful and violence free environment.  The Government had also entered
into a formal ceasefire with NSCN (K) group which had been extended up to 28 April, 2003.  With a view to make the
ceasefire effective, ground rules were formulated to enforce the ceasefire between the security forces, NSCN (I-M) and
NSCN (K).  Ceasefire Monitoring Group and Cease Fire Supervisory Board had been set up for enforcing ground rules in
respect of NSCN (I-M) and NSCN (K), respectively. 
 
17.3     While making a mention of the Memorandum of Settlement signed between the Central Government, the
Government of Assam and Bodo-Liberation Tigers (BLT) on 10 February, 2003 for durable solution to the Bodo issue, the
Home Secretary stated that it had been agreed to create the Bodoland Territorial Council (BTC) within the State of Assam
and under the Sixth Schedule of the Constitution of India.  Adequate safeguards had been built-up for the interests of non-
tribals in the BTC area.  The Government had also agreed to consider favourably the inclusion of Bodo language in the
Eighth Schedule of the Constitution.  Besides, it would also be the official language of BTC along with Assamese and
English.  He stated that Government would provide Rs. 100 crore per annum for 5 years for socio-economic and

Left Wing Extremism
 
41.       Left wing extremism is proving to be a serious challenge to the internal security of the country.  In 2002, a sudden
spurt has been witnessed in the level of violence by left wing extremists.  During the year, 1465 incidents of violence have
been reported as against 1208 incidents in 2001.  Today, its tentacles have spread to new areas also such as North
Chattisgarh, Western districts of West Bengal, parts of North Bihar, Eastern UP and Eastern and Southern Jharkhand.  In
all, 53 districts in 9 States of the country are under the sway of this movement.  The Government of India along with the
Governments of the affected States has been adopting a two pronged strategy which aims at neutralizing the cadres of the
extremist outfits, PWG and MCC and at the same time focusing its attention on developmental activities in the affected
areas.  A number of steps have been taken on the law & order front to control the situation.  On economic and development
front, the Ministry of Home Affairs has sent a proposal to the Planning Commission for the inclusion of these 53 affected
districts in its new scheme ‘Backward Districts Initiative’ so that additional funds are earmarked to remove their socio-
economic backwardness as per this scheme.  This apart, moves are afoot to ensure good governance and make the
administrative machinery more responsive, sensitive and transparent in the affected areas.  Effective public grievances
redressal system is also proposed to be put in place.
 
41.1     The strategy being adopted by the affected States with the assistance of the Union Government does not seem to be
yielding desired results.  Although the Government can always claim success of its strategy by furnishing figures of the
extremists killed, arrested and surrendered, the bottom line is that this problem is gradually spiralling.  The growth and
spread of the movement may be attributed to its growing clout amongst the common people.  The extremists appear to have
apparently been able to strike the right chord with the people as they have been successful in convincing a section of the
people that the State and its machinery are mainly responsible for all their woes.  This is precisely the reason for a complete
drought of intelligence inputs from the people living in affected areas.  People appear to prefer extremists over the police
and other Government agencies when it comes to making a choice between the two.  It is unfortunate that people like the
extremists, are also losing faith in the State.  Therefore, the need of the hour is to restore people’s faith in the State and its
agencies.  To the contrary, the focus of the strategy to tackle the problem appears to be more on dealing with it purely as a
law and order problem than on striking at its socio-economic roots.  As a matter of fact, no serious efforts have so far been
made to go into the socio-economic causes of the problem with a view to find a lasting solution to it.  Innocent people are
caught between the extremists and the law enforcement agencies and thus are victims at the hands of both.  On the one
hand, people are forced, at gun point, to provide food and shelter to ultras and on the other, they are subjected to harassment

Modernisation of State Police Forces
 
42.       Police is synonymous with Government. Hence its strength and efficiency should always be upto the mark for
continuance of a Government especially a democratic one.  Keeping this in mind the Central Government took up police
modernization plan in late 1960s.  The Ministry of Home Affairs has been implementing a Non-plan Scheme for
Modernisation of State Police Forces since 1969-70 on a 50:50 cost sharing basis.  However, during the period 1969-70 to
1999-2000, a total amount of Rs.536.74 crore has been released to various State Governments on account of this scheme
which is in real terms a meager sum for such a vast project.  In view of the emerging challenges and threats to the internal
security, particularly in the form of extremism, terrorism and religious fundamentalism including left-wing extremism in
some parts of the country, the annual Central allocation under the Scheme for Modernisation of the State Police Forces was
enhanced to Rs.1000 crore from 2000-01 for 10 years.  Nevertheless, the sum allocated for 2000-01 & 2001-02 could be
utilised to the extent of 71% and 43%, respectively.
 
 42.1    The Ministry replying to a question mentioned that the Scheme provides for review of implementation after every
two years.  Accordingly, a Review Committee was constituted under a Special Secretary of Ministry of Home Affairs.  The
Committee visited a few major States and observed implementation of the Scheme.  The views of the State Governments
were also obtained regarding various aspects of the Scheme.  Most of the State Governments have expressed their inability
to contribute matching share due to financial constraints as per the underlying principle of the Scheme.  The Committee
submitted its report on implementation of the scheme.  It has inter alia recommended that the funding pattern of the
Scheme needs to be changed whereby Central Government’s share should be increased to 75% out of which 50% should be

98th Report of Committee on Home Affairs

in the form of grant-in-aid and 25% as loan. 
 
42.2     The Home Secretary in his presentation before the Committee on Demands for Grants (2003-04) stated that the
Central Government had been regularly reminding the States in Chief Ministers’ Conferences and through letters to the
Chief Secretaries of States, to send perspective action plans for modernisation of their Police Forces. However, the States
have been found wanting in utilising the allotted funds.  The lack of initiative on the part of a majority of States is
apparently due to their inability to spare requisite matching funds.  This has led to a situation where the Union Government
is keen to modernise the State police forces but State Governments are not too keen to implement the scheme meant for the
purpose owing to paucity of financial resources at their disposal.  This peculiar situation emanates, to a large extent, from
the federal structure of our polity.  As per the constitutional framework, the subjects of ‘Police’ and ‘Public order’ fall
within the jurisdiction of State Governments whereas the overall responsibility for maintenance of internal security has
been vested with the Union Government under Article 355.  Accordingly, creation, maintenance and supervision of police
forces including their augmentation and modernisation are the primary responsibility of the State Governments.  Therefore,
States are duty bound to ensure that their police forces are fully equipped to meet the challenges being posed on the law and
order front.  As far as maintenance of internal security is concerned, it is the Union Government which has been enjoined
upon by the Constitution to maintain it.  Thus, the founding fathers of the Constitution tried to draw a distinct line between
the jurisdiction and responsibilities of the two Governments.   However, in practice, this line has proved to be very thin and
more often than not, it has completely blurred or vanished.  This is so because what is seen as a threat to law and order also
has a direct bearing on overall internal security and vice versa.  Thus, law and order and internal security are closely
intertwined with each other.  A change in one also gets reflected in the other.  It is precisely the reason why Ministry of
Home Affairs launched a scheme for the modernisation of State police forces as it fully realized that unless the machinery
for maintenance of law & order was strengthened, it would not be able to maintain a foolproof internal security.  Now the
question arises as to why State Governments are not paying as much attention as needed to modernize their police forces? 
Why modernisation of police is not being taken up on priority by the States even though they are fully aware of the dangers
to the general law & order situation?  Here, it may be pointed out that when it comes to the control and superintendence of
police forces, the States do not want to yield even an inch of their jurisdiction.  But at the same time when it comes to
improve and strengthen their police forces, they simply raise their hands expressing their inability to do so because of
financial constraints.  The height is that most of them are not even prepared to meet half of the expenses as per the scheme
of the Union Government.  Given the situation, the Union Government must make some earnest efforts to ensure the active
participation of State Governments in modernising their police forces.  The Union Government should not have a feeling of
helplessness in convincing the State Governments to focus their attention on this issue.  After all, Article 355 places the
responsibility of protecting the States from internal disturbances on the shoulders of the Union Government.  Therefore, the
Union Government will be well within its rights if it issues necessary instructions to the State Governments to come
forward and join it in modernising their police forces in the larger interest of maintaining internal security.  Undoubtedly,
the States have to pull up their socks and exercise financial prudence so as to spare requisite funds for implementation of
the scheme.
 
42.3     The Committee while realising the importance of the role of the State Governments in modernising their police
forces is also conscious of the poor financial position of the States.  Keeping that in view it endorses the proposal which
envisages a new funding pattern based on 75:25 ratio.  This new pattern will reduce the liability of the States from present
50% to 25% of the funds to be used for the modernisation of State police forces.  Here a genuine concern arises as to what
if State Governments fail to provide even half of what they are required to provide at present.  It once again takes the
Committee back to its conclusion mentioned in the preceding paragraphs that States will have to find ways and means to
provide their share of the requisite funds.  The State Governments just cannot afford to look up to the Union Government. 
In a nutshell, the Committee while recommending a switchover to the new funding pattern of 75:25 for implementation of
the scheme, is also of the view that the Union Government should evolve a suitable mechanism to seek active participation
of State Governments in its efforts to modernise their police forces.
 
42.4     The Government of India may also consider the possibility of earmarking the States’ share for modernisation of
police forces while providing annual central plan assistance to them so as to ensure the effective participation of the States
in the implementation of the scheme for modernisation of State police forces.  The Government may further consider to
stop the release of funds so earmarked in central plan assistance in respect of States who are found wanting vis-à-vis the
implementation of the scheme for modernisation of State police forces.
 
42.5     Another issue related to the scheme for modernisation of State police forces is the monitoring of the implementation
of this scheme and the assessment of the actual benefits of the scheme accrued to the States. There has always been an
element of doubt over the actual benefits that accrue to the States out of a centrally sponsored scheme because the actual

implementation of the scheme rests with the concerned State Governments. Although there is a provision for review of the
implementation of the scheme by Ministry of Home Affairs after every two years, there is no credible information available
to assess the actual benefits. All that is available is the details of the funds allocated to various States over the years.
Moreover, the periodic review done in two years’ time is more of a routine bureaucratic exercise. Therefore, the need of the

42.6     Another aspect of the scheme for modernisation of State police forces which needs a re-look is the question as to
whether the present scheme of allotting funds to the State Governments should continue or it should be replaced by a
scheme whereby the central assistance is given in kind and not in cash as it is being done in the case of North-Eastern
States.  Here it may again be worth mentioning that the real benefits of centrally sponsosred schemes fail to reach the
intended beneficiaries quite often than not as a result of which the purpose behind a scheme gets defeated.  Keeping this
view in mind, it is felt that whatever assistance is to be extended to the States it should be in kind and not in cash.  There are
a couple of benefits of following this policy.  One, uniformity in providing standard equipments/gadgets can be ensured to
all the States.  Second, the apprehensions regarding misutilisation of allotted funds can be removed.  Third, the speedy
implementation of a scheme can be ensured if equipment/gadgets are procured at the central level and made available to the
States directly thereby doing away with the usual cumbersome cycle starting from allocation of funds to the final
procurement and installation of equipment/gadgets.
 
42.7     In case of the scheme for modernisation of State police forces, there are two aspects.  One, it aims at improving the
physical infrastructure such as police stations, police posts, buildings, etc.  And the other is the provision of modern
weaponry, equipment, gadgetry, computers, etc. to the police forces to improve their strike capability and general
functioning.  As far as the first aspect is concerned, the present scheme involving release of funds needs no change for
obvious reasons.  It is the second aspect, which happens to be crucial from the point of view of improving the strike
capability of the police forces, that needs a shift from cash to kind.  The Committee is of the view that under the scheme for
modernisation of police forces, only a part of the central share of the funds should be allotted to the States in cash to meet
their requirements  for improving physical infrastructure such as police stations, etc.  Insofar as weaponry, gadgetry,
equipment, vehicles, etc., the central assistance should always be in kind for the reasons stated in the preceding paragraphs.
 
Delhi Police

needed to be done is not being done.  The way the operators of these buses are being allowed to get away with, after
causing immense damage to the health of the people, it appears that some kind of complicity exists between them and
certain levels of Delhi Traffic Police. 
 
43.4.1  Therefore, the Committee is of the view that the situation warrants immediate action on the part of Delhi Traffic
Police to see that Delhites get rid of this menace of pressure horns once and for all.  The Committee recommends that the
Delhi Traffic Police should not only immediately launch a citywide drive against the use of pressure horns especially by
private passenger buses but should also sustain it till such time the city is declared “Free of Pressure Horns”.  It is also of
the view that stringent measures should be taken to prohibit not just the use of pressure horns but also their sale, purchase
and possession within the municipal limits of Delhi.  The Committee would like to take stock of the situation after the lapse
of three months from the date of presentation of its Report in the wake of its above stated recommendation. 
 
Modernisation of Central Para Military Forces (CPMFs)
 
44.       The Central Para Military Forces (CPMFs) have been playing a very crucial role in thwarting both external and
internal threats to the security of the nation.  The vital role being played by them can very well be understood by the fact
that most of these forces are deployed to nearly 100% of their strength which also means that their reserves too are fully
deployed.  This shows how strenuous and difficult is the nature of the duties being performed by the personnel of CPMFs. 
While performing various tasks assigned to them, these forces not only have had to maintain a constant pressure on their

98th Report of Committee on Home Affairs
file:///D/Committee%20on%20Home%20Affairs/98threport.htm[30-06-2016 17:57:49]
adversaries such as militants, insurgents, mercenaries, etc. by way of dynamic deployment, they also have to bear in mind
that their weaponry, gadgetry, equipment, etc. are in no way inferior to those of their adversaries.  With a view to enhance
their striking capabilities in terms of better firepower and a fully equipped manpower, the modernisation plans of six
CPMFs, namely, BSF, CRPF, NSG, CISF, ITBP and Assam Rifles have been put underway.  The modernisation plan for
the seventh CPMF i.e. SSB is also under consideration of the Ministry of Home Affairs.  The notable feature of this
modernisation exercise is that it is being carried out as per a comprehensive perspective plan with concrete budgetary
allocations spread over a time frame of five years.  These modernisation plans inter alia aim at augmentation of the
manpower of the CPMFs.  Accordingly, in all 209 battalions have been sanctioned for CPMFs including India Reserve (IR)
Battalions and Rashtriya Rifles.  Out of these 209 battalions, 127 battalions have been sanctioned to be raised during 2000-
01, 2001-02 and 2002-03.  The remaining 82 battalions are proposed to be raised during 2003-04 and 2004-05 at the rate of
41 battalions in each year.  While the battalions/companies sanctioned during   2000-01 and 2001-02 have already been
raised, the battalions sanctioned for 2002-03 are in the process of raising.
 
44.1     This Committee has all along been in favour of strengthening the CPMFs and will continue to support any
measure/proposal aimed at keeping them in tune with changing times in future too.  While the Committee fully endorses the
on-going exercise of modernisation and augmentation of CPMFs, it, however, would tend to draw the attention of the
Ministry of Home Affairs to some of the practical difficulties being faced by the forces in the wake of the implementation
of their modernisation plans.  The Committee is given to understand that most of the newly sanctioned battalions being
raised are, in effect, nothing but addition of a few more companies to the existing battalions.  The net result of this exercise
is that instead of raising new battalions which have their own infrastructure, chain of command, area of operation, etc., the
existing battalions are being stuffed with more personnel without any support paraphernalia.  For instance, 38 companies
have been sanctioned to ITBP to convert its 19 battalions from 4 company pattern to 6 company pattern.  But administration
staff such as cooks, washerman, barber and water carrier, and support staff for telecom, motor transport, animal transport,
dog squad, etc. have not been sanctioned.  The lack of administration and support staff not only renders the additional
companies operationally dysfunctional but also puts extra strain on the already stretched out infrastructure of the existing
battalions.  Therefore, in view of the situation as explained above, the Committee expresses the view that necessary
paraphernalia should be provided to the additional companies being raised as per the modernisation plans of CPMFs.  It is
of the opinion that the very purpose behind modernisation and augmentation plans will be defeated if the additional
battalions/companies are rendered dysfunctional due to lack of necessary support system.
 
44.2     As mentioned elsewhere in this Report, the process of raising 127 battalions out of the total 209 newly sanctioned
battalions of CPMFs including IR battalions and Rashtriya Rifles, is due for completion by the close of the financial year
2002-03.  With the raising of these 127 battalions, it is expected that CPMFs will be able to relieve their reserve battalions
from various areas of operation.  Accordingly, the Committee recommends that as soon as these127 battalions are fully
operationalised, the reserve battalions of all CPMFs should be withdrawn from operational duties to the extent possible. 
With the withdrawal of reserve battalions, the Committee hopes that sufficient number of jawans could be spared for
training, etc. so as not only to improve the quality of manpower but also to reduce the stress level of the jawans.
 
44.3     Another closely related aspect of the modernisation and augmentation process of CPMFs is the quality of their
human resource.  While there is no gainsaying the fact that CPMFs need to be modernised and their strength needs
augmentation but at the same time we should not lose sight of the quality of their manpower.  In our attempt to see that our
security forces are not outnumbered by their adversaries, we should not end up raising a huge army of jawans who are not
properly looked after, are not provided with quality diet, clothes, accessories, etc. and are not spared for sufficient relief and
recreation.  In addition, welfare measures are also not compromised.  In the view of the Committee, there is an urgent need
to focus on the well being of the jawans of CPMFs so as to improve their allround development.  Accordingly, it
recommends that the Government should lay equal emphasis on the quality of manpower of CPMFs while making attempts
to augment their quantity.
 
44.4     The living conditions for jawans in the barracks of CPMFs also need mention.  The Committee is given to
understand that the upkeep of the barracks is very poor which has a direct impact on the health and well being of jawans. 
Lack of proper attention, adequate funds, etc. are some of the reasons behind the poor conditions in the barracks.  The
Committee, therefore, recommends that adequate funds should be earmarked to improve living conditions in the barracks of
the jawans.  Besides, strict and clear guidelines should be issued to ensure proper upkeep of the barracks.

45.1     Given the state of affairs as they are in respect of the execution of housing projects of CPMFs by CPWD, the

which is now proving to be an even bigger hurdle than the GFR 136 itself.  Keeping this in view, the Committee is of the

136 for the housing projects of CPMFs located anywhere in the country so as to enable the CPMFs to get their housing
projects executed through public sector construction companies.
 
Border Security Force (BSF)
 
46.       Border Security Force (BSF) is one of India’s premier border guarding forces which is not only performing its
primary role of guarding borders but also playing an equally important role in the maintenance of internal security in
Jammu & Kashmir. At present it has 157 battalions which are fully deployed. The role of BSF in guarding Line of Control
(LoC) as well as maintenance of internal security in Jammu & Kashmir needs special mention. Out of 778 Km. long LoC,
BSF has been entrusted with guarding a long stretch of 237.20 Km. while rest of the LoC is under the direct command of
army. Similarly, its 49 battalions have been entrusted with counter insurgency duties in Jammu & Kashmir where also it is
playing an equally important role as that of army. The Director-General, BSF, in his presentation before the Committee
stated that as a matter of fact BSF is standing shoulder to shoulder with the army whether it is counter-insurgency duties or
maintaining a vigil on and guarding as crucial an area as LoC. The nature of duties being performed by BSF personnel is
same as that of the army personnel. But when it comes to payment of allowances, there is a wide gap between the
allowances admissible to the personnel of two forces. The allowances being paid to the army personnel are much higher
than the ones being paid to the BSF personnel for performing the duties which are not only equal in nature and intensity but
also involve equal physical endurance with extremely high stress and fatigue levels.
 
46.1     In view of the above, the Committee recommends that the Government should look into the anomalies.

47.       Special Service Bureau was set up in early 1963 in the wake of Indo-China conflict of 1962 to build people’s morale

and inculcate a spirit of resistance in border population in the then NEFA, North Assam, North Bengal, hills of Uttar

or exit from the territory of India and to prevent smuggling and any other illegal activities.

wing will be allowed to die its natural death.

cadre of SSB.

Criminal Justice System
 
49.       Crime has been defined as an offence against the society or   community.  The primary object of criminal law is to
preserve and maintain public order.  Besides, a firm but humane application of criminal law is essential for the proper
functioning of constitutional democracy.  Otherwise, the consequence would be one of lawlessness and anarchy.  Herein,
lies the importance of criminal law and its role in establishing a sound criminal justice administration.
49.1     However, Criminal Justice System in India has been suffering from hangover of British rule as well as some
inherent lacunae.  The reason being the inadvertence of the Government to reform the laws to suit the time.  The long
period of trial and the undertrials being in prisons for longer periods than the maximum of punishment provided for the
offence have been a major cause of concern.  In addition to these, the problem of hostile witnesses is hampering the
prosecutions.  A police officer records statements during the course of investigation and submits them with other relevant
evidence to the court along with the chargesheet.  One can well understand how weakened the prosecution case will
become if key persons such as eye-witnesses or the complainant himself/herself changes the original version to the police
while deposing in court.
 
49.2     The Home Secretary in his presentation before the Committee mentioned that the Government has already set up a
Committee under the Chairmanship of Dr. (Justice) V.S. Malimath, retired Chief Justice of Kerala High Court to
recommend the measures for revamping the Criminal Justice System.  The Home Secretary also informed that the
Malimath Committee is expected to submit its report by 31 March 2003.  He also mentioned that on the recommendations
of Malimath Committee a time bound programme would be drawn out for its implementation.
 
49.3     In the backdrop of all these developments the Committee recommends that the reform in the Criminal Justice
System should be expedited as it finds that the Malimath Committee has kept on taking extensions whereas the subject
demands immediate attention and action.  The Committee strongly feels that the process of investigation, prosecution and
adjudication should be simplified, thus making cheap, expeditious and swift justice available to the people.  This will have
a positive effect on the prison administration as well, which are crowded with undertrial prisoners.  
 
Border Management
 
50.       India shares long international borders with its neighbours.  A considerable stretch of its borders pass through very
rough and undulating terrain which makes the task of border guarding an extremely difficult one.  This apart, inclement
weather conditions ranging from sub-zero temperatures to more than 50 degree celsius temperatures test the endurance and
commitment of the forces deployed to maintain vigil along the borders.  These hostile natural and weather conditions while
on the one hand make it very difficult for the forces to check cross-border movement of people, on the other hand these
very conditions prove advantageous for militants, immigrants, extremists, mercenaries and smugglers, etc. as they manage
to sneak in and out of the country because of the porosity of borders.  The need for reducing porosity of borders as also to
plug the holes in the borders being used by militants, insurgents etc. as gateways for infiltration into and exfiltration out of
India, has since long been felt.  However, it was in the wake of Kargil intrusions that attention was paid to the management
of borders in a more systematic manner.  The Group of Ministers (GoM) on reforming national security system inter alia
recommended for creating a separate Department of Border Management in the Ministry of Home Affairs.  The
Government has since accepted the recommendation and to begin with has set up a Border Management Division which
will later be transformed into a full-fledged Department.  Thus, Border Management has assumed greater significance
keeping in view the fact that maintenance of internal security depends to a large extent on how we manage our borders. 
The Government has now adopted ‘one border-one force’ principle with a view to have one dedicated border guarding
force for one border.
 
50.1     The Committee is happy to note that the Government is now paying focused attention to manage country’s long
borders with its neighbours.  Border guarding is the primary duty of the Central forces deployed on various borders.  Apart
from having coordination and cooperation among border guarding forces, various intelligence agencies and State police, the
country should have a border management policy with a long-term perspective to deal not just with present but also with
future threats.  Therefore, the Committee is of the view that the Government should take necessary steps to ensure effective
coordination and cooperation among border guarding forces, army, various intelligence agencies and State police.  At the
same time, it should frame a broad policy for border management with a long-term perspective to meet present as well as
future challenges on our frontiers.

51.0     The Central Bureau of Investigation (CBI) is the premier investigating police agency in the country. It traces its
origin to the Special Police Establishment (SPE) which was set up way back in 1941 by the then Government of India to
investigate cases of bribery and corruption in the War & Supply Department of India during World War II.  The Delhi
Special Police Establishment (DSPE) Act, 1946 inter alia makes provision of constitution, powers, functions of SPE and
offences to be investigated by it.  Subsequently, the purview of SPE was enlarged to cover all Departments of the
Government of India and the jurisdiction was extended to all the Union Territories and could also be extended to the States
with the consent of the State Governments concerned.
 
51.1     The CBI  was created through a resolution of Ministry of Home Affairs dated April 1, 1963 to administer DPSE
Act, 1946, following which the DPSE is popularly known as the  CBI.  As per  Section 3 of DPSE Act, 1946, CBI/DPSE
could investigate those offences/classes of offences  notified in Official Gazette by the Government of India. Initially, the
offences that were notified by the Central Government related only to corruption by Central Government employees.  In
due course, with the setting up of a large number of Public Sector Undertakings, the employees of those undertakings were
also brought under the purview of  CBI.  Similarly, with the nationalization of the Banks in 1969, the Public Sector Banks
and their employees also came within the ambit of the CBI.  By now, the CBI  has become multi-disciplinary  investigative
agency with its expanding role from corruption cases to other cases viz – cyber crime, terrorist crime, wildlife crime,
narcotics, arms trafficking, arts and antiquities cases, counterfeiting of currencies, etc.
 
51.2     The SPE initially had two Wings, namely, (i)  the General Offences Wing (GOW) and (ii) Economic Offences Wing
(EOW).  The GOW dealt with cases of bribery and corruption involving the employees of Central Government and Public
Sector Undertakings.  The EOW dealt with cases of violation of various economic/fiscal laws.  Apart from this, the CBI
investigates cases referred to it by the Supreme Court ,various High Courts and the State Governments.
 
51.2.1  The CBI was divided into two investigative Divisions, namely, (i) Anti Corruption Division (ACD) and (ii) Special
Crimes Division (SCD), the latter dealing with cases of conventional crimes, besides economic offences. Again, in 1994 the
organizational structure of CBI was made into following Divisions: (i) Anti Corruption Division (ACD) (ii) Special Crime
Division (SCD) (iii) Economic Offence Division (EOD).
 
51.2.2  Three Divisions namely, Anti Corruption Division, Special Crime Division and Economic Offence Division are
Investigating Divisions.  Anti Corruption Division investigates cases of corruptions and frauds committed by public
servants of all Central Government Departments, Central Public Sector Undertakings and Financial Institutions.  Economic
Offence Division investigates economic crimes including bank frauds, cheating, financial frauds, Import Export and
Foreign Exchange violations, large scale smuggling of narcotics, antiques and other contraband items.  Special Crime
Division investigates into cases of terrorism, bomb blast, sensational homicides, kidnapping, murder and crimes committed
by the mafia/underworld. 
 
51.2.3  Following the Supreme Court Judgment of 18 December 1997, in the Vineet Narain   case, the Directorate of  
Prosecutions was set up in CBI in September 2001. The Directorate of      Prosecution is responsible for conducting
prosecution of CBI cases in various courts and also giving legal advice to CBI officers.
 
51.2.4  The CBI, through its Policy and Coordination Division coordinates investigation for the members of INTERPOLf
51.2.5  The CBI has also set up a Cyber Crime Investigation and Research Cell, which besides doing research in this field
also caters to the long felt need of the investigating agencies. It has also set up Criminal Intelligence Cell.  The

51.7.1  With regard to disposal of trial cases, the  Director, CBI apprised the Committee that the organisation has achieved
71%, 70% and 69% in 2000, 2001 and 2002, respectively which are higher than the disposal rate of any investigating
agency in the country. In the years 1999, 2000, 2001 & 2002, cases referred to CBI for investigation are 157, 113, 155 and

51.7.2  The Committee notes the rate of disposal  of cases for investigation  against the rate of registration.  It, therefore,

51.7.3  One of the important job of the Directorate of Prosecution is preparation of panel of Counsel to conduct
prosecution.  But sometimes the regular Counsel engaged by the CBI may not be able to sustain his arguments especially
before an eminent lawyer hired by the opposite party in the Court of Law resulting in low conviction rate.
 
51.7.4  The Committee therefore desires that as and when circumstances warrant the Government can engage
leading lawyers/advocates as Counsel to argue for CBI in the Court of Law to achieve high conviction rate.
 
51.7.5  The Committee also wanted to know whether a review of pending cases has been made to which  it was informed
that the CBI constantly reviews the status of pending cases on a monthly and quarterly basis, through monthly reports
obtained from all the Branches, and quarterly meetings held with the Regional Deputy Inspectors General.  In the year
2002, four such meetings were held to lay greater stress on quicker decision making.  Two meetings with the
Superintendents of Police were also held in August 2002 and February 2003.  The following steps have been taken to
reduce delays in investigation:
(i)
                  It has been decided on 10.1.2003 that in cases where only Regular Departmental Action is contemplated,
Departments/Ministries will appoint their own Presenting Officers instead of the Investigation Officer of
CBI acting as the Presenting Officer.  Thus, the Investigation Officers would have more time for
investigation purposes;
(ii)
                To expedite issue of Letters Rogatory , it has been decided that CBI will approach the Ministry of Home
Affairs direct rather than through the Administrative Department; and
(iii)               The Government is monitoring cases relating to sanction for prosecution and it is proposed to introduce a
Web- Monitoring System for effective and close monitoring.
51.7.6  The Committee feels that by appointing an officer of the Ministry as Presenting Officer in cases of departmental
enquiry, the impartiality of the investigation may be compromised.  Further, the Committee is of the considered view that
since the Presenting Officer is not an Investigating Officer, investigation by the Departmental Officer may weaken the said
process.  Therefore, the Committee recommends that a CBI officer should be the Presenting Officer in such enquiries.

51.7.8  While noting the steps devised to reduce delays in investigation and monitoring the rate of disposal of cases from
investigation of CBI effective, the Committee, however, feels that the rate of conviction from trial (71% in 2000, 70% in
2001 and 69% in 2002) needs improvement.  Considering the expanding role of CBI in cyber crime, terrorist crime,
counterfeiting of currencies, arms trafficking, etc. it is obvious that unless CBI disposes cases from both investigation and
trial at the same rate of registration, pendency in both aspects is bound to be on the increase.  It, therefore, recommends that
CBI should closely and seriously monitors cases with it and increase the disposal level of cases.
 
51.7.9  The Committee observes that a number of trivial cases are being referred to the CBI.  The Committee is of the
opinion that insignificant cases should not be referred to the CBI which fritter away its energy.  The Committee
recommends to the Union Government to apply its mind in the matter and if necessary it may amend the relevant Section(s)
of the DPSE Act, 1946 so that routine cases referred to CBI for investigation, are not accepted.

51.7.11 It may be pertinent to mention here that all cases pertaining to Senior Government functionaries are being sent to
CVC by the concerned Ministries/Departments to tender advice as to whether the case should be charge-sheeted, regular
departmental enquiry conducted or dropped.  Therefore, the urgency with which the CVC decides on the cases referred to it
plays a vital role in disposing the cases pending sanction for prosecution with CBI. 
 
51.7.12 The CVC should therefore be requested to assist the CBI in deciding the cases referred to it expeditiously so that
CBI could take appropriate action thereon.

51.9.6  The Committee, therefore, desires that the Government may consider introduction of in-house surveillance system,

51.10.3            The Committee wanted to know the reasons for the large number of vacancies. The CBI has stated that some
of the reasons for the vacancies were non availability of willing officers from State Police forces for induction in CBI in the
executive ranks; the posts in Ministerial ranks  are vacant due to non receipt of sufficient dossiers of qualified candidates
from Staff Selection Commission; in the case of Group 'A' Executive posts in CBI, the procedure of scrutiny of
applications, their processing, consultations and interview, etc. by the UPSC result in delay; the UPSC/SSC have not
provided the dossiers of qualified candidates to fill the vacancies in different ranks in CBI under Direct Recruitment quota; 
the unattractive terms of deputation in the executive  ranks, such as non-admissibility of some perks viz. Ration Allowance,
Washing Allowance and other special allowances, etc. as in State Police forces/CPOs;  vacancies in Group 'A' posts are
reported to UPSC through DOP&T which is a time consuming process; circulars for filling of the posts are sent to DAVP
through DOP&T which is again time consuming; and a large number of vacancies in the rank of constabulary exist due to
pending litigation in the Apex Court.
 
51.10.4            It is understood that investigation into organized crime involve great amount of risk due to the involvement
of under-world mafia having international connections.  Due to the arduous nature of the work, the CBI personnel work in
adverse condition.
 
51.10.5            Prior to Fifth Pay Commission, the officials of CBI in all ranks were getting higher pay scale in comparison
to officers working in same rank in Central Police Organisations.  The Fifth Pay Commission rationalized pay scales of CBI
officials by recommending uniform pay scales  to all organizations but recommended 10% of the minimum basic pay from
the rank of Constable to Inspector as allowance which was not accepted by the Government .   The Committee has been
informed that the Government is contemplating a proposal for giving 10% of the basic as allowance to CBI personnel.
 
51.10.6            The CBI has placed before the Committee for consideration granting the same allowance to the CBI as given
to the Special Protection Group (SPG) i.e. 50% of basic pay + DA accrued thereon.

51.11.0 The Committee also wanted to know what steps the organization had taken for immediate filling up of the vacant
posts to which the CBI replied that at present, a very lengthy procedure for appointment of Deputy Superintendents of
Police  (Group 'A') on deputation/transfer basis is being followed.  The CBI, however, itself inducts officers on deputation
upto the rank of Inspector.
 
51.11.1 In keeping with the extant rules and policy of the Central Government, appointments to Group-'A' posts require
consultation with the Union Public Service Commission for direct recruitment/deputation/promotion and with the CBI
Selection Board for taking IPS officers, from SP to Director, on deputation.  For the posts in Group 'B', 'C' and 'D'
categories under the delegated powers, the CBI is competent to fill the vacancies according to the mode of recruitment
prescribed in the Recruitment Rules.
 
51.11.2 For filling the existing vacancies of investigating and legal officers, besides making vigorous efforts in calendar
year 2002 that did not yield much success, numerous options are being weighed.  Realizing that 106 vacant posts of DSPs
were affecting the functioning of the Bureau, 24 posts have been diverted from deputation to promotion quota, and the
proposal to consider promotion of eligible officers against these vacancies  is under consideration of the Union Public
Service Commission.  As regards the Annual Recruitment Plan, against the 43 Direct Recruitment posts of Legal Officers
and other categories in 'B', 'C' and 'D' levels, a meeting of the Screening Committee had been scheduled on 20.3.2003. 
According to the CBI, non-availability of sufficient incentives to officers to join the CBI on deputation is one reason for
large deputation vacancies. 
 
51.11.3  The Committee wanted to know the selection procedure for the post of DSP (Group-A), where vacancies are
highest (44%) amongst investigating officers.
 
51.11.4  The CBI in its written submission has underlined that a lengthy procedure is followed for appointment of DSP on
deputation/transfer basis.  It has further stated that in the last five years the UPSC could recommend only five officers on
deputation/absorption basis against an average vacancy of 97 in the rank.  At present, out of total vacancy of 106 posts,
86% of the posts (92) are lying vacant in the deputation quota.
 
51.11.5   The CBI has submitted that the power to appoint DSP & Add. SSP may be vested with the Director CBI as is done
in the case of induction of Group B posts i.e., Inspectors and below.
 
51.11.6   The Committee takes serious note of the vacancy position in the main investigative ranks in the CBI and urges
upon the Government to take urgent steps to fill up vacancies in the rank of main investigative posts at the earliest. 
 
51.11.7 The Committee was also informed that there are several technical posts (e.g., 84 Computer personnel, 34
technical/advisory), which have been deemed abolished by the Department of Expenditure.  The need and justification for
their revival has been taken up with the Department of Expenditure.  The Recruitment Rules for some technical posts are
also yet to be finalized.
 
51.11.8The Committee recommends that with the emergence of cyber-crime and other high-tech crimes, the technical posts
should be adequate to deal with the emerging crime scenario.
 
51.11.9With regard to filling up of vacant posts existing in the CBI, the Committee wanted to know  the response  of the

51.11.10 Keeping in view the extant rules and elaborate procedure of the Government of India for induction of personnel in
